## Kannamma

> **Translator's note:** This translation follows the source-audited Tamil preserved in this repository. Kannamma's final explanation, self-justification, admissions and mockery remain her viewpoint. They are not used to rewrite the earlier narrators inside their own sections. Canonical Tamil remains authoritative and stays under the separate verification freeze.

I am only following the path shown by the gods. What greater wickedness have I committed than Ahalya did? Is what I did worse than what Tara did? Even a woman such as Parvati was deceived by Brahma; are not all the wrongs that happen in the world paths shown by the gods themselves?

Ulaganathar must be angry with me. He will call me a traitor; he will call me an adulteress—let him say whatever he likes! What new thing have I done now that I had not already done without remorse? If anything can be called new, it is that I have left town with Veeran. Did Ulaganathar conduct himself properly, that I should conduct myself properly? When I was there as his wife, why did he need desire and lust for Kumudha?

Am I the sort to betray him? I had once intended to remain in the house, pretend to be Ulaganathar's wife, and spend my days at Veeran's feast. It was my husband who ruined that arrangement! He might at least have remained a doll called a husband. By desiring Kumudha he lost even that position, poor man! How could I feel pity for Ulaganathar? I myself had been the cause of the death of Kumudha, a woman of my own sex! <!-- source join: scan 46 `காரண` + scan 47 `மாயிருந்தேன்!` --> If I had not put Kumudha through all that, would I have had Veeran's feast? Do you know why Kumudha died?

That night she was raped. Before Ulaganathar's frenzy she became like a snake shut in a box. I succeeded in the scheme I had devised to separate Kumudha and Veeran. Veeran saw Ulaganathar and Kumudha in the same room—I made sure he saw them. The next morning Kumudha came down with *vakkup-bethi*. Everyone said it was cholera. Only she and I knew that, unable to bear the humiliation, she had swallowed the diamond. You ask how I knew. My diamond nose-stud was lying there ground to powder; the diamond in it was missing. I concealed that fact completely.

Kumudha died that evening. Even then my stone heart did not melt. Veeran was angry with Kumudha, but he did not grieve even a little. Would I let him grieve?

A letter had been tucked into the cart shed. It was a letter written by Kumudha to Veeran. It could hardly be called merely a letter. She had set down the story of her life just as it was, leaving out nothing from her birth to her decision to swallow the diamond and die. <!-- source join: scan 47 `எழுதி` + scan 48 `யிருந்தாள்.` --> I took it and read it. I did not stop with tearing it up and throwing it away; I put it into the stove and burned it to ash. If that letter had fallen into Veeran's hands, and if he had learned what it contained, he would have tortured me. But now it is I who torture him with my smile.

Do you know where Veeran and I are now? We are living our life in Calcutta. On the evening Kumudha died, my husband had gone somewhere on business connected with the shop. Kumudha's death was another worry for him! That very night Veeran and I left the house. When I left I took nearly ten thousand rupees with me. That night itself we reached the railway station near our village. Veeran drove the cart there. With that, a full stop was put to his profession as a cartman. My life as a whore, too, was left behind.

In the middle of that night Veeran drove the cart very fast. I leaned on his shoulder. On the very day Kumudha offered up her life on the sacrificial altar of this wretched society, we set out on our journey of pleasure. What worry had we to trouble us?

The cart was crossing the boundary of the village. It was pitch dark. In that darkness a great flame burned steadily. <!-- source join: scan 48 `ஒரு பெரிய` + scan 49 `ஜோதி நிரந்தரமாக...` --> “What is that, Veera?” I asked. “Kumudha's corpse is burning in the cremation ground,” he answered indifferently. How much love he had once had for Kumudha! Had I not become his very empire of moksha? Poor man!

The body she had borne for Aththan had been soiled. Kumudha was bathing it in fire. Kumudha had parted not only from the world, but also from the heart of the Aththan she had trusted. Poor girl!

Our cart rattled “kadakada” toward the railway station; it had gone far away from the place where Kumudha was burning.

The terrifying howl of a jackal reached the highest pitch of sorrow. It seemed that, in this world, that one living creature alone was crying for Kumudha then!

Kumudha lies there; now hear about us. The train we boarded set off toward Calcutta.

One word about our Calcutta life! Devotees are complete fools. Why think one must go somewhere to see Indra's realm, idiots! Let them come and see that our house in Calcutta is an Indra-world! I am Indrani! Veeran is Indra! Rati and Manmatha should come to us for lessons, you know?
