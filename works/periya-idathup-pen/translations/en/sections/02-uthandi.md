## Uthandi

> **Translator's note:** This translation follows the source-audited Tamil preserved in this repository. The canonical Tamil page records remain authoritative. The English preserves the work's source-printed character accounts, rhetoric, colloquial language and historical-period vocabulary without silently modernising the Tamil. The canonical pages remain under the project's separate verification freeze.

Ever since my teeth first came in, I have worked in servile labour in the estate owner's household. Generation after generation, my grandfather and his forefathers were all slaves. Where would my father ever have got the idea of making me a free man? Ulaganatha Mudaliar is many years younger than I am. When I was grazing cattle on that estate, he had not even been born. Yet now Ulaganathar's lower belly is forever going “paseer.” For all that, he is only a Mandi Shop man. But Lakshmi plays there; she has made the place her home. If he chooses to ruin me, he can finish me off. My house, my land, my garden—everything is mortgaged to him. It is with that confidence that he has stabbed into my heart and stirred it up.

I owe him 500 rupees. I used to wonder how such a miserly man could have trusted us enough to lend me money. Only now did I under<!-- source join: scan 15 `தெரிந்` + scan 16 `தது.` → `தெரிந்தது.` -->stand the real reason for his generosity. Kumudha is my girl, my beloved daughter. When her mother died, she entrusted Kumudha to me. I thought no suffering should ever touch Kumudha's life. But God did not think so. That wretched God sent me Ulaganathar instead.

That day Ulaganathar sent for me. I thought it must be something about cultivation, so I went. “Come, Uthandi!” he called, and desire was already overflowing from his face. There was no one else where we were talking. A man who usually summoned me in a tone of command—“Hey, Uthandi!”—called me with affection that day. He had always treated me arrogantly, but for several days he had been using only kind words with me.

“Master, I heard you called me,” I said, standing bent before him. “You keep letting the debt grow; the interest keeps growing too. Don't you know that?” he asked. He laid the foundation firmly. At first I did not understand what he had in mind; I stood there silently. “Isn't it time for your daughter's marriage and all its ceremonies?” came his next question. I did not find that strange either. I was happy that Ayya cared so much about an estate labourer's family. But he did not let that thought last. Our house's **Anni** needed a maid, he said.<!-- source boundary: scan 16 `நம்` → scan 17 `வீட்டு` --> “Why don't you leave your daughter here until she gets married?” he said. Only then did I begin to falter. “She's only a child, sir. She'll be afraid to stay here alone,” I said with a nervous grin. “Are you leaving your daughter in a forest? You madman!” Ulaganathar mocked. I did not know what to say. “Does she have to stay here all the time?” I ventured. “Oh, let her stay for a month or two or three. Is your girl going to wear away or something! If she stays with Kannamma and learns from her, she'll understand household matters too; even when she goes to another man's house, she'll earn a name as a good housewife,” he began to sermonise. I could not refuse; I did not have the courage to refuse. If I had refused, I would have had to repay his debt the very next day. Where could I have found 500 rupees then? Had these wretched gods left me in any other condition? They had no concern for me at all!

“I'll pay your daughter Kumudam twenty rupees a month. Don't worry. She won't have any hardship here. She can live comfortably with Kannamma. There isn't much work either,” Ulaganathar began his sermon again. In a village, seeing money is like seeing God<!-- source boundary: scan 17 `கண்டது` → scan 18 `போலத்தானே!` -->, isn't it! And for talaiyaris like me, need one even ask? Gopalakrishna Pillai's daughter studied through the tenth class, works as a schoolmistress and earns twenty rupees a month. When my Kumudha, with none of that, was offered twenty rupees a month, temptation struck me; a little desire rose in me too. I made plans to save the twenty rupees she would receive every month and send her to her husband's house with it as dowry. I obeyed Ulaganathar's order.

I told my Kumudha what I thought. Wasn't Kumudha child-hearted? She hesitated to leave me and work in another house. “It's only in this same village, child! Why are you afraid? I'll come to the master's house often and see you. Don't be afraid,” I said, patching together hollow reassurances. She too agreed to go to Ulaganathar's house as a maid. Accordingly, she went there and began the work. Only afterwards did I understand what I had done. Blind fool that I was, I had thrown Kumudha into a pit and buried her. Shall I tell you more? One day Kumudha was sitting in the backyard of Ulaganathar's house. I suddenly entered there. Wiping her eyes, Kumudha sprang up and ran to me crying, “Appa!” “Why, Kumudha, are you crying?” I<!-- source join: scan 18 `என்` + scan 19 `றேன்.` → `என்றேன்.` --> asked. Only God knows what my heart went through. Kumudha sobbed. Her face was swollen from crying and crying. Her eyes looked a vivid raw red. My legs trembled. My hands began to shake. Kumudha started to speak. But her mouth faltered. She said a few words. They shot into my ears like spears. Before she could say more, the lady of the house, Kannamma, called, “Kumudha! Kumudha!” Kumudha ran off. I returned home. I did not sleep that entire night. “Kumudha! Kumudha!” I lamented.

I had kept my dear Kumudha for Veeran, my sister's son. I had dreamed of seeing Veeran and Kumudha in their wedding finery. But before that, they had already become a pair. I had been thinking of getting the marriage completed; but where would a poor wretch like me get the money? Wasn't it precisely the hardship of having no money that made me obey Ulaganathar's order and send my Kumudha to work as a maid!

I never knew that Kannamma was such a schemer! She has left my insides burning! You adulteress.
