## Veeran

> **Translator's note:** This translation follows the source-audited Tamil preserved in this repository. The canonical Tamil page records remain authoritative. The English preserves the work's source-printed character accounts, rhetoric, colloquial language and historical-period vocabulary without silently modernising the Tamil. Aththan! Aththan! What more is there to want? Aththan! Could that wicked woman deceive me like that? Did she think I was only an uneducated fool! From childhood I had a special affection for Kumudha. She was the same toward me.

You will naturally ask, “Why, Veera, do you call such a Kumudha a wicked woman?” Before that, listen to my history. My history is a strange one. It is full of bends like the road a cart travels. There are plenty of rises and hollows, pits and ruts.

Uncle Uthandi was a talaiyari on Ulaganathar's estate; Kumudam was his daughter. Uncle Uthandi had promised to give Kumudam to me in marriage. I thought that if I was going to marry Kumudha, should I not find some way to earn a living? Uncle Uthandi recommended me for that. I took a job as the cartman in Ulaganathar's house. The cartman's job paid five rupees a month. Food was in Ulaganathar's house itself. Pleasures beyond a cartman's lot came thrust upon me. The master would often go out of town. Whenever the master was away, Kannamma's attentions to me would be many. For a long time I did not understand why.

<!-- source: scan 38; printed page: 37 -->

One day Kannamma began a whole chapter of complaint about how Ulaganathar had no concern for the house. “Yes, Anni,” I said, nodding. Another day Kannamma said, “Ulaganathar's conduct itself is not right, Veera!” I agreed to that too and moved away. “Sit down, Veera! He isn't in town either. If you have any village stories, tell me!” she insisted. Until ten at night she chattered about all sorts of gossip. Then I went back home. What home did I have? My bed was on my uncle's front veranda.

Another day Kannamma began the story by saying, “I cannot stand even the sight of Ulaganathar. A complete fool!” I too sat and listened. “Living with Ulaganathar itself has become bitter,” she concluded. “Everything will become all right, Amma,” I said and rose. “Why the hurry?” Kannamma said, catching hold of the cloth on my shoulder and pulling it. I was startled out of my wits.

Merely sitting and talking with a woman of a great house—and that too a young woman—had always frightened me. After the situation became this extreme, what could I do? I opened my mouth to say, “Let me go, Amma.” There was no moisture in my mouth; it had dried up. I could not even move my tongue. In my dry<!-- source boundary: scan 39 phrase continues on scan 40 --> throat it felt as though some obstruction had arisen. Before I knew it, I lost awareness of myself. Kannamma's hands were stroking my head. Only then did I see that my **mundasu**, my headcloth, had come loose and fallen. My heart beat at the speed of wind in amazement that a woman of a respectable house could have fallen so low. What a reputation Ulaganathar's estate had in the village! At first I thought how degrading it was that the lady of that house should become the lover of an ordinary wage labourer. That thought did not last long. It was shattered in its infancy. I did not have the heart either to oppose the lady of the house or refuse her wish. Out of deference, I yielded. For a while I even set aside my love for Kumudha. After that, there is no need to ask about Kannamma and me. My position became one step higher than Ulaganathar's. I did not notice time passing. I had read in the story of **Rajabhartruhari** that a horseman won a queen's love. What difference was there between him and me? In truth, it was Ulaganathar and Kannamma who were mismatched. Can one yoke a “sandi” bullock and a “jati” bullock together to the same cart? Somehow I had become something Kannamma needed<!-- source boundary: scan 40 phrase continues on scan 41 -->. She lay at my feet.

One day Kannamma gave me a scrap of paper. I asked her what was written on it. “A medicine,” she said. She did not stop there; swaying her body lightly, she said, “It's all because of you.” “I don't understand,” I said, staring. Then she explained. It felt as though a **karkkuchi** had stabbed into my body with a “sveer.” She feared that if she had a child it might be born looking like me. I bought that medicine from an English medicine shop and brought it. When he gave it to me, the shopkeeper stared at me. I came away without looking back. Kannamma took it. We got rid of the pregnancy, root and all.

All of a sudden, a storm began to blow in my mind. It seemed as though the cart of my life had become trapped in a great pit and overturned. I heard that people in the village were saying all kinds of things about Kannamma and me. After that I stopped speaking with Kannamma. I would not even look up at her.

All my desires turned back to Kumudha—I waited for the day I would marry her. Uncle Uthandi said the marriage would be held in the month of Thai; I was unable to contain my joy.<!-- source join: scan 41 `கல்யா` + scan 42 `ணத்தை` → `கல்யாணத்தை` --> I trusted Kumudha with all my heart. I believed she would not deceive me. But I became like the parrot waiting for the ilavu fruit. Kumudha went to Ulaganathar's house as a maid. Even when she left, she and I spoke for a long time. She told me to meet her often. She even shed two drops of tears. At the time I did not understand that it was all pretence. Trusting her, I had been neglecting even the lady of the house.

But.... that night......... Ah!.... My blood boils even when I think of it. I had fed the cart-bullocks and was about to leave for home. Kannamma came running. She dragged me hurriedly into the house. Nothing occurred to me. I could not even swagger away. Whatever else, was she not the old Kannamma! She pulled me toward a room off the veranda. Through an opening in the room's window she told me to look inside. I looked. How can I describe what happened to my mind when I saw it? If Kannamma had not immediately pulled me away, that room would have been smashed to pieces. Kumudha would have died by my hand.

<!-- source: scan 41 / printed 40 → scan 42 / printed 41 -->

“Did you see the conduct of your beloved Kumudha?” Kannamma asked!

What could I say! My bowed head would not rise. My body burned hot. If Kannamma had not taken me to her room, I would have died from the boiling of my blood. With my own eyes I had seen Kumudha, who had said she had placed her life in me, enjoying herself with Ulaganathar. I swear: Kumudha is an adulteress!

Those eyes that had once looked tenderly at Kumudha had turned the colour of blood. Those hands that had trembled to touch her body now ached to tear her apart.

I submitted to Kannamma's consoling words. Kannamma had concern for my life, while that Kumudha had none! Even if she had none, let it be. But she had made a fool of me! Could a worse whore than Kumudha ever be born in this world? Could one even be born? Had she not said she would offer her life to me! I had seen with my own eyes that she sold her body to Ulaganathar! What further witness was needed?

The next morning Kumudha received the proper punishment. Would God<!-- source boundary: scan 43 `கடவுள்` + scan 44 `பொறுப்பாரா?` --> tolerate the wrong she had done? Had she not committed a fault that even the divine could not bear! The punishment God gave was the proper punishment. Kumudha came down with cholera. She was struck by vomiting and diarrhoea. That deceiver! The obscene life of that black-hearted harlot came to an end. Was my curse an ordinary one? Was it not the curse of a man who had been deceived? Was it not right that that murderess should become prey to the fire of the abandoned Veeran's rage! I was near her when she was dying. She looked at me two or three times. I considered even looking into that demoness's face a sin and left the place. Before the day was out, Kumudha was gone.

That destroyer who had said she wished to marry me and go in a wedding procession went instead in procession on a funeral bier. I rejoiced at the sight.

Uncle Uthandi wailed. He rolled in the street. Poor man—the curse of having begotten her; he lamented. Why did he ever give birth to that adulteress?

On the very night Kumudha died, Kannamma and I prepared a plan. That was the perfect day to put it into effect. That plan is what has made me so very fortunate now. Look at me now! Am I still Veeran the cartman?
