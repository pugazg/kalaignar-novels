# The Bud

“This wedding is something very, very new even for the village of Thirukkuvalai! There are plenty of people here steeped in the old ways! Though on one side there is a desire to walk the path of reason, this is a beautiful little village filled with people who cling stubbornly to beliefs of the past! The temple bell sounding again and again—the tower touching the sky—the lovely temple tank opposite—coconut palms along the bank and houses with raised verandas—the agraharam laid out facing south to catch the breeze! In a place so richly filled with the fruit of the old ways, it is here that I am going to have this new-style wedding. My friend! This village is indeed a long way from Chennai! Still, the two of us are so close! Somehow get leave from the office and you absolutely must come—this is an order of affection!” Rathinam had ended his letter that way. Gokul did not know what to do. For four or five years he had not attended a single wedding celebration.

He had no wish to go and sit there as the very image of sorrow amid celebrations overflowing with joy.

<!-- source: scan 7; printed page: 2 -->

If merely seeing wedding invitations left him sitting as though bewildered—what would happen if he actually witnessed a wedding in person? His own wedding—the music of the drums that had sounded then! The mischief he had shown his wife while sitting on the wedding dais! The tenderness with which she had enjoyed it! The journey of bliss that followed! Then the token of love that came! He, between honey and rock candy! The storm that suddenly swept through his life! His companion taken into a sleep from which she would never wake! His dear son left without a mother!—these events and scenes would march before his mind's eye. That was precisely why he had stopped going to weddings. But now an invitation he could not turn down had arrived. Rathinam and he were inseparable friends. If he did not attend Rathinam's wedding..... Gokul could not even bear to think of it. After two or three days of confusion, he decided that he would go to Rathinam's wedding.

Rathinam's wedding took place in a new-style manner. What he had written to Gokul—that it was an entirely new kind of wedding for that locality—was true! All the villagers, even those without invitations, had gathered to see that astonishing wedding.

Priest—homa fire-pit—ammi—arasani—none of them had any work to do. With the affection of those present as witness, the wedding was conducted in a rational manner. Gokul saw his friend in the bridegroom's attire. The grief he had held back tried to burst through its dam. He controlled himself. It was his friend's life-contract ceremony! He knew well enough that it would not be right for him to behave this way there. After the leader's introductory remarks and amid everyone's words of blessing, the bridegroom and bride exchanged garlands. Gokul's own wedding had not taken place like this.

It had taken place with Agni and Arundhati as witnesses. He thought of that divine wedding—and of those gods who had not had the power to hold on to his wife's life. He laughed to himself. The wedding ended, and the feast and the other events also proceeded well.<!-- source join: scan 7 / printed 2 ends `நடந்`; scan 8 / printed 3 begins `தேறின.`; translated jointly as the completed sentence above -->

Gokul took leave of his friend and prepared to return to Chennai. As he saw him off, Rathinam looked at him and began to advise him: “Gokul! How long do you intend to remain like this? Forget what has passed and arrange another marriage! What are you now—have you already become an old man?”

“I haven't become an old man. Nor have I turned into a sage who says I never want marriage again. But,... Kumar is there.... What if the new mother who comes becomes an ‘eclipse’ over him—what am I to do?” Gokul said, tears falling. After a long conversation, the two parted without reaching any decision.

Rathinam had told him that it would be convenient to take the train if he went to Ammanur, six miles beyond Thirukkuvalai, and had put Gokul on a two-bullock cart. Gokul reached the railway station half an hour before the train was due. The cart driver who had brought him turned back and left. At that little station he could not find even a person to talk to. He kept wandering here and there. Thinking he might at least look for a cigarette, a companion in loneliness, he asked around nearby. “There!” the villagers said, pointing with a hand, and went on their way.

He walked toward the little shop whose light showed a “flicker, flicker” in the darkness. The shop was there, but there was no one to do business. Since the shop stood on the veranda of a small house, he assumed the shopkeeper must be inside and called out, “Who's at the shop?”

“Who is it.... What do you want?” A beautiful young woman came out as she asked. Even in the dim light he could see her face. A sudden admonition rose within him that it was not proper to look at her that way, and in a hesitant voice he asked, “I want a cigarette!” She began searching for one.

As she bent and moved in the effort of searching, he could not help noticing the graceful curves of her body. She naturally possessed abhinaya movements that city women could not learn even after taking lessons from a dance master.<!-- source join: scan 8 / printed 3 ends `அபிநய`; scan 9 / printed 4 begins `அசைவுகளை`; translated as the phrase `abhinaya movements` -->

Gokul found it astonishing. The young woman had still not managed to find the cigarette. By then, a terrible coughing sound came from inside. She hesitated for a moment and turned back in. The coughing came again, more violently. She could no longer search for the cigarette. Looking at Gokul, she said, “Please find the cigarette yourself and put the money in the box!” Without even waiting for his reply, she ran inside. Gokul stood rooted like a tree in astonishment. He had heard of such a system in Western countries. A daily newspaper and a bowl for coins, he had heard, would be left by the roadside. Those who needed a paper would put in the money and take one. ‘Here in our country, isn't it enough if even locked and embedded temple donation boxes are left unmoved? How did this country girl, without a teacher, learn a civility that even city people have not learned?’ Gokul stood thinking. He dropped the one-rupee coin in his hand into the box, found a broken cigarette, lit it from the little lamp, and turned toward the railway station. He could scarcely have taken even one step when a cry came from inside the hut: “Ayyo! Appa! Appa!!” It had to be her voice. It was her voice! Forgetting himself, Gokul ran inside.

An old man lay unconscious on the bed. The young woman had rested her head on his chest and was wailing. ‘Oh, it seems he has died!’ Gokul thought as he went closer. She kept crying, “Ayyo, Appa! Appa!” Gokul placed a hand on the old man's face. He understood that the man was not dead. At once he looked at her and consoled her: “Don't be afraid, my dear! Your father isn't dead. It's only a faint!” She gained a little hope. She touched her father and checked. What he had said was true!

“Sir! Please look after Appa for a little while.... I'll run as fast as I can, bring the doctor<!-- source join: scan 9 / printed 4 ends `வைத்தியரை அழைத்து`; scan 10 / printed 5 begins `வந்துவிடுகிறேன்”` --> and come straight back!” she pleaded.

<!-- source: scan 10; printed page: 5 -->

Could Gokul say no? It was a very, very urgent situation! The bell at the railway station announcing that the train was about to arrive reached his ears. Even so, he could not refuse her pitiful plea. He nodded yes. She ran. A short while later she returned with the doctor. The doctor gave the old man various treatments and brought him out of his faint. The old man slowly opened his eyes and called, “Gomathi!” She too cried, “Appa!” and sprang toward her father's bedside. ‘Gomathi’—Gokul's lips tried pronouncing the name. The doctor left. Gomathi looked at Gokul with eyes overflowing with gratitude. Gokul wholeheartedly blessed the young bird of that poor family and said, “I'll be going, dear!” Her father, lying on the bed, asked in a feeble voice, “Who is that, Gomathi?” “Just some traveller, Appa! A very good man! He watched over you until I came back with the doctor!” Gomathi said, once again thanking Gokul with her eyes. Having already made inquiries, Gokul felt it would not be right to leave without speaking to the old man, so he asked him, “Well, elder! How are you feeling?” “How am I? Now? A little better, that's all!”

“Don't worry; you'll be all right. It's getting time for the train—I'll be going!”

After saying this, he came outside, looking at Gomathi as he did. The train—he had to catch it somehow. He looked toward the station from where he stood. Just then the train was pulling away from the station. Stunned by the disappointment that there was no longer any way to catch it, he stood there. She stood by the shop and said sympathetically, “It's because of us that this has happened to you!” Gokul even forgot that he had missed the train. Those words of sympathy pierced straight through his heart.

“It's all right. What does it matter..I'll be going!” he said, and waited for an answer.

“Why don't you sleep right here on the veranda? The train is only in the morning!” she said.

“There's no use going by the morning train. If I want to catch the Madras Mail at Mayavaram, I'll have to take tomorrow night's train. If I go in the morning, I'll only have to wait somewhere!” Gokul said as he came and sat on the veranda.

Hearing the sound of coughing from inside, Gomathi ran in. Gokul followed and looked in as well. The old man looked at Gokul and asked affectionately, “You haven't left, young man?” “Because of us he missed the train, Appa! Now it will have to be tomorrow's train. Poor man!” said Gomathi. “Is that so? Ayyo, poor fellow!” said the old man, and asked Gokul to sit near him.

Gokul could not sleep either. The old man, coughing all the while, learned the details about him. He said that it was when he thought of Gomathi that he felt afraid, that protecting her was like keeping a fawn safe among wolves, and he wept. “I struggled all these years to raise Gomathi safely after she lost her mother. I made sure she was educated enough too. I'm going without placing her hand in that of a good young man!—that's what worries me!” he said, shedding tears.

After a long time, Gokul went and lay down on the veranda. Sleep would not come to him! All kinds of whirling thoughts! Little Kumar—Gomathi... Why should the two of them keep appearing one after the other? Only toward dawn did sleep begin to embrace his eyes. But Gomathi's sudden wail startled him awake. He knocked on the door. Opening it, she cried, “Appa! Appa!” Gokul ran to the old man and looked. His body had gone ice-cold. The old man was gone. An unexpected burden fell on Gokul! Fortunately, the train was only in the evening! He took care of the arrangements needed for the old man's burial. With his great help, Gomathi's father was laid to rest. A few villagers came, sang an oppari before Gomathi, and went away.<!-- source join: scan 11 / printed 6 ends `போய்`; scan 12 / printed 7 begins `விட்டனர்.`; translated jointly as `went away` -->

<!-- source: scan 12; printed page: 7 -->

At the loss of her father, Gomathi writhed like a worm in embers. The one support she had had—that too was gone!

Night came! Gokul, who had sat on the veranda all day, got up and went inside. He saw Gomathi crying. “Gomathi!” he said, his voice thick with emotion.

Only then did she remember Gokul. ‘Ayyo, some benefactor has done so much. I haven't even served him a single meal! I haven't even asked whether he would eat!’ her conscience said. She rose to her feet, tears streaming down.

“Shall I go, Gomathi?” Gokul did not finish; before he could, she had fallen at his feet!

“Stay another two days; at least it will be a little comfort to me!”

Gokul fell silent. He stood motionless. His Thirukkuvalai friend Rathinam had told him a hundred times, ‘Stay two days before you go!’ He had not listened; he had refused. Now he stood without moving. Who was he? Where had he come from? He was a man flung to the edge of a seething furnace.... Gomathi was a flower being scorched within those same flames. Circumstance had created such a situation and clapped its hands in applause. Before his eyes, little Kumar appeared and disappeared.

“Why are you leaving?.... The help you have given since last night is no small thing!.... To me, who has been left an orphan.... to me.... to me.....!”

Gomathi sobbed and sobbed, but could not finish what she was saying! The words the old man had spoken about his beloved daughter as he neared death kept gouging at Gokul's heart.

“Don't cry, Gomathi! I will stay for two days,” he said, lifting her to her feet. He wiped away the tears that kept flowing. His hands trembled then.... Before his eyes Kumar seemed to stand there and call, “Appa!”....

In Chennai, Kumar was asleep on the mattress in Gokul's house....

A dream! What would a child's dream be? Would it dream of which innocent person to beat, whom to cheat, and how to become a millionaire? Or, in pursuit of office, would it dream of how and how to cast principles to the wind, and whose favour to curry? A child's dreams would be about a rose among thorns; they would not be like destroying the bees, taking the honeycomb and squeezing it dry!

And what would the dream of a child who had lost his mother be like? A very, very tender dream! Not even like plucking a rose, but like the rose itself coming and falling upon the child's cheek! Yes, Kumar's mother lifts him up, kisses him, holds him to her chest and fondles him. She sings ‘Aararo’... He himself refuses to sleep... Morning.... She combs his hair, dresses him neatly and sends him to school. As he leaves, she stands right at the doorway, watching him from behind.....

He returns from school.... She comes forward to receive him, kisses the crown of his head, and takes him away to play.....

Kumar wakes. He cannot see his ‘dream’ mother! He takes that picture and hugs it to his chest. Tears! A waterfall springing up from the green earth!<!-- source join: scan 13 / printed 8 ends `ஊற்றெடுத்துக் கிளம்பிவரும்`; scan 14 / printed 10 begins `அருவி!`; translated jointly as the completed image above -->

Still looking at the picture—still holding it close—he sinks back onto the bed. With the wish, “Won't Amma come again in my dream?” he closes his eyes. He falls asleep. Dawn comes. The manservant and maid have seen this scene. Thinking the child might pine away from remembering Amma again and again, they gently take the mother's picture from the sleeping bud's hand and hide it. Kumar wakes. ‘Where is Amma who was in my hand? He woke up! Who could have carried her away? Could I have left her behind in the dream world?’ he thought. Never mind—tonight, ‘I can take her when I go to the dream world!’—such was the comfort the child gave himself!

Two days had passed in Ammanur.... for Gokul! Gomathi too had in some measure soothed the loss of her father through Gokul's companionship. As she sat at the shop tending to the business, yearning over something in her thoughts, he kept admiring in her face a beauty he could not have imagined, the calm hidden within that beauty, and the affection mingled with that calm. He watched closely the affection she showed the children who came to the shop to buy snacks. She would oil and comb the hair of children who came with dry, uncared-for heads; she would advise them to study well before sending them away. All this deepened Gokul's new attachment to Gomathi. He also understood that if Gomathi were left alone, the ‘rowdy’ gang roaming that village would not leave her in peace.

A young man! And so love surges!

A father! And so little Kumar stands before him!

A good man! And so arises the sense of duty to protect a helpless woman!

In the end, in the name of duty, love triumphs! His one hope was that Kumar's scales would not tip! That hope too was one reason for love's victory! The affection Gomathi showed the children who came to the shop lifted from Gokul the veil of fear he had about Kumar!

An old woman from a neighboring village had come to offer condolences for the old man's death. She was a distant relative of Gomathi. Pointing to Gokul, she asked, “Who is this young man?”

Gomathi wondered whether she should say, “A distant relative, Paatti! Related like an elder brother!” For some reason, her mind would not allow her to say that.

“He has come from the city. He's my aunt's son!” Gomathi told the old woman. Sitting on the veranda, Gokul heard her say it and rejoiced to himself. The old woman went back to her village! “Aunt's son”—those words began telling Gokul so many delightful stories. Those stories continued until Gomathi and Gokul set out together for Chennai. Gokul decided that he would indeed become her aunt's son. Gokul, who had come alone, began the journey to Chennai with Gomathi. The life journey of the two of them had begun!

The manservant and maid saw the two of them get down from the vehicle and looked at each other's faces in amazement. Gokul introduced Gomathi to them! Inwardly, they were delighted! “Where is Kumar?” Gokul asked as he entered. “He just went to school!” said the maid. Gokul looked up toward the picture of his first wife that had been hanging on the wall. The picture was not there! He felt as though he had been jolted violently!

“What happened to the picture?”

The manservant said, “The child kept looking and looking at the picture and crying more and more! So we hid it!”

Tears brimmed in Gokul's eyes! “What picture?” Gomathi asked. “Your elder sister's picture!” he said. She asked the servant for it, took the picture and looked at it. Her eyes too filled with tears. “Our lady went away with flowers and pottu, with turmeric and mangalyam still upon her!” the maid cried.

“You have come, mother! You too look like Mahalakshmi. Our little master.. Sir's beloved<!-- source join: scan 15 / printed 11 ends `செல்லக்`; scan 16 / printed 12 begins `குழந்தையை—` --> child—the diamond born from our lady's womb—please protect him without any want, Amma!” The servant himself finished saying what Gokul ought to have said. After taking leave of Gomathi, Gokul set out for the newspaper office.

That day, every article Editor Gokul wrote was full of flavour! The editorial had a fresh twist! His friends began asking the reason. When they heard it, everyone rejoiced. That day he climbed with ease the fifty steps leading up to the editor's room. Having slipped and fallen on the first step of life, and having picked up at the place where he fell the gomedhakam gem called Kumar, only now, after all these years, had he dared to set foot on the next step. He asked all his friends whether, when there was a child by the first wife, it was right or wrong to marry another woman as a second wife. He gathered together each person's experiences. Among them he received answers both favourable and unfavourable to him.

At school, little Kumar sat in a corner thinking about something. What was he going to think about—what editorial to write, like his father, about a world war? Or, like his schoolteacher, whether the agitation for a salary increase would succeed? All his thoughts were about his mother!

The boys who were playing teased him.

“Look, Gautama Buddha is deep in thought!” a mischievous boy recited the lesson he had learned that day. Little Kumar, that tender child, did not even know what “Gautama Buddha” meant! He was in a different class altogether!

When school ended, all the children ran home in groups, romping and making merry. Kumar alone walked by himself. As the children returned home, their mothers stood at the entrances, came forward to receive them, lifted them up and kissed them. There was a weariness in that bud's walk! Turmoil in his eyes! A storm in his heart! Could that young gold bear such a cruel fire? It melted and ran! ‘If I had an Amma, she too would come forward like this, lift me up, kiss me and welcome me!’

Perhaps that crescent moon sobbed just so! All the other children had gone to their own homes. Kumar alone was walking toward his house. There—his house! Who was that new figure at the doorway? Could it be... Amma? As he stepped onto the threshold, Gomathi swept him into her arms and held him close! An unexpected feeling seemed to shake Kumar's heart! Tears streamed from his eyes in torrents. Gomathi began to tremble. She thought Kumar had not accepted her. Her heart became a battlefield! This family was a plant whose bud had yet to unfold! She feared that she herself might have become hot water poured at its roots! Before long, Kumar's body began to burn with fever. Gomathi burst into tears. Gokul, returning from the office with snacks for the child, was shaken by the sight. “Look, there's Amma, dear!” he said, pointing to Gomathi.

“I don't want a fake Amma! I want my real Amma!” Kumar lamented. To Gomathi, those words were like sparks flying in a gunpowder works! Kumar's fever rose. An opportunity arose for Gomathi to remain inseparable from the child. That opportunity helped create an undying affection between them. It is in times of danger that the ‘meter’ of friendship, love and affection rises. Why—in some cases one could even say that it begins then! Were not Gokul and Gomathi's love themselves an example of that? Little by little Kumar regained his health. When he rose well again, he was a new Kumar who called Gomathi “Amma!” with all his heart. Gomathi's joy knew no bounds. She began caring for the child so much that even Gokul grew weary of it. Kumar had found an Amma. To send him to school, to come forward and receive him when he returned from school—he had found a wonderful Amma! Now Kumar<!-- source join: scan 17 / printed 13 ends `குமார்`; scan 18 / printed 14 begins `பள்ளிக்கூடத்தில்...` --> no longer sat at school like a young Buddha.... He made mischief like a valiant Napoleon.

These days, when he returned home and saw the other boys being welcomed and fussed over by their mothers, Kumar merely glanced at them with great indifference and walked on proudly.

Before, Kumar had been one leaf and one bud. Now he had become two leaves and one bud!

Gokul had to stay awake until very late at night waiting for Gomathi to come—to the bedroom! The reason was that she would come to Gokul only after telling Kumar a story, singing him a song and putting him to sleep. Gokul even felt a little vexed by it! Yet the manservant and maid frequently kept telling Kumar that a little baby was going to come to the house to play with him.

Where would that baby come from? How would it come? What would it be like? Kumar immersed himself in this investigation and waited eagerly for its arrival.

One day Gokul took ‘leave’ from the office and stayed at home. Gomathi's labour pains became severe that day. Kumar understood nothing. “Why is Amma shouting like this?” he asked his father. He asked the servants. Even if one were the great Socrates, could one answer a child's argumentative questions? Gomathi answered Kumar through action and became the mother of a baby girl. The baby Kumar had been waiting for had arrived.

“Amma, Amma! Can I take the baby with me to school?”

Kissing Kumar, Gomathi embraced him and said, “Not now, my king!” In that loving touch, the bud lost all awareness of itself.

In this world, jealousy shows its head even when people deal with one another as equals and there is abundance; so there was nothing surprising in a similar ember of jealousy flaring within Gomathi, without her knowing it, when she saw that her own child was weaker and less robust than Kumar.

From the day the baby was born, she was constantly ill! All of Gomathi's time was spent caring for her.<!-- source join: scan 18 / printed 14 ends `செலவா`; scan 19 / printed 15 begins `யிற்று.`; translated jointly as the completed sentence above -->

On top of that, Gomathi was upset that Gokul had not taken her child to a good doctor so that the illness might go away. One day she spoke angrily to her husband: “Because she's my child, you take it lightly!” The words “my child,” drawing that distinction, struck Gokul's heart like a sledgehammer blow. Women are affectionate to other children only until they themselves have a child. Perhaps even that affection is due to the old belief that only by being affectionate in that way will a child be born! Will not the new idea—that one should always be affectionate to all children—grow in women's hearts?

Thus Gokul plunged into thought, making a sweeping reckoning about women as a whole! He had never imagined Gomathi would come to the point of speaking in terms of ‘my child—your child’! ‘Where has “Mother” Gomathi gone? Why has the Gomathi whom Kumar happily placed in the position of Amma disappeared?’ Gokul became a sea of confusion! There was no need for Gomathi's affection toward Kumar to have diminished merely because the baby had arrived! Yet the baby's health had brought Gomathi to that state!

One day Kumar, with great difficulty, lifted the baby from where she was lying and carried her toward the street. Seeing it, Gomathi flew into a rage. “Hey, Moodevi—why are you carrying the child away to kill her?” she shouted, snatching the baby from him and going inside. Kumar stood there, seen through the lattice window, thinking, ‘I must have done something wrong; that's why Amma is scolding me!’ His young mouth showed a smile even while both eyes poured tears. What did that bud know of the meaning of ‘Moodevi’?

Gomathi's anxiety over her child grew stronger. She could not bear to see the baby becoming thinner day by day. One day the child developed a severe fever. Seething with anguish, Gomathi was scolding her husband, asking whether he could not bring some good doctor.<!-- source join: scan 19 / printed 15 ends `கடிந்துகொண்`; scan 20 / printed 16 begins `டிருக்கிறாள்.`; translated jointly as the completed sentence above -->

Just then a doctor appeared before them.

“Who called you and brought you here, sir?” Gokul asked in surprise.

“This fellow right here!” said the doctor, pointing to Kumar.

At once Gokul scooped up his son, embraced him and showered him with kisses.

But, contrary to Kumar's expectation, he did not receive even a drop of a kiss from his Amma! Why? Kumar thought.... Should such a young heart be engaged in such weighty thinking? ‘I don't have a real Amma! This is a fake Amma—a doll Amma!’ Such a conclusion must have dawned in his mind! He took his books and went off to school. The maid saw him on his way. After examining the child, the doctor gave medicine and said, “It is a somewhat dangerous illness; make sure the cold wind does not touch her; we'll see afterward!” and left. Gomathi's grief multiplied many times over! “My child has become like this because she wasn't properly cared for until now!” she snapped at her husband. He was bewildered. Stroke a thazhai frond from one direction and its thorn will not prick; stroke it from the opposite direction and the thorn will pierce and wound the hand! Gokul did not want to swim against the current with Gomathi and wound his heart still further. It was the anguish of an ordinary woman. He believed he could calm it through his own silence.

After school, Kumar returned home. For the past few days it had been the maid who came forward to receive him and take him in. After dinner everyone went to bed; Gomathi and the baby were on one bed! Gokul slept reclining in a chair. Kumar, who had been tossing without sleeping, slowly got up. He searched quietly in a corner of the room. What was he looking for in this darkness? He did not find what he sought in the room. Slowly he went downstairs. He searched there too. There was a tall bureau; he placed a chair against it and climbed up. Ah! There it was—what he had been looking for! His mother's picture!
