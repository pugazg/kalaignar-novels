## 4

<!-- source: scan 33; printed page: 32; Chapter 4 begins after centered `4` -->

The beauty of Azhagappan's dreams of desire was in no way less than the beauty of Chintamani's dreams of delight. He rejoiced at the thought that, in his sprawling mansion, at every hour of every day, he would be able to feast his eyes on the marvellous sight of a peacock without ankle-bells performing, merely through the grace of her walk, a dance no nattuvanar had trained her to perform. His heart had until then been a wayside rest-house in which thoughts of love came like pilgrims, stayed for a while, and then departed. Now it would be demolished and rebuilt as a fortress permanently guarded by memories of love. His mother longed to see her son married before she closed her eyes forever.

He, for his part, wanted a life-partner who would be a feast for his eyes. The photograph that had come from Vedapuram seemed made to fulfil that wish. Holding the photograph in his hand, he built and enjoyed an empire of imagination: what would those eyes look like if they moved? What flood of beauty would pour out if those lips opened? He was the son of a great house. For people like him, passing leisure itself was a major nuisance. A little parrot was coming from Vedapuram to relieve that nuisance! He was desperate somehow to see her once in person. Only his mother was supposed to go to see the bride's house. His friend Naina was to accompany her. Naina was short for Naina Muhammad. Azhagappan and Naina were dearest friends, from the same street, and had studied in the same school. Both were equally capable of earning a "zero" in examinations. Azhagappan regarded the world as something larger than the circle immediately around him. To Naina, the world was a playground. Azhagappan thought deeply about everything. Naina brushed everything aside lightly. Life, according to Azhagappan, was a beautiful garden that a man must deliberately invite into being.

Whether it turned out to be a garden or a wasteland, Naina's conclusion was that life was simply a road a man had to cross without refusing to go along with it.

Their two conclusions often collided. Whenever they did, Azhagappan would become a slave to intense ideas. For Naina, the collision was a comedy play. He would make himself its leading character, put all his effort into making Azhagappan laugh, and in the end he would succeed.

<!-- source join: scan 33 ends `பெண்வீடு பார்ப்பதற்குத் தாயார் மட்டுமே`; scan 34 begins `போவதாக இருக்கிறாள்.` -->

Azhagappan raised the subject of the journey to Vedapuram with Naina. Little by little he revealed that he wanted somehow to go along and see the girl himself.

"You've seen the photograph. Isn't that enough?" Naina teased.

"A photograph doesn't move! It doesn't speak! It doesn't smile!" said Azhagappan.

<!-- source: scan 35; printed page: 34 -->

"Oh-ho! So desire wants to know whether she's a swan or a peacock! You need to hear whether her speech is a parrot's or a cuckoo's—to see whether her smile is a string of jasmine or a necklace of pearls—is that it?"

Naina's teasing became too much. Azhagappan lowered his head in embarrassment.

"Why give all this such importance, man? To keep the sun off a shaved head, you need a cap—that's all. Must the cap be made of silk, glittering with woven zari and all the rest? Same thing here... For life, a woman! That's all, isn't it?"

"Hey, Naina! If you don't shave your head in the first place, you don't need even the cap!"

"Shaving the head is a religious custom. In the same way, marriage is a custom of life!"

"Then wearing the cap is...?"

"Whether the life-custom exists or not, you must wear the cap! Haven't you seen some people put on a cap over cropped hair without shaving their heads? Same thing! Azhagappa, in this example, the cap means the woman!"

"Not like a cap for the head—a woman comes to run a family!"

"What then, a crown? Azhagappa! I know this much: I've seen any number of women who arrive like a cap for the head and, in the end, get worn down and taken off like a slipper from the foot!"

"All right, all right! Don't expand your research. Please stop!"

<!-- source: scan 36; printed page: 35 -->

"It's not research, man—it's truth from experience! Anyway, just as you wished, you've got a girl like a painting. We'll go and see whether that painting speaks or not, whether it smiles, whether it has teeth when it smiles—or none—whether it walks, whether it has legs to walk, whether it limps—and all the rest. You just stay without worrying!"

"Naina, everything is a game to you! You won't be able to see the girl!"

"Why? Do you have some 'gosha' system like ours?"

"Nothing like that. My father-in-law is a great Sanatana Hindu. I doubt whether he'll even allow you inside!"

"Is that true, or are you making it up so that you can come?"

"It's true, Naina, it's true!"

"Then I'll go without a lungi and without a cap!"

"Will Father-in-law think you're a Hindu? Idiot! Your head is shaved—won't that give you away?"

"I'll say I went to Tirupati. What, Azhagappa, now you're trying to pierce even my ears!"

"Hey! If you're to pass as a Hindu, you'll need to have your ears pierced too!"

"I know! A Hindu is one who gets his ears pierced! Fine, I'll have them pierced. Religion is just humans making a few changes to the limbs God created for every human being, isn't it?"

<!-- source: scan 37; printed page: 36 -->

"Enough, enough! Have you plunged into research on religion now? That'll surely end well!"

"Whatever research I plunge into, in the end it will be only your mother and me going to Vedapuram!"

"Mother has only half her sight. She won't even be able to look at the girl properly!"

"Oh-ho—if you go, you'll look properly and come back, will you? Azhagappa, this isn't some girl from an entertainment house who will stand before the groom, sing songs, dance, display every skill she has learned, and win his consent to the wedding! You won't even be able to see the half that Mother sees!"

"Naina! I'm telling you once and for all. I must come!"

"Your father-in-law has written separately: the groom must not come. If he comes, it seems the evil eye in the village may become too strong! And you're not supposed to set foot in that house this month. Your mother alone may enter. That's what it says in the panchangam!"

"Tear up that panchangam and throw it away! Burn it!"

"There's no merit in that. Become a minister and issue an order banning panchangams altogether!"

"Naina, I'm coming to Vedapuram too! I'm ashamed to tell Mother myself. You tell her."

Seeing that Azhagappan's stubbornness could not be changed, Naina gave way. It was decided that they would leave for Vedapuram on the day the astrologer had chosen according to Mother's wishes. Azhagappan, Naina and his mother set out by car after sending a telegram to Vedapuram.

<!-- source join: scan 37 ends `அழகப்பன்,`; scan 38 begins `நயினா, தாயார்...` -->
<!-- source: scan 38; printed page: 37 -->

Wanting to reach Vedapuram before noon on Friday, they had left Bangalore during the night itself. Bangalore and Vedapuram were at least three hundred miles apart. Because his elderly mother was in the car, they could only drive slowly. Naina kept Azhagappan and his mother in good spirits by telling amusing stories. Dawn was about to break. At that very hour Chintamani was happily thinking about the people who were coming to see the bride's house. Azhagappan's car was then moving slowly toward Vedapuram, still forty miles away.

His mother, exhausted, leaned back in the car and dozed for a while. Thinking the driver must be tired, Azhagappan gave him a rest and began driving himself.

"Don't drive too fast in your hurry, Azhagu! Mother is sleeping too," Naina warned teasingly. Azhagu had driven a car on countless days, but he had never felt the special excitement he felt now. Naina, sitting beside him, seemed to understand his excitement.

"In a few days, with women in this car, what a time this car will have! Will I even be able to sit beside you?" he laughed.

"Hey—Mother!" Azhagappan signalled.

"Can't you see Mother is asleep?" Naina said and began again.

"Azhagu! I'll ask you a question. Answer it. A white man and a white woman were travelling in a car, it seems. The husband was driving, the wife beside him. While the car was still moving, he kissed his wife beside him. After receiving the kiss she asked, 'When you kissed me, was your attention on my cheek or on the road ahead?' If it had been you, what would you have answered? Tell me!"

<!-- source join: scan 38 ends `காரில்`; scan 39 begins `போய்க்கொண்டிருந்தார்களாம்.` -->
<!-- source: scan 39; printed page: 38 -->

"I'll try out your story in practice first, then answer your question!"

"If the newspaper reports one day that a man coming the other way in Bangalore was hit by a car and lost his head, that'll mean you tried out my question! Right?"

"So you're saying my attention won't be on the road?"

Even as Azhagappan asked that, the car narrowly escaped crashing into a tree. Naina laughed and patted Azhagu.

A signpost announced that Vedapuram was still ninety miles away. A little after they passed it, the road narrowed and thick scrub appeared on both sides. The car entered the narrow road. Azhagappan, whose heart had been darting back and forth between Vedapuram and the car, suddenly stopped. "What? What?" asked Naina. His mother woke too. A human figure lay across the road.

"Someone's been hit and killed," Azhagappan said as he got out of the car. The moment he stepped down, seven or eight men surrounded the car with a great shout. The man who had been lying across the road also leapt up with a terrifying laugh. Azhagappan and Naina had no idea what to do. His mother screamed. "Ayyo! Leave us! We'll give you whatever you ask!" she begged, crying. "Take me if you want! Don't do anything to my son!" she wailed, revealing the height of a mother's love.

<!-- source join: scan 39 ends `சாலையின் குறுக்கே`; scan 40 begins `கிடந்தவனும்...` -->
<!-- source: scan 40; printed page: 39 -->

A blow landed on Naina's back. Naina began fighting them. Azhagappan had no other choice. The driver entered the fight too. For a short time the road became a battlefield. How long could three men hold off seven or eight—especially a gang that had eaten Tiger's food and fattened their bodies on it? Azhagappan struggled to manage them.

Suddenly an idea came to Naina. He sprang toward the front of the car, took the revolver kept there, and shouted, "Azhagappa! Here! Shoot! Shoot!" as he threw it to him. The moment the revolver reached Azhagappan's hand, Tiger's men, frightened, ran toward Naina's side. "Hmm! Run away! I'll shoot!" Azhagappan warned. The ruffians shouted, "If you shoot us, we'll strangle him to death!" and closed around Naina. Naina, staggering, fought them off.

"I can't bear it any longer! I'll have to shoot!" Azhagappan shouted loudly and pulled the trigger. Two explosions. "Ayyo! You've killed me, Azhagappa!" Naina Muhammad cried and fell to the ground with a thud. Azhagappan's body trembled. His hand went limp. His mother beat herself and wailed. The ruffians began running so fast their heels seemed to strike the backs of their heads.

<!-- source join: scan 40 ends `அடித்து மோதிக்`; scan 41 begins `கொண்டு அழுதாள்.` -->
<!-- source: scan 41; printed page: 40 -->

Azhagappan dropped the revolver and collapsed over Naina Muhammad. Was the shock of having killed his dearest friend an ordinary thing? In that frenzy he fell upon him.

"Hey! That hurts, man! Why are you falling on me like a cow?" Naina Muhammad laughed and sprang upright.

Azhagu stared in astonishment. "Naina! You're not dead?"

"No one can match your aim, Azhagappa! Good thing that, for the spot you aimed at, this turned out to be a toy pistol instead of a real revolver!"

"Quick—quick! Get in!" he urged, making everyone sit in the car. Filled with the joy of having received life anew, they resumed the journey.

Sivanesar and Sivakami, who had returned after leaving Chintamani in the hospital, waited to receive the groom's people. A little later than the stated time—but without missing by even a moment the auspicious period the astrologer had named—the groom's car stopped before Chintamani's house. Sivanesar and Sivakami went out, received them and offered hospitality.

"I thought the groom would not come?" Sivanesar said.

"He didn't want to come. I was the one who forced him and dragged him along," Naina Muhammad replied, introducing himself as he spoke.

<!-- source join: scan 41 ends `தன்னைத்தானே`; scan 42 begins `அறிமுகப்படுத்திக்கொண்டு...` -->
<!-- source: scan 42; printed page: 41 -->

"The alignment isn't right for the groom to come to the house now. The panchangam says he should come only next month! That's why I thought... he shouldn't come..." Sivanesar dragged out the words. His Sanatana mind, enslaved to rites and sastras, refused to accept the groom's arrival against the planetary indications. Nor could he keep that feeling from showing.

"Even if the planets aren't right, the astrologer said some dosha-remedy would set it right! That's why we brought the boy!"

"Removing dosha or whatever else—that's all trickery! Lies astrologers tell for a living!"

"But the astrologer said the very same panchangam that gives the planetary results also gives remedies for the dosha!" Naina said.

"What do you know about it, man? You're one religion and we're another!" Sivanesar said sharply and put an end to the discussion. Naina looked at Azhagappan's face and laughed teasingly. A foolish embarrassment spread across Azhagu's face. Needling like this was ordinary for Naina. Then his mother told them how they had escaped the thieves on the road. "All of it is the consequence of going against the planetary indications!" Sivanesar pointedly declared.

If he had said anything of that kind once more, Azhagappan would have taken his mother and returned to Bangalore; that was how angry he had become. Only after Sivakami advised Sivanesar, "If you speak like this to people who have come to our house, won't they think badly of us?" did he calm down a little. The mother's gaze moved around the whole house. Because her eyesight was poor, she peered very closely in every direction. Azhagappan was doing much the same. At last Sivakami gently began the subject and told them about Chintamani's accident.

<!-- source join: scan 42 ends `ஏற்`; scan 43 begins `பட்டுவிட்டது` -->
<!-- source: scan 43; printed page: 42 -->

Azhagappan was astonished that danger had befallen his future wife on the same day it had befallen him. His mother wanted to go to the hospital and see Chintamani.

"The omens were bad on the road when you came; here too the omens are not good. So seeing Chintamani today doesn't seem very proper. Another day..." Sivanesar drew it out.

With everything turning upside down, the mother was somewhat shocked. Sivakami understood and felt distressed.

"What's wrong with seeing a girl who is unwell? Wouldn't I want to see her too?" the mother insisted stubbornly.

Naina seconded her. Azhagappan also indicated the same view by gesture.

Sivanesar thought everyone was speaking against the sastras.

"Are all of you atheists?" he asked the mother with anger mixed with mockery.

<!-- source: scan 44; printed page: 43 -->

"Siva! Siva!" the mother cried, striking her mouth in horror.

In the end Sivanesar's opinion prevailed. It was decided that the groom's family would return home that night and come again to see the bride's house on another good day. Azhagappan and Naina went out to look around the village. A good idea occurred to Azhagappan and he revealed it to Naina.

"This is the right time for us to see Chintamani. We can go to the hospital and see her casually. What do you say?" he asked. How could Naina refuse?

The two inquired their way to Anandi's hospital and reached it. Tiger was there. Anandi was there. Azhagappan said he wanted to see Chintamani. At first Anandi refused. Then Tiger recommended it: "They seem to be relatives of hers. All right, let them see her and go."

Azhagappan and Naina were admitted as relatives. Vembu took them inside, introduced them to a nurse and said, "Take them to where Chintamani is," then turned back. The nurse led them away.

Azhagappan was troubled by the question of whether he should tell Chintamani who he was. Along with it another question burrowed into him: was it right to come like this without Sivanesar knowing? A man beginning to do something wrong often feels greater fear when he reaches the top than when laying the foundation. Azhagappan had reached that state. The nurse took them to the bed where Chintamani lay.

<!-- source join: scan 44 ends `நர்சு அவர்`; scan 45 begins `களச் சிந்தாமணி...` -->
<!-- source: scan 45; printed page: 44; Chapter 4 carryover before centered `5` -->

"Hmm! This is Chintamani—she's fast asleep. Don't wake her!" the nurse said.

Azhagappan looked eagerly toward the bed. His eyes looked as though fire had struck them. "Ah!" he screamed. Chintamani woke with a start. Beside her lay a baby.

"Shh! Don't make a sound—she's exhausted from childbirth. Don't trouble her," the nurse said authoritatively.

"You sinners! Thank goodness—I escaped! Is this why that Sivanesar kept stopping me by putting all the burden on days and stars? You cheats!" Azhagappan shouted in fury. Naina Muhammad clapped a hand over his mouth and hurriedly dragged him outside.
