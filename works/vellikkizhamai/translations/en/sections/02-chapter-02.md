## 2

<!-- source: scan 13; printed page: 12 -->

Her marriage had been settled. They were coming from Bangalore to see the bride's house. Even that was only for form's sake. Everything else that had to be discussed had already been discussed—the bride had seen the groom's photograph, the groom had seen the bride's, and both had expressed their approval. All that remained was to fix a date for the wedding the following month... Before that, according to custom, the bride's house and the groom's house had to be seen. The groom's house had already been seen.

The Bangalore groom came from a very wealthy household. He owned a great deal of property. There were more attendants and helpers than anyone could need. A whole spread of houses—almost a palace! And he was the sole owner of it all. He was the only son in the family. His mother was an elderly widow. The young man's name was Azhagappan. One had to call it a name that suited him: he was that striking a young bull of a man. After seeing his photograph, Chintamani was like a golden beetle that had found a flower garden. Lips on which a smile seemed always to rest. A budding moustache that polished and beautified them. A prominent nose. An upward, proud gaze. If that silent photograph itself had the power to sketch a preface to an epic of love, how magnetic must the living figure be when seen in person? Chintamani delighted in the thought. Her parents gave a short answer to the happy question overflowing within her.

"An Arjuna in beauty—don't ask anything beyond that!" said her father Sivanesar, a man who had mastered the Bharata.

<!-- source: scan 14; printed page: 13 -->

Andal happened to be standing nearby. She was a mischief-maker—would she keep that restless mouth shut and stay quiet?

"Only in beauty, uncle, he is Arjuna, isn't he? Not in his deeds, surely?" she asked. Chintamani was delighted that Andal had asked the very question she herself wanted to ask.

"In character, he is Dharma himself, like Dharman," said Chintamani's mother Sivakami.

"Does he have a habit of horse-racing?" Andal shot back at once.

"You mischievous donkey—if he mounts a horse... ah! He looks like Maharaja Shivaji."

"Ah... tell me comparisons like that—ones I know!" Andal said, laughing.

Chintamani stood there with honey filling every thought. This exchange had taken place on the day they returned after seeing the groom's house.

Chintamani and her parents were very pleased with the Bangalore alliance. "Why wouldn't they be! A handsome specimen, and they say a lakhpati too! Sivanesar has really been struck by fortune!" That was how the talk spread through the village. For Chintamani, a beautiful future came and stood dancing in her dreams and thoughts.

She was bathing in the pond. Her thoughts were dancing too.

"Father said he was as handsome as Arjuna!... Ah! How blessed I am! Whether the Bharata really happened or not—whether it is truth or tall talk—I don't care about any of that.... The character Arjuna who appears in it is a handsome man. They say Goddess Parvati herself wished to see the beauty of his face and told her husband Paramasiva of that desire. To fulfil his wife's wish, Paramasiva is said to have drawn Arjuna into a trial of strength and, while the two fought, turned Arjuna's face toward Parvati so that she could see and enjoy his beauty. Such was Arjuna's beauty. Otherwise, would Alli have become his captive? Would Pavalakkodi have twined herself around him? Would Subhadra have gone circling around him! They compare my man to that king of beauty. Am I fit to stand before him? Even with a ladder, my worth could not reach his! A fruit beyond reach—a thing I could never obtain—has come into my hands. When I was little I used to throw tantrums and ask Mother to catch the moon and give it to me. It feels as though, after trying and trying, she has only now caught that moon and placed it in my hands!"

* * *

On the heart's bridal bed spread with the silk of youth and strewn with the jasmine of thoughts, that future in which I would lay my man upon my lap and feast my eyes on him......

He would call me, "Silver platter born of the sky!" and I would answer, "Blessing born to this earth!" He would droop in defeat and lean upon my shoulder... ah, that future......

He would seize the hands with which I covered his eyes in play and pull me close... I would resist... "It is in resistance that the delight of love grows, girl! Give me the honey of your lips," he would say—and take it before I gave it! I would tell myself that what I had waited for had happened. Why must that blessed day be so slow in coming?

<!-- source: scan 15; printed page: 14 -->

A pleasure boat—with a graceful song upon it—the lovers of stories—the white doves of the city of delight that appear in romantic scenes: there would be no wonder if all of them had to concede defeat and flee. The deathless characters of the great epics of love ought to feel shy before me. The whole village should praise us: Is she not the lamp of domestic life? Is this not a good family—an example of the saying that a family is a university of many arts?...

* * *

Chintamani had submerged her body in water and her heart in thoughts.

When those pleasurable thoughts struck her heart, even the touch of the water's ripples upon her body made her feel shy.

She compared the embrace of the waves with the soft hands of the bridegroom she had imagined and blushed to herself.

On her body, spread with the softness of a rose—at her tender feet—the little fish in the pond nibbled and nibbled, creating a new excitement. Competing with those little fish that bit her body and gave her affectionate trouble, her friends began biting at her heart with their lips and tormenting her. A bite in which no teeth touched! Yet was she not a vine in the bloom of youth?

Struck by thousands upon thousands of feelings, she lost herself. Andal, Radha and the other girls painted Chintamani's beautiful future with a brush dipped in mockery. Liquor tastes bitter when first poured into the mouth. Yet when people think of the pleasurable intoxication it gives a little later, they do not reject that first bitterness altogether; they stop with a mere grimace. That same grimace appeared on Chintamani's face when her friends teased her. In truth, she longed all the more for them to keep talking a little longer about him. Enduring the bitterness while asking for "more, more," she became the perfect example of a man drinking toddy.

"Do you know Chintamani's husband's name, girls?" one asked.

"Bilvamangala?" another replied.

"Go away... don't you have any work?" said Chintamani.

"Why, Chintamani dear, they say your mother-in-law can't see properly—only half her sight—and can walk only by feeling her way and stumbling!"

"That's wonderful! Chintamani will have a grand time! She can spend night and day with her man in the inner rooms and fool the old lady by saying, 'I was in the kitchen, Mother.'"

"Then who will supervise the cooking, girl?"

"In that case she'll seat her man right there in the kitchen and, when Mother asks, lie that 'he has gone out'!"

"Andal, what shall we give Chintamani as a wedding present?"

"I'm going to give her a paladai feeding cup—and a kendi! Radha, what about you?"

"A cradle!"

"Susheela! You?"

<!-- source: scan 17; printed page 16; next reply begins scan 18 -->

"I'm going to give her a clock that doesn't run. Then they can go on talking of love without any limit of time."

"Hey, Saro! What about you?"

"I'm going to give her an easy-commentary edition of the Kamba Ramayanam—to learn what she hasn't learned!"

"Kanta! What's your gift?"

"There's a thing called a 'tape recorder,' isn't there? That!

"Record what they say at night... listen to it in the morning... a fine device for making them look at one another and blush!"

"Right enough—Kanta is spilling out experience! She's a married woman, isn't she?...... All right...... Jaya! What's your gift?"

"My gift? Two iron shields for Chintamani's cheeks!"

"Enough—enough, stop it, with your presents and your pomp!" Chintamani cried, scooping up water and splashing it over them.

"Tell us, dear! Who all are coming today to see the bride's house?" Andal asked.

"I don't know—the mother will come!"

"Whose mother?"

"His mother, of course!"

"Whose mother?"

Chintamani's cheeks now became true red lotuses. Without answering, she plunged her head under the water.

<!-- source: scan 18; printed page: 17 -->

"How did they suddenly decide to come and see the bride's house?"

"They had said they would choose a day and send a telegram. The telegram came yesterday, it seems, saying they would come today!"

"Yes, yes; today is Friday—a good day! Looks as though the groom's household is very orthodox too!"

"Both sets of in-laws are orthodox old-timers! Who knows what the groom is like?"

"Will the groom also come to see the bride, Chintamani?"

They prodded at the mouth of Chintamani, who had been bathing without saying anything.

"He may come," said that peacock in the bloom of youth, barely moving her lips.

At that, all of them burst into peals of laughter.

The next bathing steps were approached by a dignified-looking man brushing his teeth, savouring the sound of their laughter as he came.

The laughter stopped at once. They spoke softly among themselves.

"Hey, hey... look there! Tiger! Tiger!" they pointed out to one another in fear.

"Has that frog come... that mandukam?" Chintamani asked, her voice mixing mockery and anger.

"I told you earlier that we should go home quickly! Did any of you listen when I said we should leave before that wretch laid eyes on us?" Kanta lamented.

<!-- source: scan 19; printed page: 18 -->

Standing on the next set of bathing steps, the man devoured them with his eyes. He did indeed look like someone they might call Tiger. The trace of a beastly nature seemed carved into his face. Terror flashed in his large eyes. Even the manner in which he brushed his teeth made it plain that he was tormented by a fire of lust blazing in his heart and scorching his body.

Chintamani and her friends hurried to climb out of the pond. As he stood facing them and cast an animal stare without caring about anything else, another man laid a hand on his shoulder and shouted, "What, Tiger! Looks as though you've forgotten the whole world?"

Startled, Tiger said, "Oh! Vembu? Come, man—come!... Did you see? ...It's as though heaven itself has been imported into this sacred pond!" He spoke loudly enough for the women to hear.

"Oh-ho! So that's why Lord Indra has paid a visit this way?" Vembu said.

"Hey, Vembu—heard the news?...... Chintamani's marriage has been fixed!"

"Hey! Is that so? When?"

"They haven't fixed the date yet... today they're coming to see the bride's house!"

"Then... what happened to your story?"

"My story is only going to continue from now on. Yesterday I went to Sivanesar and asked for the girl one last time. I told him about all the old family ties between me and that old fellow and argued, like a lawyer, that Chintamani belonged to me!"

<!-- source: scan 20; printed page: 19 -->

"What was the judgment?"

"Not favourable! The old monkey refused to give me the parrot!"

"What does the parrot say?"

"The little parrot repeats whatever it is told! Whatever the old man and old woman say, she says the same!"

"Then... what are you planning to do?"

"I'll see to it that her marriage cannot take place! They say they're coming to see the bride's house—the bride's house!...... Do you think they're going to arrive there smoothly?"

"What are you saying, Tiger?"

"You know Tiger's way of taking matters into his own hands...... I'm going to take every step necessary to make Chintamani mine."

"That's right—bravo, Tiger! What else? In this village you're a big man! A 'minor' among minors, a great man among great men! The whole village is afraid of you... and only this useless Sivanesar refuses to respect you...... You mustn't let that go!"

"Vembu!... The groom's car coming from Bangalore... it'll have to come along the road, won't it?"

"Without a doubt!"

"Watch—the car won't arrive in one piece!"

"Ah! That's splendid!"

"Right at the start, on the way here... an ill omen... the groom's people are going to receive it properly before they ever arrive!"

"Tiger! Are the men all ready?"

"Everything's ready—I was looking for you. Good thing you've come back to the village too!"

"The lady doctor has arrived, hasn't she?"

<!-- source: scan 21; printed page 20; reply begins scan 22 -->

"She has!...... Wasn't it she who asked me to bring you? Madam has been lying there pining for ten days without seeing you!"

"Is that so?...."

Tiger let the sweetness of the thought linger as he stepped into the pond and washed his face. By then Chintamani and the others had begun climbing out, one after another. Chintamani came last, her sari wet—and with a new beauty born of it—a pot at her waist as she climbed the steps. At that moment a small stone Tiger threw struck her shoulder.

Chintamani started and turned. The sudden shock made her lose her footing. Her foot landed on moss covering the step. She could not recover and slipped. She rolled down five or six steps and fell face-first into the water. Her friends rushed down, gathered around her and lifted her up. Tiger and Vembu were nowhere to be seen; they had vanished. Chintamani had suffered a very hard blow to the chest and lost consciousness. She did not speak; only a faint breath remained. Her friends lifted her and walked toward her house. Sivanesar and Sivakami cried out, wept and lamented. The people of the street said she would survive only if she were rushed to a doctor's house. Sivakami wailed, "Ayyo—on a good day like this—just when the groom's family is coming to see the house—how could this happen!" The villagers rebuked her: "Is this the time to cry?"

Chintamani was taken to Lady Doctor Anandi's hospital. A grave doubt arose in Sivanesar and Sivakami's minds: would she survive?
