## 10

<!-- source: scan 85; printed page 84; post-centered 10 -->

Filled with satisfaction as he thought of the oath he had obtained from Sivakami, Tiger walked straight toward Anandi’s hospital. He knew Sivakami feared breaking an oath and, above all, would never dare act against her family deity. That strengthened his confidence even further.

His boots went *thud-thud* as he planted his feet quickly on the stairs leading to the upper floor of the hospital. From below, Vembu called up with a teasing laugh, “What is it, sir?”

<!-- source: scan 86; printed page 85 -->

“The matter is almost completely settled, Vembu! The person is safe, isn’t she?” Tiger asked eagerly.

“Oh! The goods are inside, still locked up exactly as before! You can go—go and fulfil your wish!” Vembu mocked.

“No, Vembu! Right now I am going to take her back and leave her at her own house. From now on, there is no doubt at all that she and I will certainly be married!” Tiger said excitedly and ran up the stairs.

Seeing Tiger’s happiness and excitement, Vembu scratched his head. “All right, my job here is done. I’ll go to the Palaiyur hospital and attend to what Madam needs.”

Tiger dismissed him, pressed a ten-rupee note into his hand and said, “There is a man named Naina Muhammad at the Palaiyur hospital. Keep an eye on what he is doing too.”

Vembu touched the note reverently to his eyes, showed his teeth in a grin and left.

Tiger opened the upstairs door and entered. A large room there was also locked. He opened that door too. Darkness filled the room. He switched on the electric light. In the light he saw Chintamani lying rolled on the floor. Her hands and feet were tied.

Pretending to be struck by shock, Tiger cried, “Ah! What horror is this! Chintamani! Who has tortured you like this?” and released her from the bonds.

Exhausted, Chintamani rose to her feet.

Again Tiger asked, with apparent urgency and anger, “Tell me, Chintamani! Who put you through this suffering? Why?”

<!-- source: scan 87; printed page 86 -->

He acted the part with great skill.

“Why ask as though you know nothing? I understand very well that all of this was arranged by you!”

“Empty imagination! Chintamani—even in a dream I would never bring such suffering upon you. Believe me. That Vembu fellow—”

“He is the one who brought me here, saying I had to stay in the patients’ ward upstairs! He is the one who locked me in this room and went away saying, ‘Wait until Tiger comes.’ Do you understand now?”

Sparks rose in Chintamani’s eyes. Her face grew redder with anger.

“That idiot Vembu has babbled foolishly—the fool,” Tiger muttered to himself as he paced the room. He had come planning somehow to make Chintamani believe he was innocent, to tell her that he had come only to save her and, through that, to make her happy. Her answer had been the opposite of what he expected.

“You must believe what I tell you, Chintamani! This is the work of Lady Doctor Anandi, done to separate you and me! She has an uncontrollable passion for me. I have one for you. She understood that. She was waiting for a chance to separate us. This morning at the pond, in playful love, I threw a stone at you; you turned and cast a sidelong glance at me; by an act of fate you fell and came to this hospital. That made it easy for Anandi to separate you and me. She hid you where no one could see you and arranged some dreadful scheme. Before she could finish it, God paid her wages. She has been caught in a car accident and lies in the Palaiyur hospital fighting for her life!

<!-- source join: scan 87 ends `ஆனந்திக்கு உன்னையும்`; scan 88 begins `என்னையும்...` -->
<!-- source: scan 88; printed page 87 -->

“Your parents searched all over the village when they found you missing and finally came running to me. I called that Vembu fellow, threatened him, frightened him that I would kill him—and then he spat out the truth. Only after that did I run here. I found you! I freed you!

“You said Vembu told you, ‘Wait until Tiger comes.’ Do you understand what that means? It was his arrogant way of saying, ‘No one can save you. Even if your beloved Tiger comes, he cannot!’ You listen to his words and suspect me.”

Tiger finished his performance after opening up this sackful of lies. Chintamani could neither believe him nor entirely disbelieve him. She suffered in confusion.

“Whatever the truth may be, it is enough if you take me home,” she said.

“That is exactly what I am waiting to do. I ran here only to take you home. You are mine! If I do not protect you, whom else will I protect?”

“Tiger! There is no need for useless talk. Let us go home.”

<!-- source: scan 89; printed page 88 -->

“We can go, Chintamani—but one request! It has now become certain that the two of us are going to marry and live as husband and wife. So......”

“So......?”

Unable even to finish his words, Tiger moved closer to her.

Chintamani understood his intention. Her body and mind trembled. Fear of what might happen melted her tender heart. The pain in her heart felt as though a broad iron hand had gathered a rich hanging bunch of grapes and crushed it all at once. The pain that rose in her chest then exceeded the pain caused by her fall at the pond.

A woman might lose anything—but could she bring herself to lose her honour? Surpanakha lost her nose and lost her breasts, yet because she did not lose her state of chastity, was she not still able to claim the distinction of being Ravana’s sister? Chintamani imagined Tiger as though Lakshmana stood before her. The fear that she might become a mullai flower crushed in his rough hands deepened her faintness.

She steadied herself. Somewhere, in some court, a lawyer argues a case of sexual violation.

The lawyer appearing for the accused is said to have ended his argument like this: “It is said that this woman was violated by this man. It is also explained that the act took place against her wishes. If a woman refuses to the very end, it is no ordinary matter for a man to succeed against that resistance. At the place where the force is said to have occurred, she was not tied up. Nor was she rendered unconscious. Yet we are told she lost her chastity. That is surprising. Here—I am moving a needle in my hand. Try threading it!”

He was said to have taken a needle and moved it gently, and no one could pass a thread through it.

<!-- source join: scan 89 ends `மறுத்துவிட்`; scan 90 begins `டால்` -->
<!-- source: scan 90; printed page 89 -->

“In the same way, if the woman’s mind had stubbornly continued to resist, this man could not have succeeded. He succeeded only because she too yielded at least a little to feeling. Therefore both are guilty!” the lawyer was said to have declared.

Chintamani could not remember whether she had heard of this case from someone somewhere or read about it. But at that moment the case and the lawyer’s explanation returned to her mind. She became the moving needle.

Tiger, surging toward her, seized her hands and pleaded, “My dear! My dear!!”

She knocked his hands away and moved farther off.

“Tiger! Is this the fine way you came to save me? Leave this place properly and go!” she shouted.

His confidence fell. The thought that he could bend her to his will turned into a thorn. Yet one temptation remained: the marriage, after all, was now certain to take place. He decided he must not create a confusion like this and spoil matters before then. So he did not move closer to her again.

<!-- source: scan 91; printed page 90 -->

“All right, Chintamani! You seem to think such things should not happen before marriage. That old-fashioned custom has not left you. Never mind. Come—I will take you home exactly as you wish,” Tiger said, changing the whole manner of his speech.

Chintamani was inwardly afraid. Tiger understood that she feared he might pretend to take her home and instead carry her somewhere else.

“Do not worry that I will trick you and take you somewhere. I am not coming with you. I will accompany you only as far as the entrance of this hospital and see you off. After that you can go home yourself,” he said.

Chintamani thought that was a good idea, but wondered whether her body was strong enough to walk all the way home. So she asked for a vehicle.

“I’ll bring one now,” Tiger said. He went downstairs and brought a two-bullock cart.

Chintamani struggled down the stairs. Tiger longed to use even that excuse of helping her as a chance to touch her body. But she came down slowly, supporting herself on the handrail.

It was a high, covered, two-bullock cart. One had to leap up in a single movement to climb into it. Chintamani hesitated. Tiger stood ready to help. He looked at the cartman.

“Hey! Take her and leave her at her house. Here is your fare,” he said, extending eight annas.

The cartman took the money. “Come on, Amma! Get in,” he urged.

Chintamani had to climb into the cart. Even a healthy person needed to know a little “high jump” to get into it. Her body and mind were weakened and she had no energy, so she struggled. Finally she gathered herself and tried to spring into the cart. She did not succeed. Tiger did.

Her body had reached the edge of the cart only up to the waist; her legs hung below. She could neither lift her legs and seat herself fully nor dare jump back down. Tiger used the moment well.

He ran forward, swept her into his arms and seated her on the cart. To Chintamani it felt as though he had lifted her only to throw her into a pit of fire.

Then words more cruel than that fire came from somewhere and struck her ears.

“You prostitute! You whore!”

Covering her ears, Chintamani turned and looked. Sivanesar was running furiously toward the cart.

“Father!” Chintamani screamed and fell from inside the cart to the ground.
