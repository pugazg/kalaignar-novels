# Friday

## 1

<!-- source: scan 4; printed page: — -->

“Tomorrow dawns Friday. A very special day for me. How many Fridays have come and gone in my life; many more are yet to come. Yet where has this one Friday alone acquired such immense importance! The more I think of it, the sweeter my heart feels. My wonder only grows. When I was young—in my school days—the day on which my mother gave birth to me was simply Sunday. But now the question, ‘Even if a thousand Sundays came, could they equal the Friday that is about to come to me?’ rises majestically in my heart like a warrior riding on horseback with a banner flying. How is that?

“This evening a band of mendicants went along the street begging. One of them went singing, ‘The horse was born on Friday, Raja Desingu.’ I have read that song many times in our large-print Raja Desingu book; I have heard it too. Why should it stir in me today a feeling it never stirred before! Whether Raja Desingu’s horse was born on Friday or Saturday—or whether it had never been born at all—my heart used to pour out a dismissive laugh. Why is that rustic song alone now sinking deep into it and producing some<!-- source join: scan 4 ends `ஏதோ`; scan 5 begins `இன்பக்கனவுகளோ` --> sweet dreams? My friend Kili in the next street says that in her Christian religion one Friday in the year is a Friday of great significance, a day sacred to Jesus, and she goes to church. Even then Friday never acquired such great importance in my heart.

<!-- source: scan 5; printed page: 4 -->

“‘On a Friday of all days—without cleaning the house and threshold, without smearing the floor with cow-dung, without drawing a kolam and beautifying the place—can a family woman be so lazy?’ I have seen mothers-in-law in many houses turn that question into a thunderbolt for battle. I have also seen them light a fine lamp for the household deity on Friday evening and make an offering.

“I remember well a Bhagavathar at a comic concert held in our street saying that Friday was as sacred to women as the mangalyam, turmeric and kumkum, and winning the unanimous applause of the women of the street. Whenever anyone starts listing the names of the weekdays, laughter wells up in me.

“Monday—Somavaram! A divine day, they say, for the Somavara fast! ‘Tuesday—or an empty mouth!’ they say! You may get hold of gold, but never of Wednesday, they say—such a rare day it is. I even joke that only the fellow who begins sleeping on Tuesday night and wakes on Thursday morning fails to catch Wednesday! When the old folk describe the weekdays in all these ways, I am the one who turns those very<!-- source join: scan 5 ends `அவைகளே`; scan 6 begins `கேலிக்குரியதாக` --> days into objects of ridicule. Why, then, has this Friday—the Friday that is to arrive tomorrow morning—captivated me like this!

<!-- source: scan 6; printed page: 5 -->

“The day Mahatma Gandhi, that noble man of the world, was killed was also a Friday. A Friday bathed in the tears of the world’s people, tossed about by their sighs, and gone forever! Only now do I realize that Friday has the power not merely to become the lap that bore Gandhi’s corpse, but also to become the auspicious turmeric that will plunge me into joy. Do I realize it... or am I babbling something? Shame! Shame! It feels as though I have spoken aloud—what if someone hears me! If they do, by morning they will beat the drum all over the village and make us hang our head before they attend to anything else!”

Chintamani kept tossing on her bed. Waves of thought rolled and rolled within her.

She raised her head and looked all around. The fear that someone might be awake made her do it.

She turned up the lamp beside her and looked at the clock hanging on the wall. Five o’clock! At that very moment five great strokes sounded from the clock tower at the end of her street, telling her that dawn would soon break. The rooster that habitually crowed by the clock-tower time began its “cock-a-doodle-doo” song and sang a wake-up hymn to the sun! Only then did Chintamani’s astonishment overflow: she had spent the entire night without sleeping!

<!-- source: scan 7; printed page: 6 -->

She had heard the rooster’s cry on countless days. Not once on all those days had she thought about it! A rooster is male—a hen is female. Why was she thinking of all this now and smiling to herself? The rooster was crowing; was she wondering whether the stage at which a rooster would crow had arrived in her own life too? Was she growing shy at those sweet thoughts? The clock tower and the great temple bell were all carrying out their duties at dawn as they always did. Unless the clock-tower mechanism broke down, unless the temple bell-ringer overslept, those sounds of daybreak never ceased. But in Chintamani’s heart alone there rang a sound of the yaazh never heard before! This was no ordinary clock sound or temple-bell sound. It was a sound greater than either!

At dawn the conch-blower went down the street blowing his conch. Night, curled up and cramped on its bed of silence, seemed to shake off its weariness, stretch lazily and sit up—perhaps that mute sound was finding voice through the conch-blower’s shell. *Thel! Thel!! Thel!!!* The conch-call seemed to invite Chintamani away into some new world. “Blow the conch that is to be blown, and let the dawn that is to dawn, dawn!” says a proverb. She knew that day did not break because a rooster crowed, a conch was blown or a temple bell rang. Yet on that day alone she imagined that dawn was going to come because of them, and she kept blessing the rooster, the conch-blower, the temple bell-ringer and the clock-tower clock as though they were the causes of daybreak.

<!-- source: scan 8; printed page: 7 -->

She imagined that all of them together woke the sun sleeping at the foot of a mountain—or perhaps beneath the sea. She feared that if the sun, exhausted, overslept, Friday itself would be late. After the conch-blower had gone came the cry of the kuduguduppai man, parading with his hereditary refrain: “Good times are being born—good times are being born!” Did that sound like mere shouting to her?

They say kuduguduppai men sleep in the cremation ground at night. Their chosen deities, they say, appear in their dreams and hand them a list of whose houses in the village are about to see good times and where someone is going to go crying “Aiyyo!” The kuduguduppai man must announce that list through the village before dawn. That is why he comes once at daybreak and says all sorts of things as though his chosen deity had told him. Later, after dawn, he comes once again seeking old clothes and freshens up the refrain he sang in the night, polishing it a little. Chintamani knew very well that all of this was false talk devised for his livelihood. Until now her house had never given the kuduguduppai man even one torn old cloth. But now she felt as though she ought to give him a new cloth at once! He was saying, “Good times are being born!” What a mad thought!

“Kudu kudu kudu!” The kuduguduppai in his hand, like a rattle that delights children, seemed to have gained a new power—and it had made her childlike too. Chintamani rose from bed and looked out at the street through the window. The eastern sky had begun<!-- source join: scan 8 ends `கிழக்கு வானம் வெளுக்கத்`; scan 9 begins `துவங்கிவிட்டது.` --> to grow light. Was that a new announcement about Chintamani’s future? There—the farmers had already set out toward the fields, driving their cattle and carrying their ploughs!

<!-- source: scan 9; printed page: — -->

Once they reached the fields, they would yoke the cattle to the ploughs! People compared a family to the plough and husband and wife to those cattle. Whenever she heard that, Chintamani would laugh aloud and mock them: “But both the cattle yoked to the plough are bulls! Does that mean two men live together in a family?” Now, before her very eyes, stood the bulls, the ploughs and the farmers carrying them. All her old teasing and mockery had gone off somewhere and hidden themselves. She looked almost ready to tell her own heart the truth: the elders used that example only to stress that married life needed the love and cooperation of two people.

In the sky-garden where the night had bloomed and swayed with mullai flowers, now a single red cotton blossom alone was visible! And that too in the east!

All night she had been confused by thoughts, troubled by sleeplessness, finding a kind of pleasure even in that trouble, and setting out with the joy of taking her first step on a new path. Yet the young ray she saw in the east also looked to her like a red warning light announcing danger! She started. Then she composed herself.

<!-- source: scan 10; printed page: 9 -->

In a little while her friends would come to call her for a bath! They came every day. Vedapuram was neither quite village nor quite town, and until seven in the morning its large pond belonged only to the women of that area. Young women in the bloom of youth left home at daybreak like pigeons flying from their cote and finished their duties before men began moving about, returning home again. Chintamani belonged to one part of that flock. Was she not a young vine in bloom too? And among that whole group, Chintamani was the one who rose from bed a little later in the morning!

Her friends would come, pester her awake and take her along to bathe.

One of them had memorized the *Tiruppavai*—and her name too was Andal! Every day she would sing a *Tiruppavai* verse to wake Chintamani:

> “In the pond in the garden behind your house,
> the red lilies have opened their mouths and the ambal blooms have folded theirs;
> the white-toothed ascetics in brick-red robes
> have set out for their holy temple to sound the conch.
> O girl who boasted that you would wake us before we woke you!
> Rise! Shameless girl!”

She would sing it tunefully. Another would shout, “Hey! Young parrot, are you still asleep?” Friends who raised such a racket were about to arrive now. She was already awake to meet them—<!-- source join: scan 10 ends `இருந்தாள்—`; scan 11 begins the quoted question --> they would ask, “After all these days, how did you alone wake up so early today?” Afraid of that question, Chintamani lay down pretending to sleep.

<!-- source: scan 11; printed page: 10 -->

As usual, her friends arrived. Chintamani squeezed her eyes shut and pretended to be sound asleep. Andal began singing the *Tiruppavai*:

> “In the pure jewelled mansion, with lamps burning all around,
> with incense spreading fragrance, you sleep upon your couch—
> O uncle’s daughter! Open the bolt of the jewelled door!”

As she sang, Andal used her fingers to open the eyelid-doors that covered Chintamani’s beautiful dark eyes. Chintamani too rubbed her eyes and sat up as though she had been sleeping deeply.

“Hmm! Now we sing the *Tiruppavai* to wake you. After a little while, his friends will sing the *Tiruppavai* to wake him!”

“Whom, girl? Whom?” Chintamani asked, lowering her head in embarrassment.

“Him, of course! And do you know how they will wake him? Hey, Andal! Sing that *Tiruppavai* and show her!” said Radha.

Andal began another *Tiruppavai* verse:

> “With the standing lamps burning, upon the ivory-legged cot,
> atop the soft fivefold bed,
> O flower-chested one lying against the breasts of Nappinnai,
> whose tresses are decked with clustered flowers—open your mouth!”

<!-- source: scan 12; printed page: 11 -->

Before Andal had even finished singing, Chintamani could no longer bear her anger and embarrassment.

“Andal! What kind of indecent song is this? Stop! Stop!” Chintamani ordered.

“This is not a song by *this* Andal, dear! It is a devotional song that Mother Andal, the sacred daughter of Periyalvar, sang to Perumal!” Andal said with a laugh. The whole group burst into laughter and set out toward the bathing pond.

With a copper pot resting on her slender waist, Chintamani walked like the moon among the stars. She became the lotus of that lotusless pond. Even the green sari she wore did not have the fortune of enjoying the full beauty of her body, which could make one wonder whether it was ivory or gold; but that crystal-clear pond enjoyed that great fortune every dawn. Scooping up and splashing the pond water, singing and dancing, carried by a flood of feeling that rose without her even realizing it, Chintamani bathed, swam and played.

The Friday she had been waiting for had arrived, had it not? She had to return home quickly; they might arrive at any moment, and before that she had to finish her adornments. That hurry too made her lose her composure. What, then, was the event that could give her such unprecedented joy?
