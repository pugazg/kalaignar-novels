## 16

<!-- source: scan 127; printed page 126; post-centered 16 -->

By then the news had spread throughout the train. "Some woman was caught by the police while carrying opium," people said, and the story reached Azhagappan's ears. Tiger, in the last carriage, heard it too, but did not know that Chintamani was the one who had been caught.

The train reached Bangalore. People poured out of the carriages as though a cage had been opened. Surrounded by police, Chintamani too stepped down. The ostentatious woman who had escaped by placing the blame upon her left the train with an expression of perfect innocence arranged upon her face. Tiger hurried forward looking for Chintamani.

A crowd gathered around the police and their prisoner to stare. Seeing the policemen's heads, Tiger entered the crowd uneasily.

"There she is! That's the woman the police caught!" people said, pointing as they moved around.

Tiger stretched his head through the crowd to see the arrested woman. For a moment he lost his balance. What was this sight before him? Had she escaped from him only to be trapped by the police? The question took possession of him.

How could he know the suffering she had endured? Friday dawned—and she was branded a prostitute. The hour of ruin came. Now she had fallen into the hands of the police. Must still more cruelty grow in a woman's life? Were these not enough to keep her alive and kill her atom by atom?

<!-- source join: scan 127 ends `வாழ்`; scan 128 begins `விலே` -->
<!-- source: scan 128; printed page 127 -->

"She has escaped from me!" Tiger sighed.

If a parrot escapes the cat's mouth only to be locked inside a cage, would not the cat feel longing? Chintamani had become a parrot troubled on both sides.

Was there no way to free her? Tiger thought. He himself was a man who had deceived the police and escaped. If he now went voluntarily into their reach and got caught, what greater foolishness could there be? Confused, he decided to let events take their course and watch from a distance.

Azhagappan and his mother came down from the first-class carriage and tried to move toward the side where the crowd stood.

"That is the woman who was caught carrying opium!" people announced, pointing her out. Azhagappan too became curious to see her. Slowly guiding his mother, he stretched and craned through the crowd but could not get a clear look. The police moved ahead with Chintamani; Azhagappan and his mother followed behind.

Tiger saw Azhagappan going after them. Fear seized him that Azhagappan and Chintamani might meet. It surrounded him even more strongly than his fear of the police who were searching for him. He followed behind, watching Azhagappan closely.

A police van stood in front of the railway station. The police took Chintamani to it. As she climbed in, anyone could have seen her face. Azhagappan was now certain to see her. She would immediately tell him her story and cry. He would believe her. Then all Tiger's plans would become water poured upon useless grass.

Thinking this, Tiger trembled. As Chintamani climbed the steps into the van, Azhagappan hurried closer, wishing to see the prisoner. She did not know he was coming, and he did not know the woman was Chintamani.

Had she turned her face toward the crowd while entering the vehicle, Azhagu would have recognized her at once. But why would she turn toward them? Was she a "social worker" going to prison after struggling for public demands and suffering repression? No. In the eyes of society she had become its "filth"—a human worm described as having become pregnant before marriage and living by smuggling opium. Why would she turn to look at the public?

She did not. Azhagappan did not recognize her. He saw only the back of the poor girl's head.

<!-- source join: scan 128 ends `ஏறும்போது`; scan 129 begins `அவளை` -->
<!-- source: scan 129; printed page 128 -->

"She's quite young, Amma," he said.

"Looks like a clever operator!" his mother replied.

The jewel that should have become her daughter-in-law had been swept away and ruined in a river of misfortune.

The police van departed carrying that virtuous woman who had been thrown into dust.

Azhagappan and the old mother hired a car and left for home. Tiger wanted to learn Azhagappan's address, so he followed in another hired car. Once he had discovered exactly where Azhagappan lived, he turned his car back.

<!-- source join: scan 129 ends `அழகப்ப`; scan 130 begins `னுடைய` -->
<!-- source: scan 130; printed page 129 -->

Chintamani was taken from the policemen and shut inside the lock-up. In that dark room filled with bugs and white worms lay the diamond moon that had set out searching for the light of life.

That "mangakara" Friday closed its shop and made way for Saturday.

* * *

Golden rays of the rising sun stretched through the window. In the Palaiyur hospital Anandi woke from sleep. Naina Muhammad came near and asked, "Shall I make you Horlicks?" She nodded that she wanted it.

The words spoken during the night had submerged both of them in shyness. As a continuation of that conversation, many kinds of change shone in their glances. Through the whole day the two hearts remained silent, while thoughts alone spoke loudly.

It is a grammar of love that a better conclusion can sometimes be reached through silence and the movement of the eyes than through words. Even those whose hearts agree to love may hesitate, through shyness or fear, to give the thought verbal form. If a man suddenly goes up to a woman and says, "I love you," even if she desires him she is more likely to say, "Stand back," than to come close saying, "Embrace me, beloved!"

That is why the eyes are praised as the first entrance to the mansion of love. If there were no eyes, lovers would have to climb over the wall called the heart and jump into that mansion.

Long live those eyes! With such a blessing upon them, the day passed. Night came. After attending to Anandi's needs, Naina Muhammad sat nearby.

<!-- source join: scan 130 ends `காதலர்கள்`; scan 131 begins `இருதயமென்னும்...` -->
<!-- source: scan 131; printed page 130 -->

"Aren't you going to sleep?" Anandi finally asked.

"There is no hurry," he replied.

Silence for a while. Anandi suffered inwardly at the thought, *I have disturbed his mind for nothing and am now watching the result.*

For one moment she thought: *If I had not told him the truth about Tiger—if I had simply said that I was a woman whose chastity had never been lost—I could have received Naina's complete love.*

"Tch! If the two of us became one like that and the truth came out one day, how would his heart shatter?" She praised herself for having told the truth.

"Please hate me," she said, tears filling her eyes.

"I cannot, Anandi. I have reached a firm decision. From now on, I will be the companion of your life."

"Then...?"

"Either you or I must change religion."

"Why must religion change? It is enough if the heart does not change," Anandi said, gripping his hands tightly as tears poured from her eyes.

Those tears were not foul water from a wounded heart. They were rosewater sprinkled at a festival of ecstasy. Their hands intertwined—the sign that their hearts too had joined and become one.

* * *

More than a week had passed. Azhagappan was longing for some reply from Naina about Anandi's health.

He believed that Anandi alone had the power to erase the humiliating disappointment caused by Chintamani, and that thought gave him pleasure. His mother did not like highly educated women; if the woman was a working professional she would object even more. Yet Azhagappan believed he could persuade his mother in Anandi's case. That belief was the offspring of desire.

From the time he returned from Vedapuram, he had wandered about repeating Anandi's name.

"Will no letter come from Naina at least today?" he wondered as he sat waiting.

Tiger appeared before him. Azhagappan started at the sight. Tiger stood there looking overwhelmed with grief. The moment he saw Azhagappan he cried aloud, "Anandi!"

"What about Anandi? What happened?" Azhagu asked, rising with a tremble.

"Anandi died the day before yesterday!" Tiger said, and began crying continuously.

Azhagappan could not remain standing. The castles of his mind collapsed into dust. His dreams of desire withered in the bud. He felt as though he had been thrown at the mouth of a dark cave.

<!-- source: scan 133; printed page 132 -->

"My condition is like that of a blind man without even a walking stick, abandoned in a forest full of rocks and thorns," he thought, shedding tears.

A long silence followed. The old mother came in, staggering. Hearing the news, she too collapsed in grief.

"What happened to Naina Muhammad?" Azhagu asked, suppressing his tears.

"Aiyyo! Why ask? Three days ago your friend bundled up the jewels from the house and made a clean escape somewhere..." Tiger said, crying still more.

Azhagappan could not truly believe those words. Yet Tiger was standing there weeping as he said them. What else was he to believe?

Could his friend be such a great scoundrel? Azhagappan fell into confusion. Tiger quickly drew him to his side. The old mother too joined Tiger's side and began pouring curses upon Naina.

In the end Azhagappan gave Tiger a bundle of thousand-rupee notes and tried to console him.

"A thousand rupees—can it equal Anandi's life?" Tiger began another lament.

Azhagappan and his mother comforted him and sent him away.

Tiger left the bungalow blowing his nose. Within fifteen minutes of his departure, a postman handed Azhagappan an envelope. The address appeared to be in Naina Muhammad's handwriting.
