## 18

<!-- source: scan 142; printed page 141; post-centered 18 -->

> "I believed and believed in day, star, temple, victory, auspiciousness and all such things, and in the end I was ruined. The day I trusted, the star, the temple, the deity—none of them allowed me or my family to live with honour. How did my daughter become a prostitute? How did she become a clever smuggler of illegal opium? I found no answer. God too became mute.
>
> "If I remain alive I will keep asking these questions and tormenting my mind. To release both my heart and God from them, I am becoming a corpse. I am going to find peace.
>
> "Sivakami will lose her turmeric and kumkum. I will be accused of forcibly cutting away the mangalyam I tied around her neck. Let it be. There is no other way to quiet the boiling within me.
>
> "Do not think some madman has scribbled this! My madness has cleared. Only after that did I choose this good decision.
>
> "If Chintamani is ever truly proved unstained, let her scatter flowers over my grave with her beautiful hands. I know very well that this is only an empty dream.
>
> "As though *she* could ever be proved spotless! Sinner! Wretch! Ruin of my household! Do not even tell her the news of my death!... If it becomes certain that she is a good woman, then perhaps tell her!... Tch! What confusion even now, when I am about to find peace!"

These words were written in the soaked letter found at the waist of Sivanesar's body floating in the water. The letter was useful when the village elders gathered and concluded that it was suicide.

The villagers came together, buried Sivanesar's now peaceful body and returned. Even they were astonished. How had such a terrible change come so suddenly upon a family that had been living well? Even a storm shows signs before it strikes. A flood can be detected before it arrives. But the events that destroyed Sivanesar's family had given no warning at all.

<!-- source join: scan 142 ends `வாசகங்கள்`; scan 143 begins `இருந்தன.` -->
<!-- source: scan 143; printed page 142 -->

People throughout the village began speaking in many ways about Sivanesar's family.

Confusion began rising in Azhagappan too. How had Chintamani, who supposedly had just given birth, gone to Bangalore? How could she have smuggled opium? How could her physical condition have allowed any of it? The questions rose one after another and filled his heart.

At the same time, the way he himself had behaved toward Anandi—and the way she had received it—filled him with fear and embarrassment.

Naina Muhammad, who had intended to explain Chintamani to him, did not raise the matter at all. He kept sighing whenever he remembered the scene he had witnessed that morning. Had he known from the beginning that his friend Azhagappan held such thoughts, he would have kept far away from Anandi from the start.

His greatest mistake was that he had never told Azhagappan earlier about his own affection and love for Anandi. Had he said it, perhaps Azhagappan would never have begun thinking of her. Naina struggled with the question of how this problem could be brought to an end.

<!-- source: scan 144; printed page 143 -->

Anandi suffered because Naina Muhammad had now formed a wrong idea of her, and she had no opportunity to meet him alone and explain.

Though the two friends were tormented by a thousand thoughts, they remained together, talking and sleeping without separating. Anandi therefore had no chance to speak privately with Naina.

On any other day Azhagappan might have asked Naina for answers to the questions surrounding Chintamani. Now he felt embarrassed even to speak to his friend about a woman. He knew very well that Naina had seen him embracing Anandi and had seen her run away. Naina might therefore think that a love relationship had formed between him and Anandi.

*Why not strengthen that very impression ourselves?* Azhagappan thought.

In the end he told Naina an invented story, describing matters as though a love relationship really had developed between him and Anandi.

Naina could not remain calm after hearing it, but he became deeply distressed. His grief did not arise because Anandi had been lost to him. It arose because Azhagappan too had fallen into the net of the captivating Anandi.

How could he explain this to his friend? If he did, Azhagappan might form a wrong opinion of *him* instead. It would not be surprising if Azhagappan decided that Naina was inventing a conspiracy merely to break his love.

So Naina summoned patience and resolved to watch how the changes unfolded—not with the happy heart of a poet watching clouds become sheep, cattle, deer, peacocks, monkeys and men, but with the fearful eagerness of a person descending by parachute from a crashed aeroplane and wondering every second: *Will I land in a forest? In the sea? Beside a bear?*

* * *

In prison, news reached Chintamani that a wealthy man had filed an appeal seeking her release from rigorous imprisonment.

Who could that wealthy man be? A doubt arose in her. Could it be Azhagappan? The thought gave her happiness, but it also produced fear: after everything that had happened, how could she face him?

Then she heard that along with the appeal order permission had been granted for her release on bail.

"Some Thevar, they say. A very rich man. He has hired a lawyer, argued the case, paid five or six hundred rupees in cash bail and is taking Chintamani out," the jail warders said as news drifted through the prison.

To a woman shut up like a caged parrot, the reports were strange, but they did not fill her with ecstasy.

"Which Thevar? Could it be Balagangadhara Thevar? I have heard of him but never seen him. Why should he have such concern for our family? Perhaps he is a noble man whose nature is to help others!"

<!-- source join: scan 145 ends `ஏது`; scan 146 begins `அவ்வளவு அக்கறை!` -->
<!-- source: scan 146; printed page 145 -->

While she was longing and thinking like this, the order releasing her on bail arrived and removed the uncertainty.

Chintamani left the prison fortress in bewilderment. At the gate stood a man in a black coat, short in body but with a large head. Beside him stood another broad, impressive man with a young beard and turban, dressed in a silk shirt and trousers. He welcomed Chintamani.

"You are...?" she asked, with shyness and a flood of pain in her voice.

"I am Balagangadhara Thevar! From a village near yours. And this is our lawyer—Bangalore Periyasami, a very great advocate!" Thevar said, introducing the lawyer.

The lawyer greeted Chintamani. With tears overflowing, she returned the greeting.

The three got into the lawyer's car and went to his house. Seeing Chintamani's suffering, the lawyer's heart melted.

"They have unjustly placed such an accusation upon such a good woman," he lamented.

Chintamani thanked Balagangadhara Thevar deeply.

"It is astonishing that you have taken so much trouble for me," she said.

The lawyer immediately replied, "What can be done, Amma? Your father too killed himself..."

<!-- source join: scan 146 ends `கூறியதுதான்`; scan 147 begins `தாமதம்;` -->
<!-- source: scan 147; printed page 146 -->

That was enough. The world seemed to spin around Chintamani.

Thevar's face changed. Turning sharply to the lawyer, he said, "What is this, sir? I told you not to say it, and you have told her!"

The lawyer blinked nervously. "Forgive me, Mr. Thevar! The truth slipped from my mouth. It is all right; one day she had to learn it anyway," he said, trying to cover the mistake.

Chintamani clutched Thevar's feet.

"Send me to Vedapuram immediately, somehow. I must at least see my mother at once!" she cried.

Thevar tried many ways to console her. "Let us first win the case and obtain your freedom. Then you can see your mother; by then she too will have some comfort," he said. But would Chintamani listen?

The lawyer argued on her side. "She does not have to come to court until next week. Let her go and return before then."

He won that argument too.

Chintamani wanted Thevar to accompany her. He explained that he had a very important matter in Bangalore, would finish it and leave the following night, and could meet her in Vedapuram the day after tomorrow. Then both could return to Bangalore and attend to the case.

He arranged for Chintamani to leave for Vedapuram on the daytime train. She promised to wait for him there and return with him to Bangalore when he came.

Then she set out for Vedapuram—to meet the mother she still believed remained to her.

At about one in the morning she got down at Palaiyur and went toward Vedapuram, partly running and partly walking. The dark streets of Palaiyur reminded her of Tiger chasing her and stirred frightening sensations.

"I have become a sinner who cannot see my father's face even once more. How will I look at my mother's face after she has lost her turmeric?" she lamented, running toward Vedapuram like a moon upon the earth.

<!-- source join: scan 147 ends `சந்திப்`; scan 148 begins `பதற்காக!` -->
<!-- source: scan 148; printed page 147 -->

She reached Vedapuram. To her, Vedapuram looked only like a city of pain.

If dawn came, she could not show her face to anyone. She was the rejected woman, the woman without morality, the small woman, the woman who had gone to prison—such had been the ritual of abuse performed over her.

The village lay quiet, submerged in darkness.

Chintamani ran toward her house. Her father was not there. She could neither bear the thought nor carry its weight. How would her mother run to embrace her and cry? Aiyyo, cruelty! Cruelty!

Trembling as though a thousand red scorpions had stung her, Chintamani reached the door.

Without realizing it she cried, "Amma!" and collided with the door. "Father!" she screamed.

The door had not been bolted and swung open.

"Amma! Amma!!" she shouted.

There was no mother.

Something above looked as though loosened hair had been kicked aside. Chintamani lifted her eyes. Two legs were hanging down.

She screamed.

Sivakami was swinging peacefully, without raising her eyes to look at Chintamani.
