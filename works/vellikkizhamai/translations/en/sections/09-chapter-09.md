## 9

<!-- source: scan 75; printed page 74; post-centered 9 -->

That laughter did not rise from a heart filled with peace; it was like great waves rising from a stormy sea.

“Chintamani! Chintamani!!” he called—and laughed aloud.

“My daughter! My daughter!” he wailed.

“You shameless woman!” he snarled.

“Woman who has lost the path! Faithless fool! Neeli! Kali!” he raved, pouring out one name after another.

What more reason was needed to conclude that Sivanesar had gone mad? Though Sivakami fell before him and tried to stop him, he pushed her aside and began running into the street.

The villagers gathered. Sivanesar began attacking them all with crazed words. They forcibly dragged him away, took him home and locked him in. Sivakami’s mind went numb. She became motionless. *What is left to happen now? Everything has already happened,* was the cry rising within her.

Inside the locked room Sivanesar shouted, beat on the door and created a commotion. All through the village people began talking about Chintamani in different ways. Her friends tried to argue in her defence.

“She looked like such a perfect innocent. Has *she* been living the life of a courtesan?” many wondered.

“It is precisely the innocent-looking ones you must never trust these days! Chintamani is the perfect example!” many others decided.

“Poor Sivanesar is rather particular about honour! Something must have happened without his wife Sivakami knowing. Perhaps she herself was the reason Chintamani went bad!” some hasty people piled on this baseless accusation.

“Where could she have run, carrying the child she gave birth to? What a capable one she is! A body just after childbirth! A raw postpartum body! And she has run away in that state—the shameless creature!” The women’s congress sank into astonishment, fingers to noses and hands to chins.

“Say what you like—our Chintamani is spotless gold! If anyone speaks even an atom of suspicion about her, may their tongue rot!” Andal cursed them.

“Is a tongue some citron fruit, to rot away? Get lost, you rogue girl, and mind your own work!” an elder woman of the village silenced her.

<!-- source: scan 77; printed page 76 -->

“The truth must have come to light. That is why the groom’s people went back broken-hearted,” declared some people who liked firm conclusions, clearing away even their own half-formed doubts.

Somehow a stain had been invented for Chintamani, and it spread through the whole village. Sivanesar’s own relatives began looking at his house with open contempt. When hardship strikes a man, are not relatives often the first to rejoice—and then, while pretending to share his distress, create still more trouble?

Why should the people of Vedapuram be an exception? “Sivanesar has been properly trapped! An alliance involving women indeed! An alliance with a grand household! Everything has been smashed to dust!” His relatives, little creatures in pettiness, swelled with private satisfaction.

Chintamani’s friends stood distressed. They suffered at the humiliation that had fallen upon their beloved friend, but even they could not solve the riddle. The village’s endless talk began to create divisions among them.

“Could there perhaps be some truth even we do not know?” doubt began to sprout in some of the girls.

If a magician, in a place where there is no smell at all, says a hundred times with conviction, “There is the smell of lemon here,” even we may begin to feel that we smell it. They say that if a lie is asserted ten times it can take on the appearance of truth. Is that not how, by such saying, the very word “God” has been printed in large letters in the dictionary of life?

If falsehoods that deceive the people of the world can take shape as truths, is it any great task for stories about Chintamani to spread like fire until people decide that they simply *must* be true? In a world surrounded by jealous people who speak evil even of the pure and the famous, would the “buffaloes of spite” leave red lotuses like Chintamani untrampled?

One part of the women’s circle kept showering arrows of mockery on Chintamani. Some among them did not know the meaning of the three-letter Tamil word *karpu*—chastity. Without turning to look at the faults piled up within themselves, they created faults that did not exist in others and laughed over them. Is that not a favourite game on this strange earth? Chintamani, a pitiable young woman, became the sacrifice in that painful game.

After locking Sivanesar inside the house and consoling Sivakami, the villagers and neighbours went away to attend to their own duties. Sivakami sat without knowing what to do. There was no way to ask her husband for advice; he lay there shouting like Kali. She did not know which way Chintamani had gone. No one in the street offered any clear suggestion either. “Let it be; we will see tomorrow,” they said, shedding responsibility as they left.

The whole village was speaking suspiciously about Chintamani. Even the friends who had always been with her had sunk into confusion. Sivakami had been left alone. What could she decide by herself?

Only a mother had no doubt about her daughter. Sivakami alone knew without confusion that her child was a pure flame. But now she could not even see her daughter and learn what had happened. Where could she have gone? Perhaps, after hearing the news and unable to bear the pain, she had run somewhere and... Aiyyo! Sivakami could not even complete the thought.

The clock struck nine. In darkened Vedapuram, Sivakami floundered as though blindfolded. She heard someone approaching the entrance.

“Chintamani!” she screamed and ran.

Hearing her cry, Sivanesar shouted from inside the room, “Chintamani? That ruined woman?” and began raving again.

Sivakami ran into the street and stopped in confusion. Tiger stood before her.

“What is this—such an uproar in the house! What is the matter, Aunt? Where is Uncle?” Tiger asked.

“Uncle! Here he is! Here stands the uncle stripped of honour! Open the door! Open the door! Otherwise I will destroy this whole village! I am the Lord with flowing matted hair who burned Tripura! Holding the deer, holding the battle-axe, bearing the Ganga on my head and Parvati at my side! I am Paramasiva! Hey, rakshasa! Will you open the door at once? Shall I send my bull vehicle to gore you? Hey, Parvati! Hey, Sivakami! Open the door! Otherwise I will sing a Tevaram hymn and open it as the door at Tirumaraikkadu was opened!”

Words poured from Sivanesar as though a sea sluice had burst open. Realizing that his madness was worsening, Sivakami began striking her own head and crying.

“What is this, Aunt! Haven’t you any sense at all? What has happened now? Why are you carrying on like this?” Tiger asked.

<!-- source: scan 80; printed page 79 -->

“What more must happen, son? Once the family’s honour is gone, what remains? Only my life! And even that wretched God refuses to take away!”

Sivakami shed garlands of tears.

“Don’t worry, Sivakami! Here, I am calling our Yama. Come to Devaloka with him! Run! Run!” Sivanesar shouted.

“What greater cruelty than this must come to me, son—and at this age?”

Sivakami’s lament began again.

“Do not be afraid, Aunt! I take responsibility for Uncle getting well. Finding Chintamani too is my responsibility!”

“Tiger, no one can change our fate. Go and mind your own affairs,” Sivakami said through tears and anger.

“That is right! That is right! No one can change fate! Chintamani! Could I change your fate? Fate decreed that you must become a ruined woman! Fate decreed that you must become pregnant before marriage! Fate! Fate! Fate!... My fate! My fate! Kama Satyabhama! Open the door! Open it!!”

Sivanesar’s shouting grew louder. Tiger began speaking to Sivakami with great humility and softness.

“Aunt! The account you and Uncle have kept of me is wrong. I am good to those who are good, and bad to those who are bad. Even if you disregard me, I will not simply watch the suffering that has come upon you. I swear: bringing Chintamani back and placing her in your hands is my responsibility! My duty! Whether you ask me or not, that is my work!”

“Chintamani returning and Chintamani dying wherever she is are one and the same! The village has already passed judgment on her! It has declared her a symbol of disgrace! Rather than have the apple of my eye return and spend her whole life in tears and misery, an unmarried woman no one will take, it would be better if she died!”

Sivakami leaned her head against the wall and shed tears as she spoke.

“A stain on Chintamani! That is what you fear, Aunt, isn’t it? You are worried that no one will marry her now, aren’t you? Even if a thousand more slanders and ten thousand more accusations are heaped upon her, I am ready to marry her. What do you say?” Tiger asked, acting like a good man, standing humbly like a noble soul.

Sivakami stared at him. Anger appeared in her eyes. Her lips trembled.

“Hey! Ramakrishnu! All of this is your plot, isn’t it? You arranged everything so you could marry Chintamani, didn’t you?” Sivakami hissed, overflowing with anger.

“What is this, Aunt? Have you caught something like Uncle too? Tell me the truth—do you believe Chintamani is unstained or not?” Tiger asked.

<!-- source: scan 82; printed page 81; bottom standalone `6` excluded as printer/signature mark -->

“My daughter is Kannagi Devi, boy—Kannagi Devi!” Sivakami cried.

“Then why do you refuse to have that Kannagi Devi return alive and call you Mother?”

“Who refused? If my gem would show me her face once, that alone would be enough!”

The feeling of motherhood burst through its dam inside Sivakami. She herself had first said, “Rather than have Chintamani return after becoming the target of village slander, it would be better if she died.” Now the same woman said even one sight of her daughter’s face would be enough.

The first was human feeling bound by a sense of honour. What now surged up was the still greater feeling of motherhood. Tiger Ramakrishnan planned to turn that maternal feeling to his advantage.

“Chintamani has not gone far at all. I can bring her here. But on one condition—you must promise to give her to me in marriage!”

“No marriage! There will be no marriage for Chintamani! She is a Manimekalai! An ascetic! A renunciant! A Buddhist bhikkhuni! There is no marriage for her! That is my decision! She is a brahmacharini! No, no—a woman who bore a child without losing her maidenhood! She is the real Kunti Devi! Hey, Pandu Maharaja! I cannot give her in marriage! Run away! Run away!!” Sivanesar thundered in a fit of madness that was reaching its height.

“What do you say, Aunt? Chintamani must come back, mustn’t she?”

“Yes! Certainly!”

“Then the marriage?”

“Marriage?” Sivakami sighed deeply.

“Aunt! Give me a final answer. If you give your word, the marriage will take place publicly and in a proper manner. Otherwise, you will merely hear the news that we have married!”

“What is this, son—are you threatening me? Have you hidden Chintamani somewhere? Do you think this is the age of jungle kings? Have you forgotten there is a police department?”

“There may be a police department. But if the police come, they will bring only your daughter’s corpse! Remember that!”

Sivakami started in shock.

“What exactly are you saying now?” she asked in a pleading tone.

“What am I saying? I will bring Chintamani back. I am asking you to agree to give her to me in marriage.”

“All right, let it be so! Bring her back!” Sivakami said.

“Saying it like that is not enough, Aunt. You must swear before your family deity.”

“Swear?”

“What else? Do you think you can fool this fellow afterward? That won’t work. You must swear before the family deity and give your consent.”

<!-- source: scan 84; printed page 83 -->

Sivakami thought. War and storm rose in a mother’s heart.

Could she bring herself to lose Chintamani? At the same time, could she agree to make Tiger Ramakrishnan her daughter’s husband? All Sivakami wanted now was to meet Chintamani once and learn the truth about the accusation laid upon her. She burned to remove somehow the darkness of slander surrounding her beloved daughter.

She believed that if Chintamani returned, she could erase that accusation from the villagers’ minds. A suspicion also arose in her that Tiger himself might be responsible for everything that had happened. If she promised to give Chintamani to Tiger, perhaps he would admit to the villagers that he himself had placed a false accusation upon her and would turn her daughter, in their eyes, into a pure woman again. The stain upon her would disappear; the baseless scandal would die.

She thought she could somehow endure Chintamani living with Tiger—but how could she endure the name that her daughter was a stained woman? If that name was to disappear, she concluded, the right course was to give Chintamani in marriage to Tiger. A mother’s heart could reach just such a decision.

Sivakami prepared to swear before the family deity. Sivanesar continued pounding on the door and shouting.

She had come to believe completely that the chaos in the house and the villagers’ laughter and ridicule would all be set right once Chintamani returned. She also thought that if Tiger became the son-in-law of the house, fear would shut the village’s mouth.

For Sivakami it was enough if her daughter could be proved “unstained.”

So she placed the whole burden of blame and sin upon the deity and submitted to Tiger’s condition.

Tiger lit a kuthuvilakku before the family deity and placed a dot of kumkum.

“Now, Aunt! You may give your word,” he said.

Standing before the family deity, Sivakami declared, “By our family deity! I swear that I will give Chintamani to you in marriage!”

Hearing it, Tiger exclaimed joyfully, “Bravo, Aunt! Bravo! Chintamani will come now! All your suffering will disappear!” and left the house.
