## 22

<!-- source: scan 166; printed page 165; post-centered 22 -->

The heart that had gone to the very boundary of fury slowly began to receive the shadow of calm. Yet the stillness did not fail to conceal within it the graveyard quality of grief.

"When dawn comes?" The question quickened Chintamani's heartbeat.

*Mother's suicide will spread through the whole village. The panchayat, the inquiry into the body—people of Vedapuram will gather for all of it. I too must stand before them, carrying the title of prostitute, hearing the village say that I was caught smuggling opium and came out on bail. Amma! I cannot bear it. I must not fall within the eyes of Vedapuram at all!*

She began thinking that she too could end her life before her mother and hang as another corpse nearby.

Even then the villagers appeared before her mind's eye. If she too killed herself, what would Vedapuram say the next day? In the morning the village would see three corpses—Anandi, Sivakami and Chintamani. What judgments would be spoken about them?

"Aiyyo! That noble Anandi was so helpful to the village. The sinners have killed that innocent woman!" people would say with sympathy.

"Poor Sivakami! She built so many castles in her mind, and before they were finished she went into a castle of earth," they would say with pity.

If Chintamani's own body lay beside her mother's, perhaps the villagers might not even turn to look at either corpse.

<!-- source continuation: scan 166 ends `என்று பரிதாபம் காட்டுவர்—`; scan 167 begins `அம்மாவின்...` -->
<!-- source: scan 167; printed page 166 -->

"Wretch! After doing everything you did, you finally went and died?" they would mock her and spit.

No—no. Do not die. The decision grew strong within her.

She must free herself from both accusations laid upon her. It was not enough merely to prove that she had not smuggled opium; the true culprit must also be caught. Likewise Azhagappan must learn that she was unstained and come forward to marry her.

Once those two things happened and she had proved that she was pure, she could die even the next day. Until then she must not destroy her life.

Where should she go now? She could not remain in the village once daylight came. How could she walk the streets of Vedapuram as a symbol of disgrace?

Then she remembered Balagangadhara Thevar. His Ayyanar farm was five miles away. Why not go there?

But had he not said he would return from Bangalore only the next day? Still, she thought, if she reached the Ayyanar farm she could remain there until he came. Then she could go with him to Bangalore, settle the matter of the case, tell him about Azhagappan too, and have Thevar convince him that she was unstained.

If she said Thevar himself had asked her to stay at the farm, would they refuse her a place there? Asking and answering such questions herself, she decided to reach Ayyanar farm as quickly as possible.

Before leaving she wished to see her mother's face one final time. She pressed her mother's feet to her eyes.

<!-- source continuation: scan 167 ends `போய்ச் சேர்ந்து`; scan 168 begins `விடவேண்டுமென்ற` -->
<!-- source: scan 168; printed page 167 -->

"Amma! Here your daughter bathes your feet with these tears. I have been denied even the blessing of remaining here, bathing them and completing your funeral rites. Let it be.

"If the crimes and stains laid upon your daughter are removed, that news will give you greater happiness than any final duty I could perform. You bore me and raised me. I found no happiness. You found no happiness.

"Mother, did you follow Father because you wished to go with your flowers and turmeric still upon you? Father gave up his life because honour had gone. Do not worry, Amma. I will make the village know that Sivanesar's family is a family with honour.

"I am going, Amma. Mother who bore me—I am going!"

Chintamani left the house in the darkness and walked toward Ayyanar. Five miles. She was completely confident she could reach it before dawn.

She had perhaps gone two or three miles when she saw a man ahead walking in the same direction. His gait was unsteady. He staggered and muttered drunkenly. Chintamani understood that he was intoxicated, but she was afraid even to look closely at him. Without turning his way, she quickened her pace and went past.

<!-- source: scan 169; printed page 168 -->

Seeing her, the man called through a slurred mouth, "Hey, woman! Who's that? Who?"

Chintamani moved faster—half walking, half running—toward Ayyanar. The man remained far behind.

Shaken by fear and embarrassment, she reached Ayyanar farm. She entered through the attractive grounds near the farm entrance and came toward the inner doorway. A dog barked at the sound of someone arriving. A rough voice followed: "Who is it?"

Chintamani stood without answering.

"Who—Idumban?" a watchman called as he approached and shone a torch upward. Seeing a woman standing there, he started.

"Who are you, Amma? What do you want?"

"Thevar..." Chintamani began.

"Ayya is here. Do you want to see him?" he immediately asked.

She nodded yes. "I'll be right back," the watchman said, going inside.

Now Chintamani became puzzled. *How can Thevar be here? Did he not say he would come from Bangalore only tomorrow?*

The watchman returned running. "Amma! Ayya says to sit in this hall. He will come now."

Chintamani sat where he showed her. The watchman went out.

She waited eagerly for Thevar. She heard the *sarak-sarak* of sandals and rose facing the sound, ready to show him respect. The red silk curtain at the inner doorway of the hall moved aside.

<!-- source continuation: scan 169 ends `ஒலி வந்த திக்கையே நோக்கியவாறு`; scan 170 begins `அவள் எழுந்துநின்றாள்,` -->
<!-- source: scan 170; printed page 169 -->

He entered.

Chintamani was surrounded by bewilderment. He was not the Balagangadhara Thevar she had seen in Bangalore.

"Who are you, Amma?" Thevar asked.

Trembling, Chintamani said, "Forgive me... I... came to see Balagangadhara Thevar..."

"I am Balagangadhara Thevar. There is no one else in this village with my name—or with greatness equal to mine," he declared grandly.

"Then who was the Balagangadhara Thevar I saw in Bangalore?" Chintamani asked.

Thevar could understand nothing. It seemed someone had deceived the woman using his name, but even that did not become clear to him. The reason was Chintamani's appearance. The instant he saw her, intoxication rose above his head.

Chintamani's position had become like a deer arriving as a feast at the tiger's own den. Though Thevar's gaze frightened her, she longed to solve the new confusion.

"What is your name?" he asked.

"Chintamani."

A smile curved at the edge of his lips.

"Then who was the Thevar who came in Bangalore? Are you really the true Thevar?" she asked again anxiously.

"I myself am Balagangadhara Thevar. Have no doubt about that," he said, clearing his throat.

At that moment the watchman hurried in.

"Master!... Idumban..." he began.

Thevar rose immediately. "Chintamani, wait a little. I'll be back," he said, and went outside with the watchman.

Nothing made sense to her. Who could the Balagangadhara Thevar who had freed her in Bangalore be?

<!-- source continuation: scan 170 ends `“எஜமான்!....இடும்பன்...”`; scan 171 begins `என்றான்.` -->
<!-- source: scan 171; printed page 170 -->

Chintamani rose and paced in the hall. From there she looked toward the garden. Thevar was hurrying that way with the watchman.

She hid beside a doorway and watched. A man approached Thevar, staggering. It had to be the same man she had passed on the road.

"Master! I finished the job!" he cried, his speech slurred.

"Hey, Idumba! Don't make noise!" Thevar snapped.

Now Chintamani understood who Idumban was. He must indeed be the man she had met on the road; even the voice was the same.

The watchman shone the torch on Idumban. His clothes were covered in blood. In his hand was a gleaming aruval, wet all over with blood.

Chintamani felt she might faint, but controlled herself and watched.

Thevar asked Idumban, "What happened? Nobody saw you, did they?"

"No, Master! I waited upstairs. There were two fellows awake for a long time. One of them was that Naina Muhammad. The two of them made all kinds of trouble. There was a quarrel between Anandi and those fellows. During that quarrel she fainted and fell. Nobody was nearby. I gave her one blow on the head and came away. By now... she'll be finished, Master!"

<!-- source continuation: scan 171 ends `வம்புச் சண்டையெல்லாம்`; scan 172 begins `நடந்தது.` -->
<!-- source: scan 172; printed page 171; Chapter 22 carryover before centered 23 -->

Sweat poured over Chintamani's whole body. Her lips dried. The shock that she had come and become trapped inside a murderer's fortress tormented her.

"All right, all right," Thevar told Idumban. "Dig a pit and bury the veshti, shirt and aruval—everything. Now. We can talk about the rest in the morning."

The watchman dug a deep pit near the garden fence. The aruval and Idumban's bloodstained clothes were put inside and buried.

"Idumba! Drink another potful and go sleep quietly. Go! Go!" Thevar ordered.

Then he returned toward the hall, eager to speak with Chintamani.
