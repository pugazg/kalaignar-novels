## 3

<!-- source: scan 23; printed page: 22 -->

Lady Doctor Anandi was still quite young. Though she had begun her hospital and practice in Vedapuram only two or three years earlier, she had already become a well-known lady doctor in the region. More than Anandi's professional skill, it was her manner with people that could win anyone over. Her youth suited it. Her appearance helped as well. True to her name, she was always seen as a figure of cheer. Even when patients pleaded for their lives, she offered comfort through her smile. Whatever the hour, whatever the condition, if people afflicted by illness came to her, the whole village praised Anandi as unmatched in attending to them with sympathy and taking care of whatever had to be done. She had no habit of flying into a rage at people writhing in sickness.

One might say that this is only the alphabet a doctor needs for the profession. Yet how many sinners forget even that child's lesson, hang the stethoscope around their neck like a noose, glare at patients like the Yama of the old myths appearing with his rope of death, and growl and pounce! Good heavens! It is in that medical world, populated by such noble souls, that helpless patients sometimes get trapped and die struggling, unable even to give up their lives in peace. There was nothing surprising in Anandi appearing to them like water in a desert mirage! In curing a patient, the place immediately after the doctor belongs to the doctor's pleasant face. Anandi not only understood this truth; she also took care to work accordingly. Her name therefore shone with influence throughout the Vedapuram region.

Old women who came to the hospital became grandmothers to Anandi. Middle-aged women came under the affectionate address of "Mother." Women of her own age were bound to her as sisters. Whether she truly thought of them that way in her heart—or adopted the manner for the growth of her medical practice—what did fever patients care? What they needed was health! So neither her regular patients nor newcomers showed any interest in entering Anandi's heart and conducting research there.

Tiger alone took the interest no one else in the village did. He spent many days trying to discover what kind of heart Anandi had. He created occasions to meet her often. He was not a patient, yet he was desperate to find out whether she possessed the medicine that could cure his own disease. Tiger had gone through no small amount of trouble in Vedapuram to help Anandi establish her clinic.

When Anandi wished to settle in that town and practise, her elder brother had written to one of his childhood friends in Vedapuram, asking him to do whatever was needed to help his sister. That friend was Tiger. He did not neglect his friend's request; he took care of every arrangement needed to start Anandi's hospital. He was an influential "minor" in the village and a pampered son at home. In villages, is it not men like these who can appear as Krishna Paramatma? Tiger was always surrounded by a troop of young men: to drink the liquor he left unfinished, to smoke the cigarette butts he threw away, and at other times to carry out his errands and feed themselves. With such a troop behind them, how could men like Tiger fail to roam as heroes in places like Vedapuram?

Tiger was not his birth name. His parents had named him Ramakrishnan. His late father had been a devotee of Paramahamsa and named his son in that memory, thrilled by the hope that the boy would grow into a Rama-like saint. Instead, he grew into a man worthy of the title Tiger—a hero of Vedapuram. Rama had a troop of monkeys beside him; so did this man! Krishna needed a troop of maidens. Tiger too was rising toward that state.

<!-- source: scan 25; printed page: 24 -->

In one way or another, the name his father had given him had turned out to suit him after all. Ramakrishnan had become Tiger. Many young deer had been caught in that Tiger's hunt. Chintamani was related to him—his maternal uncle's daughter, by one line of kinship. Tiger planned to enjoy her under the name of marriage. Chintamani's father did not bow to Tiger's threats. Neither bullying nor pleading helped bend him toward Tiger. He knew all of Tiger's activities. The whole village knew. Why, even Anandi knew what kind of man Tiger was. Only Tiger did not yet know what kind of woman Anandi was.

Perhaps to reveal that secret, Vembu came to work in Anandi's house as a servant. The villagers praised her as skilled and compassionate. Her patients recovered through the medicine she gave and, even more, through her loving care, and blessed her.

<!-- source join: scan 25 ends `சேர்ந்`; scan 26 begins `தான்.` -->
<!-- source: scan 26; printed page: 25 -->

Yet in her own personal life, such a woman had fallen into a condition far more wretched than that of an ordinary woman. It was not a condition she had sought. It had come without being sought. Once it came, it also became a condition she could no longer simply cast away—one bound up with desire and need.

Tiger began cultivating the servant Vembu. For hunters of white doves, are men like Vembu not the arrows? Tiger sharpened that arrow for two days. Tea. Night-time outings with Tiger. That was all. Vembu began spilling truths.

A conversation that began with inquiries about Anandi's family reached the boundary of Tiger's real target. "Anandi is a bold woman, man! Living all by herself—what a capable one!" Tiger praised her and prodded Vembu's mouth open.

"Yes, in that family Anandi Amma is the brave one! A good woman! Her brother is a coward—once the lamps are lit, he won't even step outside! And for all that, he's the editor of a paper called *Idimuzhakkam*!" Vembu went on describing matters Tiger had no interest in. Tiger struggled to twist and bend his tongue in the desired direction, warmed it with a little tasty drink, applied his own skill at conversation, and finally succeeded.

"Even though she's such a clever woman, our madam got cheated once, didn't she?"

"How, Vembu—how?"

<!-- source: scan 27; printed page: 26 -->

"At the fifth mile there's that big estate called Ayyanar, you know?"

"Yes, I know it! I even know the estate owner—Balagangadhara Thevar!"

"Ah... One night a man came running in a hurry saying there was a childbirth at Thevar's house!"

"To call Anandi?"

"Yes! Madam immediately took me along and left in the car that had come from the estate. The two of us went and reached the place!"

"Where? The estate house?"

"Yes, that's what they said! It was pitch dark. A lamp was flickering in the street. We got down at the entrance. From inside, I kept hearing someone cry out as though in labour pains. The estate owner—Thevar himself—received madam and took her inside. I alone sat at the entrance. I had a doubt: 'This is such a big man's house, he even owns cars—why isn't there a single soul to be seen at the doorway?' I wondered."

"Hmm. Then?"

"What then... I sat there for a long time. After a while there was no sound from inside at all. I had heard cries of labour pains; even those stopped!"

"Oh-ho, so the child must have been born!"

"That's just it—it hadn't! Only later did I realize there wasn't even a woman there to give birth!"

"What—what? Tell me properly!"

<!-- source: scan 28; printed page: 27 -->

"Wondering why there was no sound, I went near the door. It was bolted from inside. I thought I'd look through a window. Every window was shut. I knocked and called out. I heard Anandi Amma's voice. 'Ayyo, sinner! Let me go! Let me go!' she screamed. I couldn't bear it. I understood. Balagangadhara Thevar had been casting his net for many days, and that night he had finally done what he wanted—I knew it! I opened my mouth to shout at the top of my voice. That was all. Someone gagged me. Someone tied ropes around my hands and legs. They rolled me into the veranda. No one covered my ears. I listened to what was happening inside. For some time I could hear Anandi Amma's furious voice. Then... no sound at all! I felt as though I was going to faint.... The door opened. My bonds were untied. Doctor Amma stood motionless like a statue. On her face... ayyayyo... I saw something deathlike spread across it and I trembled. Thevar told us to get into the car; we did. He came and stood near the car to send us off. 'Careful! If this matter gets out, you'll lose your head. Yes—you! I'm talking to you,' he said, giving me a knock on the head. The car began to move. Right in front of me and the driver, Thevar kissed Doctor Amma on the face. Madam was like a corpse. She did not say a word in protest!"

"After that, does this Thevar... come and go?......"

"Maybe he does, maybe he doesn't. I haven't seen him. But Amma fell at my feet and begged me not to tell the matter outside. Now I'm falling at your feet and asking you—wash your ears clean and forget it!"

<!-- source join: scan 28 ends `விஷயத்தை வெளி`; scan 29 begins `யில் சொல்லாதே...` -->
<!-- source: scan 29; printed page: 28 -->

Tiger had won. He had learned a secret that could determine Doctor Anandi's future. He set out resolved to build the fortress of his desire on that secret.

As always, Anandi welcomed Tiger that day too. However wastefully he might roam about the village, toward her he behaved as her brother's childhood friend and had done her so many favours—that was how she thought of him. What did she know? A tiger that would leap upon a spotted deer the moment it saw one and sate its hunger was holding back its frenzy and waiting for the right moment to test its claws on a cow.

So she trusted him. She also felt affection for him. She had not painted that affection with the colour of any particular relationship, but it was affection without stain.

That day Tiger looked sad in a way she had never seen before. Anandi looked at him in surprise and asked, "Why are you so dejected?"

Tiger's voice trembled. He sobbed and sobbed.

"Why? Why like this?... Has something happened... to someone?" Anandi asked anxiously.

"Nothing has happened to anyone! Anandi, I'm afraid even to look at your face......... Ayyo, I can't even stand before you!"

"What are you saying? Is my face frightening?"

<!-- source: scan 30; printed page: 29 -->

"Your face used to be like an unplucked red lotus—I don't know how so many scratches of stain came to fall upon it... I used to go around praising your face as a moon of milk; how could that moon wane like this?"

"What? What are you talking about?"

"Anandi! Why did you hide this from me all these days? If you had told me the very next day, I would have cut that fool's throat and set his corpse afloat. Demon! Madman!! Wretch who does not know the dignity of womanhood!!!"

"What you are saying is......?"

"I am talking about Balagangadhara Thevar—the sinner who violated your chastity!"

The moment those words struck her ears, Anandi fainted and fell. She became an example of the way some women suffer more from the shame when the fact that their chastity has been taken becomes known than from the loss itself. When she regained consciousness she screamed, wept and lamented.

Tiger used the moment skilfully.

"Don't worry! From now on, no one but me will know this news. Wasn't it because a woman was alone that room was given for that Thevar's arrogance? From now on, I am the guard for you and for your chastity!" Weeping, sobbing, he took Anandi's hands and pressed them to his cheeks. A sense of pity and obligation now enslaved Anandi.

<!-- source: scan 31; printed page: 30 -->

All she could do was sigh at the thought that there was at least one man who would care about her. She did not even register as something different that her head had come to rest upon his shoulders.

She certainly did not wish for the rough feet of men like Tiger to be planted on her heart, softer than cotton and silk. But what was she to do? A jasmine field needed a thorn fence.

Thus Anandi became. She was a jasmine garden, he said. Rough cattle like Thevar must not enter it; Tiger would be the thorn fence. A tiger became the guard for a deer! The circumstances could not be changed, so that young woman altered her mind to suit the circumstances.

How many more vultures like Balagangadhara might fly in to peck at her youth and torture it? To escape their dreadful claws, Anandi—the goat—thought it only reasonable to choose the sacrificial altar of a goddess as her place of safety.

Tiger rejoiced, treating this as sugarcane obtained through Vembu. Anandi kept her relationship with him somewhat secret, fearing damage to her professional standing. Tiger would console her by saying that chastity taken by force was something eligible for absolution from sin and for atonement. She would take comfort in it. He would think about the two of them becoming life-partners and hint at it. "I am waiting for the right time. There are three people in this world who know about you. One is Thevar. Another is his car driver. The third is Vembu. If I get rid of all three, there will be no one left in the world to talk about Anandi's stain. After that—marriage," Tiger would say. Anandi did not like that argument. Yet she endured it. How was she to live after leaving him too? People who want to stay alive will try to reach the shore holding even a weak moringa branch. Anandi was of that kind.

It was to her hospital that Chintamani was brought. Anandi realized that Chintamani's condition was serious. She carried out every treatment that had to be done urgently and necessarily, assured the parents that Chintamani would survive, and said it would be best for her to rest in the hospital under Anandi's observation for two days.

Sivanesar and Sivakami pleaded with Anandi to restore their daughter fully to health somehow and returned home. The groom's family would arrive at the appointed time. They wanted to take leave of Chintamani, who lay in bed.

But Chintamani had regained consciousness only a short while earlier and had closed her eyes again in exhaustion. So without speaking to their daughter, they told the doctor they would return in the evening and left for home. A short while after they had gone, Tiger arrived at the hospital with Vembu. Anandi told him about Chintamani's accident. Tiger and Vembu went over to the bed where Chintamani lay with her eyes closed in fatigue, looked at her, and returned. A new kind of whirl appeared in Tiger's eyes; long, deep, frightening furrows formed on his forehead.

"Anandi! I need to speak to you alone for a little while. Come this way," Tiger said, taking her toward a room.
