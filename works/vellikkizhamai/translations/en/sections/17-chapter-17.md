## 17

<!-- source: scan 134; printed page 133; post-centered 17 -->

> Dear Azhagu! Greetings. Anandi has recovered fully and returned to Vedapuram. I will arrive in two days. There is much to talk about!
>
> Your loving friend,
> Naina.

Azhagappan's tongue faltered.

"Amma! We have been deceived!" he cried.

He immediately telephoned the police, gave them Tiger's identifying details, and was told that they would begin searching at once.

Azhagappan felt he had to go to Vedapuram and see Anandi once. But how could he tell his mother? Naina had written that he himself would arrive in a day or two. Even so, Azhagappan felt his longing would not settle until he saw Anandi.

He told the old mother that his mind was in utter confusion and that he was going to Vedapuram to learn the facts. That very night he left. By eight the next morning he reached Vedapuram.

Anandi and Naina Muhammad were drinking coffee. Hearing someone enter, they turned and saw Azhagappan standing there. Anandi sprang to her feet and welcomed him with "Come!" Naina embraced him.

"What, man! Such a sudden visit?" Naina asked eagerly.

The question did not even catch Azhagappan's attention. Looking at Anandi, he asked, "How is your health? Completely recovered?"

She nodded, smiled and said, "I'm well."

"Have some coffee," Naina said, seating Azhagappan beside him. Anandi sat opposite them.

<!-- source join: scan 134 ends `நயினாவின்`; scan 135 begins `கேள்வி` -->
<!-- source: scan 135; printed page 134 -->

"Is Mother well at home?" Anandi asked.

Do you know what that question did to Azhagappan? When a daughter-in-law asks after her mother-in-law like that, would happiness not overflow in the bridegroom's heart?

"You look very thin," he said, looking at Anandi.

"You too look a little thinner," she replied.

*How much concern she already shows for my health! If it is like this now, what will the future be like?* A sweet question rose within him.

"I have put on a little weight, haven't I?" Naina asked, smiling toward Anandi.

Azhagappan then told them in detail how Tiger had claimed that Anandi was dead, that Naina had run away with the jewels, and had cheated him out of a thousand rupees.

"Sinner!" Naina cried. "I am not even surprised that you believed Anandi had died. But how did you believe that I stole the jewels and ran away? After all these years of friendship, is that all the account you have kept of me?"

Naina spoke with hurt. Azhagu offered a thousand explanations.

<!-- source join: scan 135 ends `வருத்தத்`; scan 136 begins `தோடு` -->
<!-- source: scan 136; printed page 135 -->

Anandi wanted somehow to bring up Chintamani and make Azhagappan understand the truth. She signalled to Naina. He failed to understand the gesture and thought she was asking him to leave so she could speak privately.

"All right, you two talk. I'll be back. I'll go as far as the market street," he said, getting up.

He also thought it might be better for Anandi herself to explain Chintamani than for him to do it. So he left, believing it useful to stay out of the way for a while.

Azhagappan inwardly rejoiced over how he would thank his friend. Anandi spoke of one thing and another while searching for a path toward the subject of Chintamani.

"How far have you got with the matter of marriage?" she asked.

Azhagappan did not answer at once. He was astonished and delighted that she had boldly raised the very matter he had wanted to ask her about.

"Is marriage something that can be completed as easily as one imagines?" he replied with a question.

"You are talking about the method of conducting a marriage. That is not what I asked. I meant the bride..." Anandi drew the words out.

<!-- source: scan 137; printed page 136 -->

"I too am speaking about that. Unexpectedly we ran into a mountain and became entangled in trouble—my mother, my friend and I. At least the next attempt should not break at the very beginning, should it?"

"There is a proverb: what the eye sees may be false, what the ear hears may be false; only thorough inquiry is truth. I believe it completely. You...?"

"How can I understand if you speak in circles? If we say it briefly and a little plainly, neither of us will remain confused."

"I certainly have no confusion. You are the one who..."

"True—I am confused. Not now; the confusion began last Friday itself. I have only been wondering what your answer would be."

"My answer will be favourable to you. It will bring happiness to your life."

"Why separate it as 'your life'? Why not say 'our life'?"

Anandi's face changed at once. Concealing it from him, she said, "All right, it is almost time for tiffin. Why don't you brush your teeth?" and rose.

She hurried Velu to bring breakfast quickly. When Velu left, Azhagappan realized that he and Anandi were alone. He longed to express his thought completely.

"Is there tooth powder?" he asked, peering into Anandi's room.

"Here—I'll bring it," she said, hurrying out.

<!-- source join: scan 137 ends open quote `“இதோ,`; scan 138 begins `கொண்டுவருகிறேன்”` -->
<!-- source: scan 138; printed page 137 -->

Azhagappan's tongue began to falter. An uncontrollable tremor shook his nerves. Anandi brought the tooth-powder tin and handed it to him. As he took it, the soft touch of her hand made him dizzy.

After brushing he asked for soap. She brought that too. Though a towel already hung over his shoulder, he asked for another to wipe his face. When she gave it to him, Azhagappan's fingers closed firmly around hers.

"Will you eat tiffin now, or after he returns?" she asked.

"Why not the two of us eat now?" he replied in a trembling voice.

Anandi struggled with emotions she could not show. She had hoped to bring up Chintamani and seek cleansing for her own wrongdoing; Azhagappan's conduct shocked her.

Velu arrived with breakfast. Anandi asked him to serve it. Azhagappan expected her to sit nearby and eat too. She did not. Velu placed tiffin and coffee before him and left to attend to hospital work.

Azhagappan did not even like gingelly oil, but asked, "Is there a little good oil?"

With no other choice, Anandi brought some and poured it. Saying, "Enough! Enough!" Azhagappan caught her hand to stop her. The oil jar in her hand overturned onto the table.

Unable to remain there, she went rapidly toward her room. Azhagappan himself gathered what oil he could back into the jar and wiped the table. When he tried to eat, he could not; his throat closed.

<!-- source join: scan 138 ends literal `‘மளமள’`; scan 139 begins `வென்று` -->
<!-- source: scan 139; printed page 138 -->

He rose and entered Anandi's room. She sat leaning on a sofa, one hand to her head.

"Anandi," he said softly.

"Please go outside and sit," she pleaded.

He did not know what to do or say. A struggle arose within him: *Was the way I behaved right or wrong?* If it was wrong, he thought, he somehow had to make her agree to that same wrong so that both would become guilty; otherwise he would forever have to stand before her with his head bowed as the sole offender. He clearly realized that he had been hasty.

Deciding it was best to learn her view, he asked, "Anandi! Are you saying that what I think is wrong?"

"What do you think?" came her trembling reply.

"You must have understood what I think."

"Men make your calculations about women so casually!"

"It was wrong, Anandi. Without knowing your heart I acted according to my own. Forgive me. But listen—last Friday I became like a man struck by a storm. You gave me peace. Today again storm, earthquake and volcano have all risen in my life!"

<!-- source join: scan 139 ends `புயலால் தாக்குண்டவன்போல`; scan 140 begins `ஆனேன் அன்று!` -->
<!-- source: scan 140; printed page 139 -->

"I suffer greatly for you. I too was one of those responsible for the cyclone that blew through your life. I am now the one stepping forward to calm that cyclone—but not in the way you imagine."

"Then in what way, Anandi?"

"The ones who made you believe Chintamani was stained were that sinner Tiger—and me."

Azhagappan did not allow her to continue. He concluded that she was again devising a trick to turn Chintamani into a good woman and tie her around his head.

"Do not even bring up Chintamani with me. I want to know about you. Answer only that."

She said nothing. She stood silent. He too remained near her instead of leaving the room. Once again the cord of emotion spun him like a top.

"Anandi, answer me! Why are you silent? Isn't silence a sign of consent?" he asked, putting an arm around her shoulder.

At exactly that moment Naina Muhammad entered.

Anandi broke free at once, left the room and went toward the garden. Naina said nothing, behaving as though he had not seen such a scene at all.

"What, Azhagu, finished your tiffin?" he asked with his usual expression.

Embarrassment ran across Azhagu's face, but he managed to say, "I've eaten." Sweat poured over his whole body.

<!-- source join: scan 140 ends `நயினா`; scan 141 begins `எதுவும் பேசவில்லை.` -->
<!-- source: scan 141; printed page 140 -->

"Anandi! Anandi!" Naina called.

She came in from the garden asking, "Why?"

"Have you seen the paper? Two pieces of news from Vedapuram have appeared," he said, handing it to her.

"What news from Vedapuram?" Azhagu asked, trying to compose himself.

"One is about Tiger cheating you."

"And the other?"

"They say Chintamani was caught by the police carrying opium and has been sentenced to three months' rigorous imprisonment."

Azhagu was not greatly shocked by that news.

Anandi cried, "Ah!"

Just then Velu ran in quickly.

"Amma! Amma! Listen to the news! Chintamani's father, Sivanesar, fell into the river and killed himself!"

Silence settled over the hall. Azhagu, Naina and Anandi stood motionless, none wishing to look at the others' faces.
