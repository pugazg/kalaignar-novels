## 6

<!-- source: scan 52; printed page 51 -->

Azhagappan immediately lifted her and rested her against his lap. Blood was gushing from her head. “Who is it? Doesn’t she look like the Lady Doctor?” Naina Muhammad said in surprise. “Anandi Amma! Anandi Amma!” Villagers gathered at the spot with frightened cries.

Anandi did not open her eyes. She was unconscious. The blood flowing from the split in her head would not stop. Azhagappan looked at those nearby and urgently asked, “Where is there another good hospital in this village?”

“What hospital is there here? There is only one hospital—and this has happened to the very person who runs it!” the people around them lamented.

“If you go five or six miles, there is Palaiyur. It is like a small town. There’s a good hospital there!” one man said, showing them the way.

Azhagappan and the others lifted Anandi into the car and set off. She lay against the old mother’s lap. Naina Muhammad pressed a cloth firmly against Anandi’s head, trying to reduce the flow of blood. Blood that seeped past the cloth was turning the inside of the car red. The old mother’s mind was in torment.

“I don’t know, son! Ever since we spoke of an alliance with that cursed house, Saturn has caught hold of us!” she lamented.

Emergency treatment began for Anandi at the Palaiyur hospital. The doctors said that she had lost a great deal of blood and that, had there been even a little more delay, her life might have been in danger. Leaving the old mother at the hospital, Azhagappan and Naina set out to inform the police of the accident.

All the immediate treatment Anandi needed was completed. There was a serious wound on her head, and a few stitches had to be put in. But she remained unconscious. Now and then she would twist her body as though trying to turn over in bed. At those moments her lips would utter, “Stop! Stop!”

The doctors concluded that she was raving from the shock of the injury. It was true that she was raving—but what certainty was there that it was the fright from the injury that made her rave?

“Stop!”

“Stop!!”

She might have been raising that cry to stop Azhagappan from his hasty conclusions too.

Anandi knew very well that Chintamani was not the mother of a child—that she was an unmarried young woman.

Her good heart had, for the first time, tried to bring harm to another person, and had collapsed in that very attempt. The car accident was its consequence.

When Chintamani had stumbled and fallen at the pond and been brought to the hospital, savage plans had flared up in Tiger’s mind. To carry out that plan he chose Anandi. Men had already been sent to finish off the groom’s party on the road. Tiger had been roaming about wondering what to do if they escaped those men, when Chintamani happened to be brought to the hospital.

He decided that the groom’s people would somehow have to come to the hospital to see the bride. Nor was he wrong in thinking so. Despite all the barriers of sastras, custom and omen that Sivanesar raised, Azhagappan had indeed wanted to steal a look at Chintamani. Understanding this turn of mind, Tiger proposed to Anandi a plan to make Chintamani an object of disgust. She could not even bring herself simply to dismiss him.

“An unforgivable sin!” she protested.

Tiger raged that Azhagappan had long been his enemy and that he had to prevent him from marrying Chintamani at any cost.

“Her life will become a wilderness stretching before her!” Anandi said with tears in her eyes.

<!-- source join: scan 53 ends `அனுப்பப்பட்டிருக்க`; scan 54 begins `கிறார்கள்.` -->
<!-- source: scan 54; printed page 53 -->

“I care about that more than you do! Chintamani is my relative. It is my responsibility to find a groom suited to her and get her married!” Tiger spoke with great concern.

Anandi’s body trembled. She had not trembled so even in Balagangadhara Thevar’s bedchamber.

“What now? Will you do as I say, or are you going to forget me?” he growled.

Fear and agitation tossed her about. She had to agree, did she not? Had she not, at the very beginning, yielded herself to him out of helpless deference?

The plan was simply to place beside Chintamani a child born to another woman. A child lying beside a woman may not sound terrible by itself—but in a maternity hospital its meaning was entirely different. And this was a young woman whose tali had yet to be tied, a maiden whose unmarried state was intact, a girl for whom only the fixing of a wedding date remained. Tiger used the opportunity perfectly and carried the plot through successfully.

<!-- source: scan 55; printed page 54 -->

After Azhagappan had poured out words like tongues of fire at the hospital and left, Anandi lost her composure. She hurried toward Chintamani’s bed. Chintamani lay exhausted, looking in bewilderment and distress at the child beside her. She could neither move nor rise.

“What is this, Amma? Whose child is this?” Chintamani asked Anandi.

Tears spilled from Anandi’s eyes. She ran over, lifted the child and said, “Chintamani! Chintamani!” Her throat closed before she could go on.

“What is it, Amma? I don’t understand anything! A child beside me! And when I ask, you cry! Two people came; they said something about my father! What is going on?” Chintamani began to question her.

“Nothing, Chintamani! Don’t strain yourself! You are still dazed; that is why it seems so to you,” Anandi said. Carrying the child, she left the room. But Chintamani’s unstained face would not leave her eyes.

<!-- source join: scan 55 ends `அறைவிட்டு அகன்றாள்`; scan 56 begins `ஆனந்தி.` -->
<!-- source: scan 56; printed page 55 -->

Anandi could not bear the pain when she thought that the young body pulsing with life and the sweet face that revealed a heart worth gold would be soiled by the label “the woman who slipped and fell.”

“How much I suffered after being caught in Balagangadhara Thevar’s grip and shattered! How I trembled when I learned that Tiger had come to know of it! If it was like this even for me, what will happen to an innocent woman if the village mocks her? Aiyyo, cruelty—terrible cruelty! I am not fit even for the work of a Lady Doctor! I have heard of some Lady Doctors using their calling to rescue women from pits of disgrace; I do not know whether that is true or false—but as for me, I have made an innocent woman lower than a dog on a rubbish heap.”

Anandi groaned and wept. She panicked over how she could cleanse herself of her wrongdoing. At that moment Vembu arrived. That loose-tongued man blurted out that Tiger had planned all this so that he could marry Chintamani himself.

The news poured fire into Anandi’s belly. For Tiger’s sake she had dared to commit a great wrong. She could not bear that the same wrongdoing was becoming a road toward Tiger’s new aim. She resolved that a man who had blackened the future of a woman white as the thumbai flower and was also plotting betrayal against her must not succeed. Her disturbed heart reached a firm decision.

Tiger intervened, but he could not stop her. With a diamond-hard heart determined to establish the truth, she set out.

“Go! Go, woman! No one can throw earth over Tiger’s purpose!” he roared, then laughed aloud. He turned and growled toward the place where Chintamani lay. By then Anandi had begun running down the street. She was running to Sivanesar’s house to meet the groom’s family, tell them the truth and ask forgiveness.

<!-- source: scan 57; printed page 56 -->

But her wish was not fulfilled. She met with the accident.

“Stop!”

“Stop!!”

Only that word came out as her lament. The doctors called it delirium.

After finishing matters at the police station, Azhagappan and Naina Muhammad returned. Anandi had still not regained consciousness.

Azhagappan noticed that she kept repeating only the word “Stop!”

He asked Naina Muhammad whether there might be some connection between the dreadful scene he had seen in the hospital and her repeated cry. Naina thought deeply but did not answer at once.

“What are you thinking about like some great Buddha? Answer me, man!” Azhagu snapped.

“Was Buddha the only one who thought? Did our Prophet not think?... In your... Hindu religion—”

<!-- source: scan 58; printed page 57 -->

“No one has thought at all till now—hey, Naina! Is this the time for religious research? We are caught in such a crisis... and you forget that—”

“What crisis, man? How many times have I told you, ‘Take everything very casually’? Whatever happens is for the good!”

“What is for the good? All this trouble at the very place we came to see a bride—is that for the good too?”

“Yes! How good that we learned at the very beginning that the girl was wrong! We discovered just by looking whether the sweet was bitter or sweet, didn’t we? Our good fortune was that it did not even get the chance to spoil our tongue!”

Unsatisfied by Naina Muhammad’s words, Azhagu rose and began pacing along the veranda. At that moment Tiger hurried into the hospital. Seeing him, Azhagappan, with a trace of fear, received him with, “Come! Come!” and asked forgiveness.

“Tch! Tch! That is nothing. I don’t think about things like that at all. Mistakes happen!” Tiger said with a grin, offering consolation. He then went to see Anandi. She had still not emerged from unconsciousness.

“Stop!”

“Stop!!”

Only that word kept sounding intermittently.

Tiger again came out to the veranda, talking with Azhagappan.

“What relation is there between Anandi and you?” Azhagappan asked.

<!-- source: scan 59; printed page 58; Chapter 6 carryover before centered 7 -->

“Think of her as a sister—a distant relation. That’s all!”

“Where was she running in such a hurry?”

“All for your sake!”

“For my sake?”

“Yes—to change your suspicion; to free Chintamani from the stain on her name!”

“What? What? To free Chintamani from the stain?”

“Yes. She is a very good woman!”

“Who?”

“Anandi!”

“Chintamani?”

“She too is pure gold!”

Hearing this, Azhagappan sank into astonishment.
