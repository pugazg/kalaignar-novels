## 14

<!-- source: scan 115; printed page 114; post-centered 14 -->

An arrow he had never expected had struck Naina. It took him a long time to regain control of himself. The self-abasement in Anandi's words was not visible on her face; he could see that too. He told himself that there was nothing surprising in heated words flying like sparks when a woman disgusted with life, yet afraid to die, angrily exposed the darkness of her private life.

Instead of contempt for her, he felt sympathy. What circumstances had surrounded her? Why had she been forced into such ruin? He tossed questions about in his mind and could find no answer. One thing alone was clear to him now: Tiger had not been the medicine Anandi had chosen for the itching of young desire in her heart.

For only a short time did Naina wonder whether he should continue his effort after hearing her words. Then he reached a decision. There was a slight change in the purpose behind his desire. One could call that change marriage. Along with his wish to fulfil his own longing, he joined a broader purpose: her life must prosper, and he must become the person who supported it.

<!-- source: scan 116; printed page 115 -->

Anandi was confused now. Not wishing to trouble her further, Naina let her sleep and returned to his place on the veranda. *Let morning bloom; then I will make a plan for a rebirth in Anandi's life too,* he thought, sitting and watching the darkness begin to lose its hold.

* * *

Sivanesar, Sivakami and Chintamani, whom Tiger had sent away, reached home. With great difficulty they shut Sivanesar inside the house.

Chintamani stood with tears in her eyes, thinking of all the trouble that had arisen since morning. As though to break her already shaken heart further, Sivakami told her everything without omission: how the Bangalore groom's family had formed a wrong opinion of her, how they had left in anger, and how Anandi had been struck by the car and taken to the Palaiyur hospital.

Chintamani could not bear the terrible fact that a stain had been imagined upon her chastity. How could she? She was a painted bird with wings made to fly through a sky of life filled with charm and beauty. If her wings were cut and she was thrown into an iron pit, how could she accept the pain?

Why had such suffering come to her? She could find no answer. People who refuse to sharpen their reason may speak of the merit earned in a former birth. Chintamani could find no peace in such Vedantic ideas. She searched for an answer and wept against her mother's breast.

"Why, Amma? Why has such misery come to me? What betrayal have I committed against anyone? My whole life has been wasted, Mother! They might as well have killed Chintamani—Aiyyo! From now on no one will show respect even to my corpse!" she cried.

Her mother could not stop the stream of her daughter's tears. She too wept. Chintamani thought about who could have profited by making the groom's family believe that she was stained. Tiger—surely Tiger! She reached the firm conclusion that he was the one who had wanted this plot to happen.

"How am I to escape from this cruel accusation, Amma?"

"I don't know, my child. Whatever explanation we give, the groom's family will not accept you now."

"Let them not accept me. I do not care. But at least I must make them change the false opinion they hold of me, Amma!"

"Chintamani! Do not torture me. I never imagined such an hour of ruin would enter our family!"

"I must establish that I am pure. I want no marriage—no stone memorial either! It is enough if my father and the world say that I am unstained. I would not mind dying the instant I heard those good words. Until it is proved that I am innocent, I will not die! I will not die!"

Chintamani shouted like a woman possessed. Sivakami moved to calm her and make her sit—

<!-- source: scan 118; printed page 117; literal source begins after unresolved physical discontinuity; no inferred completion -->

No matter how much she tried, it did not work.

Chintamani declared in fury that she must go to the Palaiyur hospital immediately, see Anandi and ask why she had allowed this cruelty to be done to her. No explanation from Sivakami could stop her.

"I must go to Palaiyur now! I must learn the truth there and then go straight to Bangalore!" That was her angry decision. Her mother tried to prevent her from going anywhere in the darkness.

"Darkness? Is it more terrible than my life? How can I face the people of this village in daylight? How can I lift my face before my dear friends? Only after I have cleared away the fog of disgrace covering me will I return to Vedapuram. Amma, look after Father carefully. I will return."

Sivakami blocked her path.

"My child! The stain can do nothing to you. To me you are gold of the purest touch, a flawless gem. Why should we care what the village, the world or those hasty people of the groom's family say? Will they even forget that you are my daughter? It is only the marriage that will fail. Let it fail!" she sobbed.

"I am not worried about marriage, Amma! I have read *Manimekalai*. I too can create such a heart within myself. But the slander—the word of disgrace—thrown upon me must be removed completely. Until then I have no sleep, no energy for anything else. Do not stop me! My heart is burning like live embers. Water must be poured upon it.

"I will return... I will return victorious. If not, you and Father may remain and perform the final rites that must be done for me!"

<!-- source: scan 119; printed page 118 -->

How would the womb that had borne her not burn when a daughter spoke like that? Sivakami could neither sob nor speak; she became a statue. Nor did she any longer wish to stop Chintamani.

Chintamani fell at her mother's feet, bowed, and ran from the house. The night had already taken its first step toward turning into dawn, and she kept running through it.

She had to meet Anandi at Palaiyur. She had to demand an answer for why Anandi had allowed her to be turned into a symbol of disgrace. If she did not receive a proper answer, she would take revenge. Carrying such vengeance, she ran through the streets like a storm.

Was running from Vedapuram to Palaiyur such an easy thing? That fear did not rise in her, nor was there any reason for it to rise in the state she was in.

Running and running, Chintamani finally entered Palaiyur. Even a racing athlete might not have come from Vedapuram to Palaiyur so quickly. A racer's purpose is to win a prize. Was not hers to wipe away disgrace?

She wandered the streets of Palaiyur looking for the hospital. A police vehicle came toward her. Tiger, arrested at the hospital, was in that very vehicle.

Chintamani did not know that the poisonous serpent that had come to scatter venom over her life was passing directly before her. But Tiger saw a woman approaching quickly and noticed that she looked like Chintamani.

When she passed beneath a lamp post, Tiger's suspicion became strong. He told himself that there was a ninety-percent chance it really was Chintamani. Perhaps she had heard what had happened, become heartbroken and set off somewhere.

He yearned to catch her somehow, at once. Is it false that when women are concerned even great danger looks like a mere straw?

While the police vehicle was still moving, Tiger jumped out. The police chased him, but he ran somewhere and disappeared.
