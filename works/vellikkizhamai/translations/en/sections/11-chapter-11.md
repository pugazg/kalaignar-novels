## 11

<!-- source: scan 92; printed page 91; post-centered 11 -->

Delighted that he had been given yet another chance to touch that mango-golden body, Tiger hurried to lift Chintamani from the ground. She struggled to free herself from his grasp and tried to run toward Sivanesar, crying, “Father!”

“Don’t go near him—he has gone mad!” Tiger shouted, wrapping his arms around her to pull her back and refusing to loosen his hold even slightly.

<!-- source join: scan 92 ends `வெறிபிடித்துப்`; scan 93 begins `போயிருக்கிறார்` -->
<!-- source: scan 93; printed page 92 -->

Sivanesar came rushing toward them like a charging tiger.

“Destroyer of the lineage! Woman who went down a crooked path! Ahahaha...... my rare Chintamani...... my Abithana Chintamani...... Aiyyo! Aiyyo!! Murderess who killed me and my wife! Ruin of the household! Chintamani—ruined gem! Woman who ought to have been born a demon! Fiend born as a woman!” Sivanesar showered her with abuse.

Chintamani could only cry, “Father!” She could say nothing else.

“Be quiet, Chintamani! Your father has gone mad; you must not speak to him!” Tiger said, covering her mouth.

Chintamani’s anger crossed all bounds, and a new strength rose within her. She swung and struck Tiger on the cheek. The bangles she wore shattered, leaving lines of blood across his face.

“Hit him! Hit him!! Hit him properly! Hit him! Hit him, you sly woman! Why are you hitting your husband?” Sivanesar cried, then began to sing:

> “What wrong did he commit?
> Why strike him on the cheek?
> Woman, you need only speak!......
> Then alone will my anger rise!”

Chintamani now understood that what Tiger had said about her father was true.

> “Love—love—love—love!
> Secret love!
> Love within;
> True love!
> One-sided love!
> Love that comes to nothing!”

<!-- source: scan 94; printed page 93 -->

Raving these words, Sivanesar began dancing in the middle of the street. He laughed loudly in a frightening tone. Suddenly he cried. Then he shouted in anger.

> “Let earthly life go—let earthly life go!
> Groom and bride,
> Coconut shoot in the grove......
> Let earthly life go!”

As he chanted, he scooped up street sand and flung it into Tiger’s and Chintamani’s faces. Tiger let go of Chintamani to wipe his eyes. She ran to Sivanesar, clutched his feet and sobbed, “Father! Father! What state is this, Father?”

“State? This is disorder! A hurried state! A hurried kolam flung and scattered! A new kolam drawn for Pongal! The ascetic form of this Dasaratha who has come to Dandakaranya! A Saivite sacred form! Hey, Karaikkal Ammaiyar! Change your body at once into a demon form, walk upside down and run to Kailasa! I am Lord Siva—the one with the eye on his forehead! I am setting out to test the wife of Iyarppagai Nayanar. I am setting out to cook and eat Siruthondar’s child as meat. I have arisen to test all the wives of the rishis of Darukavana. Here is my Garuda vehicle! I am going to mount it and set out to test queens of chastity!”

Raving like this, Sivanesar climbed into the cart and sat down. At that moment Sivakami came running toward them in agitation. Mother saw daughter; daughter saw mother. They embraced one another and sobbed. Sivanesar saw them and laughed loudly. The place itself became the stage for a scene of grief.

<!-- source join: scan 94 ends `இருவரும் ஒருவரை`; scan 95 begins `தழுவிக்கொண்டு` -->
<!-- source: scan 95; printed page 94 -->

When mother and daughter met, neither could think of anything to say.

“What has happened to Father, Amma? He was perfectly well this morning!” Chintamani asked in overwhelming sorrow.

“All of this disaster came because of you, my child! Friday had only to dawn—and the hour of destruction came upon our family too!” Sivakami began sobbing.

Tiger interrupted the tragic moment. “Aunt! Must you enact your whole Ramayana in the middle of the street?” he asked, somewhat sternly and harshly.

Sivanesar sprang forward as though to answer him.

“Where else should the Ramayana be enacted if not in the middle of the street? The most important part of the Ramayana happened in the forest, didn’t it? Only one quarter happened in the country! Rama and Sita were in the forest for sixteen years; later Valmiki and Sita were in the forest for many years; between these came the Asoka grove, the sea Hanuman crossed, the forest where he and Sugriva hid and wandered; in the Balakanda too, wandering with Visvamitra, killing Tataka, stepping on Ahalya with the foot and blessing her with the mouth—all of it happened in the forest! So the Ramayana is a forest story! A story written with some forest creatures as hero and heroine! Therefore the Ramayana may be enacted in the middle of the street! Hey, Sivakami! You enact the Ramayana!”

<!-- source: scan 96; printed page 95 -->

“Here she is—the missing Sita! Hey, Ravana! Did you carry away my Sita?...... Speak! Speak!”

Watching Sivanesar stand there saying and raving whatever came to his mouth, Sivakami gripped him and demanded with some menace, “Are you going to keep quiet now or not?”

Tiger ran over to help and bound him with a cloth. Then, with the cartman’s help, they tied his legs too and laid him in the cart. Tiger had Sivakami and Chintamani get in, told them to go home, and said he would come in the morning and attend to everything. Then he took leave.

Sivanesar rolled from side to side inside the cart so violently that he looked capable of overturning the whole vehicle.

Chintamani and her mother sat looking at one another in pain, their faces reflecting the longing that there was no one to console them in their suffering. They worried only about somehow reaching home. The cart, as though painting a picture of the condition of their hearts, swayed and jolted along the unrepaired village road, climbing in and out of pits and hollows as it moved toward their house.

At the Palaiyur medical ward, Naina Muhammad had been yearning for a chance to give Anandi something with his own hands. Such an opportunity seemed to arrive at midnight.

Under the rules of the ward, Naina could stay only in the outer veranda. He had no chance to remain close beside Anandi. Yet he did not sleep. Sitting in a chair on the veranda, he watched her from there.

“Will she not move? Will she not wake even for a moment?” he asked himself.

He realized too that no one in the hospital except him seemed to be awake. A desire came upon him: to look at Anandi’s face for a little while without blinking.

Can one stare continuously at a woman while she is awake? Even if one can, there is no delight in it. If a man and woman keep looking at each other without blinking, there is no *rasa* in that prolonged gaze; only awkwardness appears. When he looks, she looks at the ground. When he does not look, she looks at him. As their eyes are cast at one another in turns like this, suddenly both pairs of eyes are caught together like thieves. Shyness enslaves them both. In shyness mixed with womanhood there appears a special greatness, a great beauty. The man savours it. A lover who looks without interruption at the sleeping face of the woman he loves receives the same delight. This condition too has its place in love.

Driven by that desire, Naina Muhammad slowly rose, crossed the veranda and tried to go inside. A fire of longing had risen in him to look at her leisurely and enjoy the sight. He walked step by step.

It is precisely when one tries to move stealthily like this that a wretched joint in the foot chooses to crack! *Patak—patak*, the toes make a sound. More dangerous still, something seems to block the throat and one suddenly feels the need to clear it. These obstacles did not spare Naina either.

He sat back in the chair, cracked his toe joints thoroughly, cleared his throat, drank a cup of cold beverage, wiped the body that was sweating despite the cold, and slowly began moving toward Anandi’s bed.

Of all moments, why did the clock that had been quiet until then choose that instant to strike? And not one or two strokes—twelve!

Naina, frightened, returned and sat in his old place. Having thundered twelve times, the clock hung on the wall competing with the sound of Naina’s heart. He himself hung between love and fear, struggling. Then love won. Fear moved a little aside.

Again he set out to see Anandi. This time he moved his feet a little faster and reached the side of her bed. In the dim green light, Naina Muhammad stood absorbed, gazing at her face.

Peaceful sleep.

He decided that even painters could not imagine such beauty. Her eyes were closed—yet how much enchantment remained in that face! In white marble statues shaped by a sculptor, no colour is painted into the dark pupils, yet not an atom of their beauty is lost. In the same way, Naina saw beauty in Anandi’s eyes even though their dark pupils were hidden.

Nor did he imagine those eyelids in any ordinary way. He described them in his mind as the cover of an epic. He compared them to the petals of a flower closed over pollen within. If only he himself could be a protection for her like those eyelids......

Naina’s heart pounded. Afraid that the sound might reach her ears and wake her, he pressed a hand against his chest. He could not bring himself to take his eyes from her face. He stood motionless, intoxicated by the sight.

In that intoxication he did not notice a dark figure standing by the window. There was a pistol in that figure’s hand. The figure and the gun it held fell as shadows upon the window curtain. With Naina’s eyes fixed on the beauty of Anandi’s sleep, how could he think of turning toward the window? The shadow clearly showed the figure checking the handgun.
