## 15

<!-- source: scan 120; printed page 119; post-centered 15 -->

The police could not discover the direction in which Tiger had disappeared. Leaving the side he had actually taken, they began searching other areas. Tiger slipped into a lane, entered a sewage channel and emerged into another street. He decided that this was the right route to meet the running woman and cut her off from the front.

Just as he expected, she came toward him.

"Chintamani! Stop!!"

She started at the voice and immediately understood that it belonged to the sinner who had brought her to this state. Fury shook her. Yet she stopped where she was. *Let him come near!* defiance surged within her. Her eyes rolled as though to say, *I will make him suffer.* Tiger walked slowly toward her.

<!-- source: scan 121; printed page 120 -->

"Come, deceiver! Come! Why have you come? Why did you ruin my life like this?" she cried.

"There is no time to talk about all that now. Danger is waiting. Come—come with me!" Tiger said, seizing her hand and dragging her along at a run.

Chintamani ran behind him a short distance in that rush, gathered all her anger and struck him across the cheek with a sharp *Palar!*

"Tell me why! Why did you make people imagine that I was stained? You were the sinner—the source of that wicked act!" she screamed.

Tiger released his grip and pressed a hand to his cheek. Chintamani seized the chance and ran. Tiger followed, but he could not run as fast. His cheek began swelling like an appalam dropped into hot oil.

He ran on through the pain. Thinking that to be caught in his hands would be like being caught in a spider's web, Chintamani ran without knowing the direction. She saw lights ahead and looked back. Tiger was visible far behind her. She ran faster toward the lights.

It was the Palaiyur railway station. A train stood ready to depart. The third bell had rung. The whistle tore through the silence of the night.

Chintamani asked where the train was going. Bangalore, came the answer. In an instant many thoughts flashed through her mind.

*Why not go directly to the Bangalore groom's house and prove my innocence to them? Yes—that is right!*

With that decision she ran toward the train. Every carriage was packed. She could not get into any of them. After great difficulty she finally climbed into the separate women's compartment, the only one with slightly fewer passengers.

The train started. From inside, Chintamani leaned out to see whether Tiger was coming. He was nowhere within her sight. But without her knowing it, as the train moved Tiger ran up and clung to the last carriage. He decided that Chintamani must be going to Bangalore to explain that she was pure; therefore he would create no disturbance on the way, but would stop her once they reached Bangalore.

All the women in Chintamani's compartment had sunk into exhausted sleep. Only one remained half awake; even her eyes were only half open. She looked like a woman from a wealthy household, but there was an unusually strong masculine cast to her face.

Around her neck were two strands of chain. Diamonds at her ears; bangles only to the elbow. She had the appearance of a rich woman. Yet her face lacked the glow of femininity. Her lips were thick, her eyes somewhat red. She wore a bright red sari, ornate shoes, and a blouse whose sleeves stopped at the shoulders. Unable to sleep properly, she shifted restlessly.

Again and again she looked through the window, then glanced beneath the bench on which she sat, then closed her eyes for a little while. Chintamani, beside her—

*[The source resumes here with the incomplete Tamil phrase `கார்ந்து கொண்டாள்.`; no missing element is supplied in English.]*

The wealthy woman stared at Chintamani. The gaze produced fear and disgust in her. When the woman closed her eyes as though asleep, Chintamani examined her more carefully and felt still more uneasy. An unscented blue kanakambaram flower sat in her hair; some perfume with the biting sharpness of chilli had been smeared on her body. Sitting without restraint or modest reserve, that "jewel among women" seemed a riddle to Chintamani.

Whenever the train stopped at a station, the "mystery woman" immediately rose and stood by the door. She leaned her head outside with expectation on her face, as if waiting for someone. When the train began moving again, she returned to her seat. Chintamani found her an unsolvable puzzle.

After a while Chintamani forgot the ostentatious woman beside her. She had a far greater knot from which she herself had to escape. Whenever she thought of it, her heart burned like kerosene catching fire.

"Prostitute!"

She had become the target of that ear-splitting word. How many innocent people are punished for crimes they never committed—life imprisonment, the gallows, transportation beyond the seas! Yet more cruel than all those punishments was to force a woman to bear the title of prostitute and pour molten lead into a mother's heart. Cruel—terribly cruel!

To end that cruelty, Chintamani was travelling toward Bangalore as lawyer and witness in her own case.

At one station the train stopped. As usual, the "kanakambaram" woman stood by the door and watched.

<!-- source: scan 124; printed page 123 -->

Chintamani too leaned her head outside to see which station it was. The nameboard was too far away to read. Her eyes swept across the platform and suddenly widened.

Near her compartment stood an elderly woman and a young man. Another man held the old woman's hand and was helping her into the nearby first-class carriage. At that moment a wealthy gentleman passing by asked, "What, Azhagappan? What brings you this far?"

How Chintamani's heart must have surged!

Yes—it was Azhagappan himself. Azhagappan, who had refused to marry her, branded her a prostitute and was returning home. She listened intently.

"Mother and I went to a village and are returning to Bangalore. The car broke down on the way, so I left the driver here to repair it and bring it later. Mother and I are taking the train."

The wealthy gentleman said, "I too am going to Bangalore," and boarded with Azhagappan. The driver took leave of them.

"Bring the car carefully!" Azhagappan called just as the train stirred and began to move.

Chintamani could no longer sit calmly. Until then she had endured both the antics of the youth near her and the penetrating smell of the woman's "perfume." Now her heart pounded. The world seemed to turn out of place. She felt as though the train were climbing into the sky, or had left the rails, struck a bridge and shattered into a hundred pieces. Her body grew hot as though fever had seized it; her hands and legs trembled beyond control.

<!-- source join: scan 124 ends `புகை`; scan 125 begins `வண்டி` -->
<!-- source: scan 125; printed page 124 -->

She was travelling to seek out the man who had judged her an "abyss of disgrace" and argue that she was innocent. Not only had he appeared on the way—he was in the very next carriage.

*Why not get down at the next station and meet Azhagappan and his mother?* The question pierced her.

"Tch! Is this something to discuss on a train? And there is a wealthy gentleman with him. No, no. I will speak in Bangalore. When they leave the train, I will follow them."

She satisfied herself with that decision. The pitiable young woman still believed that she could provide proof that she was unstained and win Azhagappan as her companion. Poor, guileless young heart! A sparrow that did not even wish to touch the frauds of the world!

Friday had only to dawn and it became an hour of destruction for her. The sufferings she had endured since morning—Appappa! Would some deliverance at least be born before Saturday arrived?

In the next carriage was the handsome young man who should have become her husband.

In the last carriage was the villain longing to crush her into a withered flower—and he, in turn, feared the police.

<!-- source: scan 126; printed page 125 -->

She feared that *he* might appear.

The train went on, carrying travellers burdened with many different thoughts.

At the next station it stopped. The woman beside Chintamani rose and went to the door. Four or five policemen suddenly climbed into the women's compartment. Why had they entered so abruptly? The carriage was searched.

From beneath the bench where Chintamani sat, the police pulled out a square wooden box. They opened and examined it. The box was packed with opium.

"Whose box is this?" the police demanded.

Many women had already left at stations along the route. Now only Chintamani, the wealthy-looking woman and a few others remained.

With a gesture, the wealthy woman indicated Chintamani.

The police threatened and questioned her. Chintamani said she knew nothing. What was "opium"? How could that innocent young woman know about carrying it away, selling it on the black market, or sending it abroad?

Of those in the carriage, Chintamani's appearance itself struck the police as suspicious. Added to that was the ostentatious woman's indication.

"You are under arrest," the police told Chintamani.

She could not believe her own ears.
