# Sarapallam Samundi

“Sarapallam Samundi” is only a fictional story! But it is a picture drawn on a foundation of history.

A.D. 1005! Rajaraja I is engaged in an effort to build a great temple in Tanjavur called “Rajarajeswaram.” That temple is not merely a work of religious devotion; it is a memorial to the victories he won! An example of the passion for art that burned brightly within him!

At a time when the world had concluded that the Chola Empire, which had risen high in the age of Karikala Peruvalattan, had fallen, Vijayalaya Chola stood in Tanjavur holding aloft the tiger flag in Chola land. The glory of bringing many countries under his sceptre and making them bow to that flag belongs to Rajaraja I, son of Parantaka II.

On the twenty-fifth day of June, A.D. 985, Rajaraja ascended the throne. Because he was crowned after gaining mature political experience, history describes his reign as a “golden age.” The Chola Empire that he created and established continued to flourish with greatness for two centuries. For four or five years after he came to the throne, no sound of war was heard. No sound was heard—but plans were being laid with great intensity.

With the intention of bringing the petty kings surrounding Chola country, and other rulers as well, under the Chola Empire,<!-- source: scan 25; printed page: 21 --> Rajaraja set to work. Reflecting that the surrounding kings could at any time bring danger to Chola country, and wishing to prevent it in advance, he spent all his strength on increasing the country's military power, strengthening its defences, and ensuring that the people of the land could enjoy a prosperous life at all times. His son Rajendra was a tiger. He was a great warrior capable of heaping up the fruits of victory wherever he turned. Rajendra's first wife was named Panchavan Madevi; perhaps because of the love he bore his wife, he gave himself the byname Panchavan Marayan! “Marayam” is an honour conferred by a king upon a military commander. Until he was crowned, Rajendra served as commander of the Chola country's army and devoted himself single-mindedly to expanding the extent of the Chola Empire. Like his father, Rajendra too had a passion for art. His wife Panchavan Madevi became the generous giver who poured that wealth of art upon him.

The abilities of father and son, and the deep love that had blossomed between them, also helped turn Chola country into a fortress of steel.

If Rajaraja had been a dull-witted man content to lie there thinking, “There is a jewelled crown on my head; chief ministers are beside me; there is a palace; there is a couch; I bear the name of king—this alone is enough for me to enjoy pleasure in this world,” his frontiers might have been lost. He might have paved the way for Chola country to shrink and fall into ruin. But he was a learned great man who foresaw the future, a statesman who understood the minds of his enemies. That is why he spent four or five years strengthening the defences and enlarging the army. After that he took stock of his enemies. He began to act after considering far-reaching plans: whom to destroy by force of arms—whom to cultivate as a friend—whom not to allow to grow, but instead compel to submit to Chola country. Then the tiger-bannered armies set out. The heroic sword of Rajendra, called Panchavan Marayan, who had assumed the post of great military commander, began to whirl. A thousand flashes of lightning,<!-- source: scan 26; printed page: 22 --> burst forth as though they were a crore. They cast the radiance of Rajaraja's victory.

With elephant troops, cavalry and infantry, together with a navy to encircle his opponents, the war cry of the great king Rajaraja roared like thunder. In A.D. 989, the Chola army entered Chera country. A Pandyan named Amarabusangan, who stood allied with the Chera, also opposed the Cholas. In the battle fought at the port town called Kandalur Salai, the Chola army destroyed all the Chera ships and won victory. Having conquered Chera country, Rajaraja caused his own birthday, the Sadhayam day of the month of Aippasi, to be celebrated there. From there the Chola army set out in pursuit of the Pandyan Amarabusangan. The port called Vizhinjam was captured by Rajendra. The war that flared between these two countries lasted for nearly four years.

Then Tanjavur set out to exact vengeance on the king of Kudamalai. When Rajaraja, king of the Cholas, heard that an envoy sent from Tanjavur to that country had been killed, he set out with an army. Crossing more than eighteen great forests, he destroyed the powerful city of Udagai, the capital of Kudamalai country, and wore the garland of victory. Learning through Rajendra that Meenisa, king of Kudamalai, had fought with great valour on the battlefield, Rajaraja granted Meenisa the city called Malavi as a gift.

A great dispute had arisen in the Eastern Chalukya country over who possessed the right to the throne. A man named Saktivarman and his lineage had been driven out of the country. Rajaraja supported Saktivarman, defeated his enemies, and made Saktivarman king of the Eastern Chalukya country. Chola country strengthened its friendship with the Eastern Chalukya realm by giving Rajaraja's daughter, the younger Kundavai, in marriage to Vimaladittan, Saktivarman's younger brother!

Later, Rajaraja wore the garland of victory in the fierce war fought in Kalinga. He marched against Gangapadi, captured its capital Talakkadu, and renamed it Rajarajapuram. At the same speed, Nolambapadi fell.<!-- source join: scan 26 / printed page 22 ends with the first part of Tamil word வீழ்ச்சி; scan 27 / printed page 23 begins யுற்றது.; translated jointly as Nolambapadi fell. -->

<!-- source: scan 27; printed page: 23 -->

Tadigaipadi too bowed before Rajaraja. Next came the invasion of Kollam; war against Kodungolur! Subduing both countries, Rajaraja earned the title “Kirthiparakrama Chola.” Determined to subdue all the kings along the western coast, Rajendra marched with an army, captured the Tuluvas and Konganas, defeated the Cheras and the Telugus, and brought an incomparable victory to Chola land.

As a sign of Rajaraja's victory in the Eelam campaign, he named northern Lanka “Mummudi Chola Mandalam.” In this war, the power of Anuradhapura was destroyed. Rajaraja established a place called “Polonnaruwa” as the Chola capital in Lanka.

“Rama built a bridge with monkeys in order to go to Lanka. But Rajaraja, without building a bridge, carried his armies across in ships and killed the king of Lanka. Therefore he is greater than Rama,” declares the Thiruvalangadu charter.

In Rattapadi, a region of the Western Chalukyas, Rajaraja fought fiercely against a powerful king named Satyasrayan. Satyasrayan turned his back and went into hiding, but he did not lose his country. Through the Rattapadi war Rajaraja acquired a great quantity of gold and wealth. It was with that boundless wealth that he began to build the great temple of Tanjavur. Having also captured Munnir Palantivu Pannirayiram and won the garland of fame called “Mummudi Chola,” Rajaraja's victory pillar stands there in the form of the art-temple called Tanjavur Rajarajeswaram!

Just like Rajaraja's incomparable heroic form, that temple too stands erect, proclaiming to the world the ancient Tamil great warrior's martial panegyric, the roar of art, and the great radiance of statecraft.

How many, how many plans Rajaraja drew up to raise that temple! Were all his intentions fulfilled? Was everything he wished to see in that temple of art completed? No! Not all his intentions were fulfilled! Why were they not fulfilled?
