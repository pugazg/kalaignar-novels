At dawn it would be Deepavali—throughout the night, fireworks filled the town. Street competed with street, house with house, as people set off fireworks and rejoiced. Sparklers showed many colours, like the chameleon hearts of opportunists. Like the protest announcements of certain political leaders, “out” fireworks shot up into the sky. Chinese crackers—needle crackers—displayed their prowess like meaningless alliterative dialogue written in the fashion of “aalukku aalu—kaanukku kaanu.” Boys and girls ran and played, delight glowing on their faces at the sight of new clothes. Couples who had come for their first Deepavali after marriage passed the time merrily amid courtesies and teasing torments.

On that night, while the whole town was immersed in joy, Narayani alone sat in her house like a shadow of sorrow. Her thought was not: “Deepavali is not a festival of the Tamils; it is an Aryan festival that celebrates the day a Tamil died. It is story-spinning that does not stand to reason. To celebrate it as our own festival and make merry is blind doctrine. Therefore one must not take part in it!” Narayani had not attained that degree of intellectual development. She too was one among the women who competed with one another to plunge into the pool of mythology and emerge from it. Even so, she did not celebrate Deepavali.

<!-- source: scan 76; printed page: 74 -->

With eyes that seemed to stretch toward her ears and a figure fashioned to captivate anyone who saw her, Narayani sat at the front of her house, heaving sighs as she watched the townspeople take part in the Deepavali festivities.

Along the street came a beautiful chariot in procession, decorated with images of Krishna and Satyabhama. Is not the myth that when the “hero” Krishna was defeated by Narakasura and fell unconscious, Bama destroyed the asura with her arrows? When the Supreme Being himself—an incarnation of Vishnu, bearer of conch and discus—had fallen, it would indeed seem wondrous that his Devi extinguished the life of that lion among asuras. Whatever the *ekkana visinulo*; whatever the *enna vakkana pesinulo*; somehow the devotees were satisfied that, through the Goddess's sacrifice, the enemy of the devas had been destroyed.

Those multitudes of devotees celebrated Parandaman's victory, placed him and his consort Bama upon the chariot, and took them around the streets. Even the playful children who knew nothing of the subtle secret of that victory shouted “Jai!” for the Supreme Being. The town's notables joined the procession. Nayanam musicians poured out their skill. Women from every house performed arati for Kannan of Ayarpadi and rejoiced. They danced and sang.

But Narayani neither danced nor performed arati; she simply sat watching the ceremonies unfold. The procession of the Supreme Being passed beyond her house. The devotees expected someone from that house to come out and make an offering to the Lord. No one came.

For a while Narayani kept looking in the direction the chariot had gone. The slaying of Narakasura had been depicted on it. She had watched that scene without blinking. A spear had pierced the chest of Narakasura, whose shoulders were like a wrestler's. He stood with his mouth open like a cavern. Before him—Krishna, in a faint! Bama—in the pose of thrusting the spear! That was the Deepavali scene depicted on the chariot. The townspeople lost themselves in that festival, a festival that dammed up reason and raised a great wall against ethnic consciousness. The chariot disappeared from Narayani's sight. The sound of fireworks kept piercing her<!-- source: scan 77; printed page: 75 --> ears. The scene of Narakasura's slaying that she had seen on the chariot did not leave her heart. Memories surged like waves.

Narayani's house was on Nadutheru—the Middle Street. There is no need for anyone to wonder how the municipality allowed a house to be built in the middle of a street, or why the municipal authorities felt such sympathy for Narayani. The street on which her house stood was itself named Nadutheru.

Narayani had been born on Nadutheru, into a middle-class family, among four brothers. Her parents took their leave of life, leaving all five children behind. The four brothers went to Mayuram to take the Tula bath in the Kaveri and never returned. The youngest was caught in a whirlpool; one after another, the other three jumped in to save him, and Narayani received only the news that all of them had gone straight to the “realm of moksha.”

When this burden of grief fell upon her head, she was a young vine in the bloom of youth! A mind-captivating form, as though a sculptor's craftsmanship itself had come alive! Her brows—her brows—everything about her was fashioned to subdue the pride of men. A statue of beauty! A wondrous maiden! A flower of honey! A grapevine! Yet that fragrant rose, without a fence—without anyone to protect it—was withering all alone. “After swallowing up my father, mother, elder brothers and younger brother, why have you left this wretched woman alone with this accursed life?” she had wept many times in the presence of the Lord.

It was on those visits to the temple to weep that she encountered the priest Krishnayyar. Though he was a temple priest, he carried himself with a smart, stylish air. Around his neck was a handsome gold chain; at its end, a rudraksha bead; at his waist, a peacock-eye-patterned veshti; above it, a white silk cloth; an American crop combined with a B.A. kudumi—these were the distinguishing marks of the temple priest Krishnayyar! I forgot; forgive me: on his chest there was also a sacred thread that went to the wash! There was a smear of vibhuti, sacred ash; and on days especially sacred to Vishnu he would wear a namam too! Not as a broad stripe; neatly; as a small line; without spoiling his looks!

His age? Not over thirty. Was this single piece of capital not enough to start the business of love?

When men of fifty or sixty roam about searching for maidens with whom to conduct the commerce of love—rubbing dye into greying hair, smiling through false teeth, and saying that even a loss would not matter—would thirty-year-old Krishnayyar alone remain idle? And when it comes flying in *puru valuvile*, circling around, need one even ask?

Every day Narayani shed tears in the presence of the Lord. Krishnayyar gave her vibhuti prasadam. For many days this went on in silence. Krishnayyar began to cast his gaze down the road, wondering when evening would come, and with it that peahen. Narayani before God. Why did she have to weep day after day? That question burrowed into the priest's heart. He decided to ask her directly. But it was precisely at the hour she came that those wretched multitudes of devotees customarily came to the temple too. He never got a chance to speak to her alone.

One day—a Monday—the abhisheka and aradhana rites at the temple were in full swing. A fine crowd. Concerts as well. It was a festival being held because one of the town's leading men had joined the Congress. In the empty pavilion opposite the temple, they slaughtered a goat in sacrifice, set out pots of liquor as an offering, and thus did honour to Gandhiji's principles. Along with that, a grand ubayam was conducted at the great temple too.

As usual, Narayani came that day. The waiting Iyer rejoiced as though the jasmine in bloom had arrived. The devotees had turned their attention to the Bharatanatyam performance and the music concert. Narayani entered the sanctum—folded her hands like a lotus bud—closed the eyes that could conquer Kama. A flood of tears burst through the dam of her eyelids and split into streaks over the rise of her cheeks. Lost to herself in deep devotion, she stood there. “Show me a way, Father! Why<!-- source: scan 79; printed page: 77 --> have you left only me alive in this world? In a large family I suffer like a lone tree; is this fair to you?” she cried.

The prayer ended. Iyer came to give her prasadam. She took the vibhuti and applied it to her forehead. She turned away.

Clearing his parched throat, Iyer tried to say something. His tongue would not rise. With difficulty, he finally forced himself to speak. Trembling, he asked, “Why do you shed tears like this every day, child?”

The question jolted Narayani. She was, after all, a young woman in the bloom of youth. For her even to leave the house was considered wrong; no one would find fault if she went to the temple. But to speak there with a man—was that something society would accept? She had no time to think through all this and decide whether to answer. Trembling too, she replied, “It is all my fate!”

“Narayani! I know very well what trouble you have. Those who bore you, those born with you—they are all gone! You're left all alone, suffering!” the priest began, laying his preamble.

Narayani's weeping grew worse. “I'm leaving!” she said, and left the sanctum.

After having darshan of the Lord, Narayani's custom was to circle the temple prakaram before going home. As usual, she was making her round of the prakaram that day. There, in the prakaram, she saw Krishnayyar coming toward her while circling in *apradakshina*, the counter-clockwise direction. Lowering her head in modesty, she tried to pass him. Krishnayyar looked all around. No one was coming along that side of the prakaram. Everyone was gathered in the concert pavilions. “Narayani, stop!” he said softly. Narayani's feet would not move. She had no choice but to stand there. Krishnayyar hurried up before her. He fell in full prostration, his head almost striking her feet.

Narayani understood nothing. “Swami! Swami!!” she cried.

“Narayani! Save this humble servant! Even before the image of the Goddess, I have never prostrated myself like this.
