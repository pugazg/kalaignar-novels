# Nadutheru Narayani

At dawn it would be Deepavali—throughout the night, fireworks filled the town. Street competed with street, house with house, as people set off fireworks and rejoiced. Sparklers showed many colours, like the chameleon hearts of opportunists. Like the protest announcements of certain political leaders, “out” fireworks shot up into the sky. Chinese crackers—needle crackers—displayed their prowess like meaningless alliterative dialogue written in the fashion of “aalukku aalu—kaanukku kaanu.” Boys and girls ran and played, delight glowing on their faces at the sight of new clothes. Couples who had come for their first Deepavali after marriage passed the time merrily amid courtesies and teasing torments.

On that night, while the whole town was immersed in joy, Narayani alone sat in her house like a shadow of sorrow. Her thought was not: “Deepavali is not a festival of the Tamils; it is an Aryan festival that celebrates the day a Tamil died. It is story-spinning that does not stand to reason. To celebrate it as our own festival and make merry is blind doctrine. Therefore one must not take part in it!” Narayani had not attained that degree of intellectual development. She too was one among the women who competed with one another to plunge into the pool of mythology and emerge from it. Even so, she did not celebrate Deepavali.

<!-- source: scan 76; printed page: 74 -->

With eyes that seemed to stretch toward her ears and a figure fashioned to captivate anyone who saw her, Narayani sat at the front of her house, heaving sighs as she watched the townspeople take part in the Deepavali festivities.

Along the street came a beautiful chariot in procession, decorated with images of Krishna and Satyabhama. Is not the myth that when the “hero” Krishna was defeated by Narakasura and fell unconscious, Bama destroyed the asura with her arrows? When the Supreme Being himself—an incarnation of Vishnu, bearer of conch and discus—had fallen, it would indeed seem wondrous that his Devi extinguished the life of that lion among asuras. Whatever the *ekkana visinulo*; whatever the *enna vakkana pesinulo*; somehow the devotees were satisfied that, through the Goddess's sacrifice, the enemy of the devas had been destroyed.

Those multitudes of devotees celebrated Parandaman's victory, placed him and his consort Bama upon the chariot, and took them around the streets. Even the playful children who knew nothing of the subtle secret of that victory shouted “Jai!” for the Supreme Being. The town's notables joined the procession. Nayanam musicians poured out their skill. Women from every house performed arati for Kannan of Ayarpadi and rejoiced. They danced and sang.

But Narayani neither danced nor performed arati; she simply sat watching the ceremonies unfold. The procession of the Supreme Being passed beyond her house. The devotees expected someone from that house to come out and make an offering to the Lord. No one came.

For a while Narayani kept looking in the direction the chariot had gone. The slaying of Narakasura had been depicted on it. She had watched that scene without blinking. A spear had pierced the chest of Narakasura, whose shoulders were like a wrestler's. He stood with his mouth open like a cavern. Before him—Krishna, in a faint! Bama—in the pose of thrusting the spear! That was the Deepavali scene depicted on the chariot. The townspeople lost themselves in that festival, a festival that dammed up reason and raised a great wall against ethnic consciousness. The chariot disappeared from Narayani's sight. The sound of fireworks kept piercing her<!-- source: scan 77; printed page: 75 --> ears. The scene of Narakasura's slaying that she had seen on the chariot did not leave her heart. Memories surged like waves.

Narayani's house was on Nadutheru—the Middle Street. There is no need for anyone to wonder how the municipality allowed a house to be built in the middle of a street, or why the municipal authorities felt such sympathy for Narayani. The street on which her house stood was itself named Nadutheru.

Narayani had been born on Nadutheru, into a middle-class family, among four brothers. Her parents took their leave of life, leaving all five children behind. The four brothers went to Mayuram to take the Tula bath in the Kaveri and never returned. The youngest was caught in a whirlpool; one after another, the other three jumped in to save him, and Narayani received only the news that all of them had gone straight to the “realm of moksha.”

When this burden of grief fell upon her head, she was a young vine in the bloom of youth! A mind-captivating form, as though a sculptor's craftsmanship itself had come alive! Her brows—her brows—everything about her was fashioned to subdue the pride of men. A statue of beauty! A wondrous maiden! A flower of honey! A grapevine! Yet that fragrant rose, without a fence—without anyone to protect it—was withering all alone. “After swallowing up my father, mother, elder brothers and younger brother, why have you left this wretched woman alone with this accursed life?” she had wept many times in the presence of the Lord.

It was on those visits to the temple to weep that she encountered the priest Krishnayyar. Though he was a temple priest, he carried himself with a smart, stylish air. Around his neck was a handsome gold chain; at its end, a rudraksha bead; at his waist, a peacock-eye-patterned veshti; above it, a white silk cloth; an American crop combined with a B.A. kudumi—these were the distinguishing marks of the temple priest Krishnayyar! I forgot; forgive me: on his chest there was also a sacred thread that went to the wash! There was a smear of vibhuti, sacred ash; and on days especially sacred to Vishnu he would wear a namam too! Not as a broad stripe; neatly; as a small line; without spoiling his looks!

<!-- source: scan 78; printed page: 76 -->

His age? Not over thirty. Was this single piece of capital not enough to start the business of love?

When men of fifty or sixty roam about searching for maidens with whom to conduct the commerce of love—rubbing dye into greying hair, smiling through false teeth, and saying that even a loss would not matter—would thirty-year-old Krishnayyar alone remain idle? And when it comes flying in *puru valuvile*, circling around, need one even ask?

Every day Narayani shed tears in the presence of the Lord. Krishnayyar gave her vibhuti prasadam. For many days this went on in silence. Krishnayyar began to cast his gaze down the road, wondering when evening would come, and with it that peahen. Narayani before God. Why did she have to weep day after day? That question burrowed into the priest's heart. He decided to ask her directly. But it was precisely at the hour she came that those wretched multitudes of devotees customarily came to the temple too. He never got a chance to speak to her alone.

One day—a Monday—the abhisheka and aradhana rites at the temple were in full swing. A fine crowd. Concerts as well. It was a festival being held because one of the town's leading men had joined the Congress. In the empty pavilion opposite the temple, they slaughtered a goat in sacrifice, set out pots of liquor as an offering, and thus did honour to Gandhiji's principles. Along with that, a grand ubayam was conducted at the great temple too.

As usual, Narayani came that day. The waiting Iyer rejoiced as though the jasmine in bloom had arrived. The devotees had turned their attention to the Bharatanatyam performance and the music concert. Narayani entered the sanctum—folded her hands like a lotus bud—closed the eyes that could conquer Kama. A flood of tears burst through the dam of her eyelids and split into streaks over the rise of her cheeks. Lost to herself in deep devotion, she stood there. “Show me a way, Father! Why<!-- source: scan 79; printed page: 77 --> have you left only me alive in this world? In a large family I suffer like a lone tree; is this fair to you?” she cried.

The prayer ended. Iyer came to give her prasadam. She took the vibhuti and applied it to her forehead. She turned away.

Clearing his parched throat, Iyer tried to say something. His tongue would not rise. With difficulty, he finally forced himself to speak. Trembling, he asked, “Why do you shed tears like this every day, child?”

The question jolted Narayani. She was, after all, a young woman in the bloom of youth. For her even to leave the house was considered wrong; no one would find fault if she went to the temple. But to speak there with a man—was that something society would accept? She had no time to think through all this and decide whether to answer. Trembling too, she replied, “It is all my fate!”

“Narayani! I know very well what trouble you have. Those who bore you, those born with you—they are all gone! You're left all alone, suffering!” the priest began, laying his preamble.

Narayani's weeping grew worse. “I'm leaving!” she said, and left the sanctum.

After having darshan of the Lord, Narayani's custom was to circle the temple prakaram before going home. As usual, she was making her round of the prakaram that day. There, in the prakaram, she saw Krishnayyar coming toward her while circling in *apradakshina*, the counter-clockwise direction. Lowering her head in modesty, she tried to pass him. Krishnayyar looked all around. No one was coming along that side of the prakaram. Everyone was gathered in the concert pavilions. “Narayani, stop!” he said softly. Narayani's feet would not move. She had no choice but to stand there. Krishnayyar hurried up before her. He fell in full prostration, his head almost striking her feet.

Narayani understood nothing. “Swami! Swami!!” she cried.

“Narayani! Save this humble servant! Even before the image of the Goddess, I have never prostrated myself like this.

“Swami! You belong to the divine caste. I am...” she trailed off.

“Great men who have renounced everything know only one caste! I belong to the male caste—you to the female caste. Come, let us both swim in the pool of love,” Iyer said, taking her hand.

“Oh, Swami! The Lord's temple...” Narayani warned.

<!-- source: scan 81; printed page: 80 -->

“Isn't the Lord himself one who keeps one beside him and another upon his head—don't you know that?” the priest joked, gripping her waist.

“Oh no, none of this is right—I don't like it!” She opened her mouth, intending to brush him off and get away, but for some reason what came out was, “Someone might see us!” She moved away shyly, twisting aside. Wasn't that sentence enough for Iyer!

“All the boys are watching the Bharatanatyam. Not one of them will come here; don't be afraid,” Iyer said, reaching out and catching hold of her. Her cheeks reddened. She surrendered herself into his hands. Suddenly realization dawned on Narayani. Clutching Iyer's feet tightly, she cried out with a wail, “Swami, save me! You alone are my support!”

Iyer swept her up, embraced her, reassured her, and sent her home. She too set out for home believing that the Lord had bestowed his grace upon her. And Iyer, immediately after tasting that goddess's cheek and without even washing, hurried toward the sanctum to chant the archana mantra to the Lord.

As promised in the temple prakaram, Iyer came all the way to Narayani's house so that he could keep and protect her always.

“The temple priest has hit the jackpot, man! She was like a guava fruit—Iyer plucked her. Whatever else, that caste is born to enjoy! So many men had been pining after her; after kicking them all aside, that spotted deer got caught in the *poonāl* net! Let him go—let him at least taste her!” Among the young bulls, talk that began in the form of envy ended in a spirit of yielding.

Narayani decided to accept the temple priest as her husband because she believed that a man who performed his duties by God's side would be a very good man; that he would not abandon one who trusted him; that, moreover, he was a Brahmin; that he would not go back on his word; that he would consider lying a sin; and that he would keep her beside him and protect her as Parvati stands beside Parama Siva. In the temple prakaram, in a place darkened by the shadow of the *kaappuk-graham*<!-- source: scan 82; printed page: 81; join: scan81 ends காப்புக் / scan82 begins கிரகத்தின் -->, they exchanged kisses. After returning home, she spent the whole night awake, thinking, and came to the decision that she would make the priest her husband.

“Why, Nara, are you worried? You're only thinking, ‘I'm a Brahmin and you're a Sudra woman,’ aren't you? There is a leader of the Brahmins—our Rajaji. Didn't he give his daughter to Gandhi's son? If caste custom mattered, what happened to it there? A Paraiyan called Ambedkar—they call him an Adi Dravidar—do you know who has married him? A woman of our caste! Where did caste custom go? Do you know the Rukmini Arundale affair? Silly girl—madcap! Listen to what I say! Don't be afraid! I'm going to marry you,” Krishnayyar delivered as a whole sermon.

“Will all the people of your caste come to the wedding?” Narayani asked.

“They don't have to—I am not going to tell my caste people at all. You just have to come with me to the temple this evening. Look, I've bought the mangalyam and kept it ready! I will tie it around your neck right before the Lord. We don't need human witnesses! The gods are witness enough—the Lord is witness enough! What do you say?” the priest asked coaxingly.

Hearing that the priest was going to tie the tali around her neck in the very presence of Mahesan, Narayani was delighted. The tali gleamed around her neck. Around the town people said that Krishnayyar was keeping Narayani as his *vaippu*—a kept woman. When people said so to him, he too said, “Yes.” The talk reached Narayani's ears as well. She complained to him: “They say I am your ‘vaippu’!”

“Silly girl! Why get angry at ‘vaippu’? They don't know how to say the English word ‘wife,’ so they say ‘vaip’! That's all!” Krishnayyar tossed off the joke.

Narayani, who believed that the husband himself was God, did not make much more of such talk and continued to live amicably with him.

<!-- source: scan 83; printed page: 82 -->

Then suddenly one day Narayani saw sorrow spread across Iyer's face when he came home. She asked why. Iyer said that the temple trustee (dharmakarta), Narasimha Naidu, had removed him from temple service.

“Why?” Narayani cried in alarm.

“All the Brahmins in the town apparently held a conference. They condemned me for having made an inter-caste marriage and living with you. They pressed the trustee, saying that a man living with a Sudra woman must not touch the deity in the temple and perform puja. So the trustee removed me,” Iyer said.

Hearing such revolutionary news, Narayani clung to Krishnayyar and cried, “O Brahmin sir! For the sake of this embrace, not only have you stood against all your customs and observances, you have treated society itself as nothing and even sacrificed your temple job!”

“Narayani! Don't keep crying like a little child! I've done so much for you. In the same way, you must be ready to do anything for me too. That's what I need!” Iyer said, running his fingers through her hair.

“Swami! For your sake I am ready to give away all three—body, possessions and soul,” said that beautiful-eyed woman, leaning into his lap and sobbing in broken breaths.

Looking at the pair “Krishnayyar—Narayani,” one would feel compelled to say, “Alas, this man is hardly a match for this young woman!” Some remarked, “She is like a parrot—he is like a rat.” There were none who did not say, “She is the birthplace of great beauty—beside her, this Brahmin looks like a firefly before a star.” But Narayani's eyes were an exception to all such judgments. Before her mind's eye, everyone else's criticism crumbled into dust.

He had lost the prestige of his caste—lost the respect attached to being a temple priest—all for her! To fulfil the promise he had given her! When Narayani thought of it that way, Krishnayyar's form before her seemed just like the form of Lord Krishna. “My dear, jewel-hued one!”<!-- source: scan 84; printed page: 83 -->—that was about the only thing she had not yet sung. Such devotion and affection for her husband had taken hold of her.

Nalayini had carried her leprous husband to a courtesan's house. Anasuya, setting out with the challenge, “If the women of the heavenly world are not paragons of chastity, should paragons of chastity exist only on earth? Let us test them,” turned the Trimurti—who came naked and demanded that she serve them food—into children and fed them while herself in a state of nakedness. Narayani wished to conduct herself as a wife who never went against her husband's word, enough to surpass all such celebrated chaste wives.

Two days after he had been removed from temple service, Krishnayyar came running home in a great hurry one night. Beads of sweat covered his body. Gasping for breath, he cried, “Narayani! Narayani!”

“What? What?” she asked, running from the bedchamber with her slender waist swaying.

Iyer stood there as though without his senses and babbled.

“Narayani! How much have I sacrificed for you?”

“Yes; what of it now?”

“Do you remember? You said you would sacrifice all three—body, possessions and soul—for me!”

“Yes, I said it—and I say it even now!”

“Narayani, danger has come upon me! You don't need to sacrifice your soul or your possessions. Sacrifice only your body—that's enough!”

“What are you saying, Swami?”

“Yes, my dear! If you want to save my life, you must sacrifice your body!”

“I don't understand!......”

“You know the temple trustee Narasimha Naidu; to him, your body...”

Iyer had not even finished speaking when Narayani fainted and collapsed.

When Narayani regained consciousness, she realized that she had been laid on the cot. Her gaze moved in disgust toward the hands that were gently stroking her forehead. As her gaze moved that way,<!-- source: scan 85; printed page: 84 --> her eyes opened wide. The man sitting beside her, leaning toward her, was not Krishnayyar.

He was a man more handsome than Krishnayyar. A young man with a well-built body. His curled young moustache made the dignity of his face stand out all the more. His broad chest and the silk shirt covering it were fashioned to catch the viewer's eye. On his forehead was a light namam—only a red line! He sat staring intently at Narayani. On seeing him, Narayani could not find words. She had no strength even to rise and run. She tried to say something. Her mouth faltered.

“You...? You...?” was all she could ask. She kept repeating it.

“I am the temple trustee Narasimha Naidu,” Naidu said, embracing her tightly.

Pulling away from him, Narayani cried, “Oh God! Is this justice from you?” What work God had gone off to do at that moment, and where, who knows? Perhaps he had sent “Kama” as his representative! Kama too joined Narasimha Naidu's side and began offering incense.

“My dear Narayani! Listen to me, girl; goddess among women!” Naidu knelt before her beauty. “If my husband consented to such a degrading act, does that mean you too, as the temple trustee, may commit a sinful act?” Narayani cried, tears streaming, as she clutched the trustee's feet tightly and trembled.

“I know nothing of sin and merit, Narayani! I don't need sermons from you about all that. Whatever else may fail once a week, Variyar's sermon at the temple never fails. I listen to it with my own ears without missing a single line,” Naidu said with a laugh, sweeping her into his arms.

Caught in his strong, youthful arms, Narayani's slender waist twisted, bent and drooped!

Still trapped in that powerful embrace, Narayani looked at him and spoke. Their faces were very close to<!-- source: scan 86; printed page: 85; join: scan85 ends ஒன்றுக் / scan86 begins கொன்று --> each other. She demanded angrily, “In the sermons you listen to, do they tell you to beg pleasure from another man's wife like this?”

“Yes, my dear—yes! Only this morning, in the thousand-pillared hall, there was a grand purana discourse about Iyarpagai Nayanar, who sent even ash-smeared Siva to amorous dalliance with his own wife,” he said, trying to press his face against hers. With a fierce determination to free herself somehow, she shoved him down in a single push, moved over to the wall, and began to sob.

Naidu grew angry. “Narayani! I lose nothing in this. Tomorrow your husband will go down the street with shackles on his hands. If you want to see that, act as you please!” he said, and left the room.

Narayani stood like a statue. Krishnayyar rushed toward her. “You sinner! What ruin have you brought upon me!” he cried, gripping her hand. Narayani had no desire to speak to him. Without a word she sat on the cot and stared at Krishnayyar as though she would burn him to ashes.

“Why are you looking at me like that? Have you too decided to put me in jail?”

Krishnayyar asked pitifully. Narayani remained silent, so he resumed speaking.

“My dear! You don't know my situation! Handcuffs are waiting for these hands! They've pinned the charge on me that I stole the Goddess's tali and the gold covering of the main idol! And the fellows who bought them where I sold them have betrayed me too! What am I to do now? Without the trustee's favour I won't escape, girl—I won't escape.”

Narayani only wept; she did not speak. How many anguished cries were in her heart! Her eyes were like tongues of flame blazing high.

“Make this sacrifice for me! It isn't such a great crime. The temple trustee Narasimha Naidu is a good<!-- source: scan 87; printed page: 86; join: scan86 ends நல்ல / scan87 begins மனுஷன் --> man—he's like a brother to me! Only if he sets his mind to it can he save me. Our Bharata itself says that a respectable woman may have relations with as many as five men! Listen, my beloved—if they put me in jail for ten years, fifteen years, won't you keep worrying? Give your consent so that the two of us can live happily without that worry!”

Iyer pleaded. Narayani's silence continued. Iyer gained a little hope. He assumed that Narayani had decided to sacrifice herself for him.

“He hasn't gone anywhere. He's right in the next room! I'll tell him to come now; don't drive him away again!” Krishnayyar said, running outside.

Narayani wiped away her tears. She rose with some decision in mind and waited for Naidu to come. Naidu too entered eagerly. Narayani looked longingly at his face.

“I've come, Narayani! I didn't go anywhere. I was right in the next room. I understood that you were only being stubborn to test my resolve,” Naidu said, grinning as he came toward her. Before he had finished speaking, Narayani caught his hands and suddenly asked, “Will you keep me and protect me always?” Then she broke down and wept.

Naidu, who had not expected such words from her, was startled too.

“I ask you in all seriousness—the man who handed me over to you, from today onward I will no longer worship him in the position of husband. From now on, you alone are my refuge! You alone are my husband! I may not have the good fortune to be your wife, but I will be your slave. Promise me that you will never hand me back into that sinner's hands.”

It was a moment when Naidu had no choice but to say that he would fulfil whatever Narayani asked! So Naidu nodded.

“It's like telling a man who asked for a piece of sugarcane to take the whole field, Narayani—your<!-- source: scan 88; printed page: 87; join: scan87 ends உன் / scan88 begins பேச்சு --> words!” he said. Then he prevailed. Krishnayyar once again became a temple priest. The police too announced that no proper clue had been found about the stolen jewels. From morning until the midnight puja, Krishnayyar's work was at the temple. Every day he preached to devotees until two in the morning. After that, his bed and sleep were in the temple pavilion itself! A child was born to Naidu and Narayani and, fortunately, died on the fourth day after birth. Had it lived, perhaps Naidu's relationship with her might have broken. Narayani forgot that she had ever had a lord named Krishnayyar.

One day word reached Krishnayyar that the temple trustee was away from town. Iyer heard that, for the municipal chairman election, Naidu had bought seven or eight members and taken them off somewhere to *oru kadaram paththirukku*, and that he would take two days to return.

Old memories of Narayani gnawed at Iyer. He tormented himself: would it not have been better to go to jail rather than offer Narayani to Naidu? Could jail really be worse than this temple pavilion? He developed a desire to meet Narayani somehow, just once. Thinking that a suitable opportunity to satisfy that desire had arrived, he set out.

He knocked on Narayani's door. Thinking Naidu had returned, she opened it. Iyer stood before her. Narayani ordered him to leave at once and went toward her room. Iyer followed her. “You'd better leave with some dignity!” she warned. Iyer pleaded.

“The Narayani whom you first met and fondled in the temple that day is not this woman! I am Naidu's wife—the property of Trustee Narasimha!” she roared.

“Whatever else, aren't you still my wife?” Iyer was determined somehow to make her prey to his hunger. She, however, was a volcano. Her eyes looked like balls of fire that would burn Iyer to ashes.

“Will you or won't you, Narayani?”—roughly<!-- source: scan 89; printed page: 88; join: scan88 ends முரட்டுத்தனமாக / scan89 begins அவளது கரங்களை இழுத்த --> pulling her arms, Iyer received a resounding “Palaaaar!” slap from Narayani across his cheek.

“I am a prostitute, Iyer—yes, a prostitute! In the midst of Harishchandras like you who sell their wives, how can women like me live in chastity?” Narayani shouted at the top of her voice.

“Ten years in prison! You could not bear that! You sold the body of a chaste wife! Like a daylight robber, you stole the Lord's property, turned your wife into a courtesan, lost your honour, and saved your life—what right do you have to call me your wife? Pah! Shameless man! Go, Iyer, go! There are devotees who praise your frauds as sermons—go to them!”

Narayani stood pointing a finger toward the street. Iyer, however, would not leave her. He was determined to quench that day's thirst before he went. To Narayani, the man before her was nothing more than some clay doll!

Iyer embraced her. “You cannot escape!” he growled. A fierce struggle went on between Narayani and him for a while. Realizing that there was no way to get free, a sudden thought came to Narayani—the handgun Naidu kept hidden in the table! She ran to the table and opened it. She took out the gun.

“Dumeel! Dumeel!” rang out.

“Aiyyo! Aiyyo!” came a cry.

Narayani was stunned. It was not Iyer's voice—it was Naidu's. She ran and looked. Naidu lay crumpled at the doorway of the room. Iyer, untouched by danger, was hiding under the cot. Having killed Naidu, Narayani fell upon him and lamented.

Setting aside even his worry over the municipal chairman election, Narasimha Naidu had rushed back in the meantime to see Narayani once. Unexpectedly, Narayani shot and killed him. But the police did not register the case that way. They made Iyer into a hero. It was ruled that Iyer—who had hidden and fainted under the cot—had shot Narasimha Naidu dead and taken his revenge.

<!-- source: scan 90; printed page: 89 -->

Life imprisonment! Iyer was delighted that, at least to that extent, the Lord had saved him from the muzzle of Narayani's gun and from the court's death sentence!

Narayani, meanwhile, was orphaned. As long as she lived as a family with Iyer, the townspeople called her “Narayani Ammami!” After Narasimha Naidu entered her life, the town called her “Mrs. Naidu.” Now she had become Nadutheru Narayani. Earlier, “Nadutheru Narayani” merely indicated the street where she lived—but now?... Nadutheru had acquired an entirely different meaning!

However ruined Narayani's life had become, Narasimha Naidu still held a good place in her heart. In trying to kill that petty-minded Iyer, had she not killed the man she loved instead? Through haste—and fury! Whenever she saw the procession chariot depicting the episode of Narakasura being slain by Bama, she remembered the killing she herself had committed. Perhaps that is why she sat so sorrowfully on Deepavali day.
