# Can One Forget the Concealment of Honour?

Surrounded by warriors and seated on horseback, Kuruvichchi Naachchiyar arrived at the palace entrance. Kaakkai Vanniyan came forward to receive her, making a great effort to put on a pleasant face, attended to her and brought her inside. Sundaralingam came with Kuruvichchi, and on seeing him Kaakkai smiled with an overflowing show of friendship. The British officers seated in the inner hall remained in their chairs and merely nodded as though welcoming her.

“Kuruvichchi is my friend from the gurukulam!” he said, introducing her to those officers. Through the proud look in her eyes, she in turn made it clear that she was not one to bend and submit before anyone.

“This is the first time your feet have stepped into my palace!” Kaakkai Vanniyan said, all awkward eagerness. “Everything must have its proper time, must it not?” Kuruvichchi replied, a bubbling smile flashing across her cheeks.

“Your coming now is certainly at a good time! But instead of coming for this, you are someone who should have come long ago—for some other reason!” Kaakkai Vanniyan said, winking. Afraid of what reply she might give, he did not let her speak and hurried on: “All right! All right! First let me attend to the guest who has come here! Let us eat!”

<!-- source boundary: scan 103 → scan 104 -->

“No! First I must speak about the matter that brought me here,” Kuruvichchi said.

“We know what brought you! To get your elderly grandfather released! What great matter is that? Now that you have come seeking my house, would I send you away empty-handed? Before you arrived, I already spoke to these officers here. They too have agreed to release your grandfather.”

Kuruvichchi could not bring herself to open her mouth and say, “Thank you.” She feared it would make the release look like a favour obtained by bowing before enemies.

Sundaralingam and she had agreed to come to Kaakkai Vanniyan's palace only on the basis of an honourable negotiation, trusting that Sangili Thalapathi could be released and taken away. So both of them listened eagerly to hear what else Kaakkai Vanniyan would say. Kaakkai continued.

“Sangili Thalapathi will be released without any major condition. Once Kuruvichchi herself agreed to come here in person, I had to work very hard to persuade these officers to relax the condition.”

“A condition! Relaxing that condition!” Naachchiyar's brows rose and arched over her forehead.

“Nothing extraordinary! What the officers first said was that, in Sangiliyar's place, one of Pandarakan's warriors should remain in prison....”

Before Kaakkai could finish saying it, sparks flared in Kuruvichchi's eyes!

“A warrior in prison in place of my grandfather? A splendid condition! My grandfather may as well remain in prison! We will rescue him in the way he ought to be rescued!”

<!-- source boundary: scan 104 → scan 105 -->

The British officers understood Kuruvichchi's anger and looked at Kaakkai Vanniyan meaningfully. At once he turned to her and said, “No! No! I had already told them that you would not accept that condition!”

The officers had already explained to him the complications that could arise if their refusal to release Sangili Thalapathi became a major issue. Besides, his chief aim now was to find a place for himself in Kuruvichchi's heart! He was therefore quite prepared to come down step by step. Kuruvichchi Naachchi stood as a warrior woman in an honour-bound camp that believed one must defeat the enemy and win—or the enemy must come to terms and let the matter end peacefully—and that, if neither happened, death itself was better. What else could Kaakkai Vanniyan's conditions become before such a woman but drops of water falling into a boiling cauldron!

Thinking, let it work as far as it can, he continued.

“Kuruvichchi! Do you know the final condition to which I have managed to get the officers to agree?”

She raised herself proudly and looked at him. The face of that golden-radiant woman, tinged with yellow, had reddened like kumkum. She watched him sharply, waiting to hear what he was going to say.

“You need only say that you killed the British soldier through carelessness and, for that, offer an apology...”

Kaakkai did not finish! Kuruvichchi trembled.

“An apology!” she cried aloud, rising from her seat.

“The officers said you should ask for one.

<!-- source boundary: scan 105 → scan 106 -->

I told them none of that was necessary—that it would be enough if you expressed regret.”

Kaakkai Vanniyan slid rapidly downward, as though slipping all the way from the summit.

“I can express regret! Poor man, a soldier died! Do we not offer condolences for the dead? In that sense!”

She said this firmly.

“That is exactly what I said too. You are repeating the very words I told the officers. That is enough! That is enough!”

Then, turning toward the officers, he said, “See! Kuruvichchi and I think alike! Did we not both study in the same gurukulam?” And then, overflowing with desire, he asked,

“Kuruvichchi! The negotiations have succeeded exactly as you wished! At least now, may we join the feast?”

he asked, overflowing with desire. “If Sangili Thalapathi, who is in prison, is also there, everyone can happily join the feast,”

Sundaralingam said with a faint smile.

“Then let us have him brought! Kuruvichchi! Come, all of you! We will go and invite your grandfather from prison to the feast!”

Kaakkai rose as he said this. At that moment a prison guard hurried in and said,

“Sangili Thalapathi in the prison asked that this letter be given to Naachchiyar,”

and held it out to Kaakkai Vanniyan.

“Give it directly to Kuruvichchi! A granddaughter needs no permission from me to read her grandfather's letter,”

Kaakkai said. Kuruvichchi took the letter placed in her hand

<!-- source boundary: scan 106 → scan 107 -->

and began to read it with some agitation. Seeing the changes that passed across her face as she read, the others watched her with curiosity, wondering what could have been written in the letter.

“My dear granddaughter Kuruvichchi, this is a land where warriors roam! A few cowards may have been born—are there not weeds among crops? Just so! I am one who has rejoiced again and again in thinking of the struggle you wage together with Pandaraka Vanniyan against the Parangiyars, so that their domination may not take root in this soil! Today is the day when dust has fallen upon that joy! Do you ask how? When I held responsibility as one of the commanders of Mullaitheevu, I lost my leg in a battle against the Ollandars, the Dutch! Why did I lose it? How did I lose it? Enemies surrounded me on the battlefield! I stood alone and fought them hard enough to exhaust them! Enemy corpses kept falling around me! The enemy saw our warriors, who had been at a distance, running toward me to help. Before they arrived, they threatened to finish me off and told me to ask for pardon if I wanted my life. I thundered that ours was not a tradition that asked for pardon. One enemy shot me with a gun. I crumpled and fell. By then our warriors arrived, and the foreigners fled from there. On that battlefield I was ready to lose my life. Not my honour. My leg was gone. But the sense of honour that our people have guarded through the ages did not disappear. Today I fear that what you have done may have become a challenge to that sense of honour.

<!-- source boundary: scan 107 → scan 108 -->

“The foreigners abducted me, brought me here and placed me under guard in Kaakkai Vanniyan's palace! You know Kaakkai! He is the kind of man who would accept a wretched office as a reward for betraying others! If a courageous granddaughter decided that she must rescue her grandfather no matter what, she should have treated her own life as a trifle and leapt into battle! Even thinking of using the warriors of Mullaitheevu for that purpose was wrong! This is our personal problem—should the army of the realm be called in to help with it? Let even that pass! If you had brought that army, broken Kaakkai Vanniyan's fort and freed me—or died in the attempt—you would have had a place in the history of valour! But now you have come to this palace—yes, to the enemy's camp—to beg, have you not! Are you not ashamed of this? It is a disgrace to my honour! I suffer at the thought that I have made my granddaughter into someone who has to come to an enemy palace and bow her head before him!

“If only I had lost my life on the battlefield that day instead of losing my leg, this humiliation would not have befallen you, would it? What troubles me is that I have become the object for whose sake you have been brought to this degradation!

“Should you make such an effort to free me?

“No! After my granddaughter Kuruvichchi Naachchiyar, born of a warrior lineage, has bid farewell to the code of warriors for my sake, I do not wish to gain freedom through her effort!

“Yours,
your Sangili Thatha.”

Her emotions surging and spilling one after another, Kuruvichchi Naachchiyar finished reading the letter. She looked anxiously at Kaakkai Vanniyan and Sundaralingam and said, “Come! Let us go to the prison!”

Only one British officer followed along with them.

The door of the prison where Sangili Thalapathi was confined was opened. Kuruvichchi looked inside with a pounding heart. He was not on the cot and mattress. Nor could she see him in the chair nearby.

“Hmm! We can go inside!” Kaakkai Vanniyan said, and everyone followed him in.

Sangili Thalapathi lay curled up in one corner against the prison wall.

“Even after all these comforts were provided, the poor man has become angry and is lying on the floor!” Kaakkai Vanniyan said as he went closer.

Kuruvichchi Naachchiyar ran to him, called, “Thatha!” and shook his body. It moved because she shook it. It had already become motionless.

“Aiyo! Thatha!” Kuruvichchi screamed.
