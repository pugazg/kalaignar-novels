# The Officer's Advice

The first report that reached Kaakkai Vanniyan said that the force led by Kuruvichchi Naachchiyar, instead of clashing with the warriors inside Kaakkai's fort as expected, had halted more than half a mile away from the fort. Another warrior came riding swiftly behind the man who brought that report.

“Kuruvichchi Naachchi and her warriors are remaining at a distance, and she has sent you a message through some warrior! He is on his way to the palace. Shall we allow him in when he arrives?” he asked.

Astonished, Kaakkai looked at the British officer beside him and sought his opinion.

“As long as we are strong, there is nothing to fear! We can find out what Kuruvichchi has sent word to say. So there is nothing wrong in allowing that warrior in,” the British officer said. Kaakkai then ordered that the man bringing Kuruvichchi's message be brought before him.

Who was this warrior bold enough to come all alone into a traitor's fort—even if it had been merely an enemy's fort, that would have been one thing?

It was Sundaralinga Kudumbar! And it was he who had given Kuruvichchi Naachchi that very advice.

“What good is it if, in rushing to rescue Sangili Thalapathi, you too go and get caught by them? The first thing we must learn is whether Sangiliyar is actually inside Kaakkai's fort. Next, we must ask Kaakkai himself and find out whether he can be released or not. Give me permission! I will go myself and speak to Kaakkai Vanniyan about it.”

Kuruvichchi liked Sundaralingam's idea and agreed. After sending Sundaralingam to Kaakkai Vanniyan, she and the warriors dismounted and sat nearby where dense trees cast deep shade. She admired the sense of duty, eagerness and fearless heart of Sundaralinga Kudumbar, who had come from Tamil Nadu. Instead of finishing the work that had brought him from Tamil Nadu and returning, he had willingly taken on the troubles here as well. Thinking of that breadth of heart, she sat on the great knot of a tree root.

Kaakkai Vanniyan was startled to see that the man being brought into the palace council hall did not look like a Tamil from Sri Lanka.

Kaakkai Vanniyan had expected him to come forward, fold his hands, cover his mouth, stand humbly and deliver his message. Instead, Sundaralinga Kudumbar went and seated himself on one of the ornate chairs decorating the council hall.

At first sight, the great difference between Pandaraka Vanniyan and Kaakkai Vanniyan

became clear to Sundaralingam. In a single moment Sundaralinga Kudumbar perceived how refinement, courage, affection, love, fearlessness and ease of manner all shone like a blazing halo around Pandaraka Vanniyan's head, while a vengeful expression and burning eyes from which treacherous thoughts seemed to pour made Kaakkai Vanniyan's character plain. Thinking that Pandaraka stood in the image of Kattabomman and Kaakkai in the image of Ettappan, tears filled his eyes as he thought, “Tamil people! Is this your condition?” Fortunately, the tears only veiled his eyes like a curtain and then receded; they did not spill down his cheeks.

He had not been told, “Sit,” nor had he asked permission to sit, yet he had already seated himself on his own. Who could this man be? What responsibility might he hold in Pandaraka Vanniyan's rule? With these questions tangled in his mind, Kaakkai Vanniyan opened the conversation with Sundaralingam.

“What is the matter?”

That was how he began.

“First I will tell you who I am. My name is Sundaralingam. I am a close friend of Vellaiyathevan, the army commander of Kattabomman, king of Panchalankurichi, who has raised the war cry against the British in Tamil Nadu! I too am a deputy commander in the Panchai army! All of you may be thinking, ‘What is this? This man could only have come secretly from Tamil Nadu, yet he is speaking the truth out in the open!’ I, who have set out raising a whirling sword to protect my own soil, freedom and self-respect—
