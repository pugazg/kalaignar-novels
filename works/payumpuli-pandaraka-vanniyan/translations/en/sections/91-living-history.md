# Living History!

Kuruvichchi laughed fiercely as she looked at Edward and the soldiers who had come with him, all staring at her with startled eyes.

“What are you so astonished about? You are wondering how a woman who ought to be lying dead in Panangamam has appeared here, are you not? I and my warriors have turned your men into corpses in Panangamam and come here to protect the Mullaitheevu palace. What do you say? Will you go back carrying your lives in your bodies—or have you come determined to leave your lives here and send only your bodies to the graveyards?”

The sword in Kuruvichchi Naachchi’s hand whirled, already painted red.

How such a scheme entered Edward Madge’s mind is hard to say. Yet instead of flaring up before Kuruvichchi’s blaze of anger, he began to answer with a very calm smile.

“Madam! You are speaking in haste! And you are angry as though something terrible has happened. Please listen to what I have to say. The war has somehow come to an end! Your beloved, Pandaraka Vanniyan, the guardian of this Mullaitheevu,

<!-- source boundary: scan 467 → scan 468 -->

was defeated in battle by us and has entered into a peace agreement. One of our commanders, Vanderi Berg, and Pandaraka Vanniyan have shaken hands and are sitting together enjoying a feast.”

Kuruvichchi readily believed Edward’s words. If nothing of that kind had happened, could Edward and his men have entered the Mullaitheevu palace so easily? It was through that doubt that she fell into the net of deception Edward had woven.

“Where is he now? At what place?”

“He is in Vanderi Berg’s camp. The two of them have forgotten their enmity and are talking together like friends who have known each other for years.”

The sword that Kuruvichchi had been holding high lowered toward the ground. Still eager for more details, she asked, “Where is that camp?”

Only if such a camp really existed and Vanderi Berg and Pandaraka Vanniyan were actually sitting there eating together could Edward have named its location. Yet he handled the situation cleverly.

“All these places are new to us! So I cannot tell you the exact name of the place where the camp stands. But if you come with us, we certainly know the route that will take us there.”

Kuruvichchi grew somewhat confused. She could not suddenly believe that Pandaraka Vanniyan had been defeated by the enemy, made a peace agreement with him, and was sitting in his camp feasting.

<!-- source boundary: scan 468 → scan 469 -->

But there was one thing she could not entirely dismiss: amid the modern weapons of the English forces, Pandarakan might indeed have suffered such a defeat.

“Was bringing me to where he is the purpose for which you came here?”

“No! No, madam! We did not even know you were here! Under the agreement Pandarakan and Vanderi Berg made, we came only to bring the Mullaitheevu palace under our control. We happened to find you here after we arrived. Why should your strength be wasted, maiden, before the very force that defeated Pandarakan? So we decided we might as well tell you the truth. Whether you come to that camp or not is entirely your choice. If you come with us, I myself will take you there. Otherwise you will have to remain here guarding this palace—or face our army. Why such astonishment, madam? If you simply come quietly with us and see Pandarakan, will you not learn every detail for yourself?”

“Very well, I will come!” Kuruvichchi Naachchiyar sheathed her sword and followed Edward. Two of Edward’s soldiers and two of Kuruvichchi’s warriors followed the pair from the front of the Mullaitheevu palace. The six horses passed through several streets of Mullaitheevu and were moving along a long, narrow road when an English soldier came racing toward them on horseback and pulled up before Edward.

Edward looked arrogantly at the soldier. “What?”

<!-- source boundary: scan 469 → scan 470 -->

he asked.

The soldier handed Edward a rolled letter. Edward hurriedly read it to himself.

“Dear Edward! Pandaraka Vanniyan, together with fifty of his warriors, has been surrounded and taken prisoner by our forces near the Nedunkeni road in the Ottusuttan region. My friend! You must go at once to Panangamam and defeat Kuruvichchi Naachchiyar, who is fighting us there with great fury.—Thus, Vanderi Berg.”

So spoke the letter. Edward rejoiced inwardly. Proud of himself for having tricked Kuruvichchi and led her away without even needing to go to Panangamam, he praised himself in his own mind. Smiling at her so that she would suspect nothing, Edward said, “You asked me first where the camp was, did you not? The information has come in this letter.”

“Where is it?” Kuruvichchi asked.

“It is said to be near the Nedunkeni road in the Ottusuttan region.”

Edward rolled up the letter and tossed it back to the soldier who had brought it. The soldier caught it deftly.

The seven horses reached the Nedunkeni road. It was a grove thick with tall trees. Inside that grove, there was said to be a camp. On each tree near the camp, a warrior of Mullaitheevu was chained. Fifty warriors had been bound in that way. In their midst, chained to a large, sturdy tree, was Pandaraka

<!-- source boundary: scan 470 → scan 471 -->

Vanniyan.

At the sight of that cruel scene, Kuruvichchi forgot herself, ran to Pandarakan, threw her arms around him and wailed aloud. Stroking her hair, Pandarakan consoled her.

“Do not worry! They suddenly surrounded us while we were resting in this grove and are now making a great show of having defeated us.”

Pandarakan growled like a tiger.

“They brought me here saying you had made an agreement with them!”

“An agreement? An agreement to enslave the soil of Lanka and Tamil Eelam to these foreign Englishmen? Do you believe my head would nod to such a thing while this life remains in me?”

Kuruvichchi stood without speaking. She thought deeply for a moment. Then, as though she had arrived at a decision, she looked at Vanderi Berg and Edward and said,

“He will speak like that—but I can bring him around to my way. Even if he has not made an agreement with you until now, I am ready to make one from this point onward. And he will not reject what I say!”

Even as she spoke, Kuruvichchi winked at Pandarakan and asked, “You must not refuse what I say. Is that all right?” Understanding her intention, Pandarakan stood in silence.

“Silence is a sign of consent! We have grown weary from fighting the English all this time. Our old weapons are wonderful! They are powerful! Yet they cannot stand against your modern arms. Even without weapons we can perform many feats. With nothing more than swords and spears,

<!-- source boundary: scan 471 → scan 472 -->

we can perform exploits that will amaze the world!”

While Kuruvichchi was still speaking, Vanderi Berg interrupted.

“With swords and spears, what feats could you possibly perform that the world would praise?” he asked in astonishment.

“We will divide our fifty warriors into two groups and make them stand on opposite sides, swords in their hands. I will stand in the middle with a large fruit impaled on the point of a spear and held above my head. Our fifty warriors will charge at one another with their swords, and without inflicting a single wound on anyone, they will cut the fruit at the spear-tip above my head into fifty pieces in a single sweep. Only one piece of fruit will remain upon the spear above my head.”

The moment Kuruvichchi said this, Vanderi Berg turned to the soldiers with an “Is that so?” and ordered, “Hey! Untie everyone except Pandaraka Vanniyan! Let them perform that astonishing feat. We shall see!”

At the words “except Pandarakan,” Kuruvichchi was deeply disappointed. Even so, she managed to conceal it.

She fixed a large fruit upon a spear, placed it above her head and stood in the middle. The warriors of Mullaitheevu who had been unbound—everyone except Pandarakan—gathered on horseback with swords, twenty-five on either side.

“Mm! Charge!” Kuruvichchi cried, raising her hand and bringing it down in a signal. In that instant the fifty warriors crashed into the English soldiers who had been standing there watching the spectacle. Amid the great commotion, Pandaraka Vanniyan’s

<!-- source boundary: scan 472 → scan 473 -->

bonds were cut away. Pandarakan mounted a horse like the very leaping tiger he was and struck Edward down. The bodies of hundreds of English soldiers were hacked into pieces.

Edward, fallen to the ground and breathing his last, raised his gun. A bullet flew toward the centre of Kuruvichchi’s forehead. Pandarakan charged toward her on horseback to lift her away, but before he could reach her, the bullets had drunk her life.

Without realizing that her breath had stopped, Pandaraka Vanniyan escaped from the English with her clasped against him on the horse. His surviving warriors followed him.

She who had longed to wear the bridal garland and taste the sweetness of life—an ideal’s sacred lamp, a flame of sacrifice that would not go out even when extinguished—now wore the garb of death, lying in Pandarakan’s lap, held tight in his embrace as the horse sped on and on.

Believing that she was still alive, he and the Tamil warriors following him travelled a long distance along the forest path.

The path they took through the forest that day was the path shown by warrior-martyrs. Pandaraka Vanniyan was not alone; many like him would arise—people of resolve, truth, radiance and feeling. The history of that great warrior did not fail to serve that purpose. And so it is a living history!

(The End)
