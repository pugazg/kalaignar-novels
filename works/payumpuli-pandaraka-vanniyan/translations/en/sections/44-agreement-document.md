# Agreement Document

Poets may paint an imaginative picture of the sun awakening from sleep and setting out to perform its duty, turning the whole earth golden. But what rest does the sun ever have? Having laboured to give light to another part of the earth, it was now rising higher into the sky after lending beauty to the palace of Kandy with its morning rays.

Though it had been daylight for a long time, Vikrama Rajasingan's eyes remained closed. Because his body was somewhat fleshy, his snoring seemed to toss about every movable object in that luxurious room. He had drunk beyond measure, and so slept blissfully! Having crossed the very limits of amorous revelry, he lay exhausted as though his body no longer belonged to him.

The door to the women's quarters was hammered upon. The noise did not startle him awake. The knocking grew louder and louder. The king slowly turned over, groaned, and without fully knowing what he was doing sat up. Before he could get up and open it, the room door opened on its own.

“Piyasili! Piyasili!” shouted Pilimathala as he rushed in at great speed. The king, who was without clothes, hurriedly picked up a golden shawl lying there, wrapped it around his waist, and looked on in alarm.

“Piyasili! Where are you, child?”

Seeing Pilimathala circling the room in agitation, the king panicked. “What? What is it?”

“Can't you smell smoke in the room? Alas! What has happened to my Piyasili?”

Vikrama Rajasingan, coming somewhat to his senses, tried to calm Pilimathala. “Nothing has happened to her. She is right here. She was with me all night. Forgetting everything that had happened, the two of us spent the night happily. She must have risen before me and gone to the bathing room.” Saying this, he walked toward the bathing room and gently pushed at its door. It opened at once.

“Piyasili! You are a little child! A baby! Your father has come searching because he cannot see you. Am I going to swallow you?” Vikrama Rajasingan laughed as he stepped into the bathing room—then cried out “Ah!” as though he had stepped on fire.

“What? What is it? What has happened to my girl?” Pilimathala cried, peering into the room.

A woman's form lay inside the bathing room, burned so badly that neither face nor limbs could be recognized, reduced almost to a charred log.

“You sinner! You have burned my daughter to death!” Pilimathala seized Vikrama Rajasingan by the shoulders and shook him violently. The king understood nothing.

Only he and she had been inside the room that night. If she was found there in the morning as a charred corpse, would not everyone

say that it was his doing? The king was shaken by fear that the blame would fall upon him. Pilimathala might normally have been a minister who had to submit to him, but because of what had now happened, the king himself was in a position where he had to submit to Pilimathala.

“Uncle! By God, I did nothing! The happiness we shared last night made me feel that every grievance I had against her had been pushed aside. I will hide only one truth from no one—we both drank far beyond our limits. In that intoxication, her clothes must have caught fire from the candle in the bathing room.”

“I will cast aside shame and ask you—how did fire catch a body with no clothes on it?”

“Uncle, why ask like that? She may have got up at dawn to go to the bathing room, wrapped her clothes around herself, and gone in. Look there at the bed—none of her clothes are there!”

“That may be so! I believe it! I also believe the truth that you followed her after she dressed and went into the bathing room, set her on fire, and are now trying to divert the matter!”

The king sat beside the charred body and shed tears. “Piyasili! What have you done, girl? When fire was burning that mango-soft body of yours, could you not have called out to me even once? Have you forgotten that last night I said I was willing even to give my life for you?”

“Ah! The ruler of a country knows how to act well indeed. If this land learns that you deceived my daughter, drugged her with desire, and then deceitfully burned her—there could be no greater stain, humiliation, disgrace or degradation than that!”

Pilimathala leapt about in fury.

“Uncle! I swear to you. I did not kill Piyasili. This was an accident. But palace servants may use the quarrel that existed between her and me to build a story that I was the one who finished her off. Both as my uncle and as a shrewd minister, the responsibility for saving me from this disgrace lies with you.”

“I stand here having lost a queenly daughter who could be called ruby, diamond and cat's-eye. The one who killed her is none other than her husband! The guardian of this country! And I am to save that murderer from disgrace! Fine justice indeed!”

Shaking his bald head, Pilimathala laughed terribly. Through that laughter, weeping too raised its head.

“Uncle! Right now only the two of us are here. Let us decide something before anyone comes. Whatever you ask, I will give. You need not tell a lie for my sake! It is enough if you say the truth—that Piyasili died in a fire accident! Save me from disgrace! The people of my land love me greatly! My penance is that not even a grain-sized wrong opinion about me should arise in their minds!”

“Poor man, I pity you when I look at you! Very well! I forgive you for killing my daughter. Do you know why? For this country! If what you did comes out, confusion will arise among the people! Their trust in you will lessen! It will give our enemies room

to act! So I forgive you! I will knowingly tell the lie that my daughter was caught in a fire accident by chance! Do you know what you must do for me in return?”

“What must I do? Whatever it is, I will do it!”

“Do not be afraid that I will ask you to hand me the responsibility of ruling the kingdom of Kandy! I do not want that! I have never had such greed! What I ask is something that will benefit both you and this country!”

“Ask! Ask without hesitation!”

“You must remain only the official king of Kandy. This palace life—its pleasures and amusements—will always be yours! But keeping you as a figurehead, I will run the entire administration of this kingdom! You will be king in name alone! I will be everything! Why? Not for myself! For the welfare of this country! For the freedom of this country!”

“All right! Let it be so!”

“You cannot simply say ‘let it be so’ and later deceive me! You must write it down and give it to me now!”

“Write what?”

“You must write and sign that from now on Pilimathala will not only be minister, but will also oversee all the king's administrative responsibilities, resolve problems arising with the British, and decide whether relations with Pandaraka Vanniyan should continue or not.”

“Will you proclaim it?”

“Am I a fool to proclaim it? That is why I told you at the outset that I would devise my plans from behind the curtain!”

<!-- source boundary: scan 217 → scan 218 -->

“All right! I accept. I will write it. But one thing—the friendship and relationship with Pandaraka Vanniyan must not be broken...”

“Did I say it must be broken? Did I not say that I would take care of that question?”

Vikrama Rajasingan stood silent. The silence was taken as a sign of consent.

With the deep sympathy of the people of the capital—and with mournful sighs—Piyasili's funeral procession took place and the final rites were completed.

At that same time, beyond the border of the kingdom of Kandy, two horses were travelling. On one horse was a handsome young man—with a light beard and a turban! Beside him, on another horse, rode a young woman—attractive in appearance! Looking at the young man, the young woman asked, “Piyasili! Is this the right road?”

At once the young man looked at her and said,

“Hey, fool! I am not Piyasili now! I am Jeyaseelan—your mute husband! Don't forget that and blurt something out somewhere!”

The two laughed. The horses kept going.
