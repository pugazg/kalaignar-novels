# Skill That Defeated Skill!

At the very time that a fierce battle was raging in Panangamam, Pandaraka Vanniyan learned that English forces were advancing toward Mullaitheevu. The forces coming from the Mannar region under Vanderi Berg and from the Thirukonamalai region under Edward Madge had gained too much of a lead. Realizing that it was now impossible for him to race them to Mullaitheevu and then attack the enemy forces from there, Pandaraka Vanniyan turned to a remarkable battle strategy.

The English troops could reach Mullaitheevu only by passing through forest routes. So he decided to cut across into the forest and meet those foreign forces there. Nor did he waste much time putting his quick and shrewd decision into action. Raising his sword toward the sky, he lifted his arm and roared out an order to his forces.

“Everyone, follow me! Let us defeat the enemy not by our valour alone, but by our intelligence as well!” Pandaraka Vanniyan’s resolute battle cry echoed throughout the region.

Within a short while after the English forces entered the forest route, Pandarakan’s warriors cut across those paths and concealed themselves among bushes and rocks. Their eyes seemed to have become ears as they waited to learn what their lion-like leader Pandarakan’s next command would be, and their sharp gazes clustered upon him. To reveal what his command was and what astonishing battle plan he had devised, that great warrior Pandarakan leapt down from his horse. From a distance, the eyes of his men moved along with every one of his actions.

After jumping down, he went to a bamboo tree there and looked up at it. Facing the bamboo, he raised both arms, pressed his feet firmly against the ground and sprang upward. With the same momentum he seized the upper part of the bamboo with both hands and bent down with it. At the tip of the bamboo he tied several fairly large stones with thin cord. Then he fastened a thick rope to the bamboo tip and held the free end in his hand.

His warriors, without so much as blinking, kept watching him, waiting to see what would come next. Just then Pandarakan spotted a wild buffalo passing that way. Signalling to the warriors to keep their eyes on him, he suddenly released the rope with which he had bent the bamboo tip all the way down to the ground. The instant he let go, the tip sprang upright with a whirr. With that force, the large stone tied there with thin cord snapped free and flew with tremendous speed toward the wild buffalo.

<!-- source boundary: scan 456 → scan 457 -->

It struck the animal. The weight of the stone and the speed behind it together brought the wild buffalo crashing down.

A smile flashed across Pandarakan’s face, adding an exultant beauty to his already heroic bearing. The very next moment his warriors had bent down all the bamboo trees in that forest. Stones hung from their tips like clusters of fruit. Holding the ends of the long ropes tied to those bamboo tips, the warriors of Mullaitheevu lay hidden behind rocks.

Just as Pandarakan had expected, the English forces began advancing along that route with drums beating. Pandarakan believed that the stones released from the bent bamboo tips by his men would be more powerful than the shells fired from the English cannon carts.

That belief was not entirely in vain. Ahead of the forces led by Vanderi Berg and Edward Madge came a defensive detachment under a commander named Johnston.

The very purpose of sending Johnston’s defensive force in front was to find out what the enemy was attempting. From their distant hiding places among the bushes and rocks along the forest route, Pandarakan’s warriors could not clearly tell whether the English force was being led by Vanderi Berg, Edward Madge, or the deputy commander Johnston.

They lay concealed, waiting for a single movement of their leader’s finger. Pandarakan’s

gaze, and the gazes of his warriors, were fixed upon the enemy force approaching along the forest road.

By sending Deputy Commander Johnston’s force ahead and diverting Pandarakan’s attention, Vanderi Berg and Edward Madge had very cleverly woven a net of deception.

Johnston’s men had passed the warriors hidden among the bushes and rocks and were moving along the forest route when Pandarakan and his men acted at a moment the English least expected. The bamboo tips that had been bent down until they touched the earth sprang upright with sharp cracks—so swiftly that even the speed of a streak of lightning would have been pushed into third or fourth place! At the same instant, the stones tied to their tips tore free and hurtled upon the English troops like arrows—or like cannonballs. Deputy Commander Johnston, among the first to be struck, not only fell unconscious but lost his eyesight from the shock of the fall. With the commander of the defensive force down, all the bent bamboo tips sprang upright. They rained stones.

Unable to withstand the sudden assault, the English soldiers scattered and ran. Pandaraka Vanniyan clapped his hands and laughed as he watched them fall wounded and dead upon the ground, while the survivors drove their horses wildly and fled in every direction through the forest, shouting “Oh!” His warriors too raised cries of victory. He waited to see whether more English troops would come that way.

Keeping watch on the road and preparing still more bamboo

<!-- source boundary: scan 458 → scan 459 -->

trees so that they could wipe out the treacherous foreigners with their battle strategy, Pandaraka Vanniyan’s warriors were caught completely unawares when English troops slowly crept up behind them and surrounded them. Pandaraka Vanniyan—the warrior and strategist who had thrown the English defensive commander and his men into utter confusion with that rare and daring method of warfare—found himself trapped with his warriors inside the English encirclement.

That fighting bull was shocked that the white men had defeated his military skill with another kind of skill. Yet he lost neither courage nor confidence. Standing tall and majestic, he raised his sword and spoke only a few words to his warriors.

“Warriors! Tamil hearts of self-respect! Every life that is born must one day die! If the life that departs is merely one among the hundreds of thousands or millions that die every day, who will value it? Who will remember it? But if the life that goes is given for Tamil—for the Tamil people—for the rights of Tamils—for the freedom of this land—then every such life will go on shining as a lamp of honour for our people!”

Pandarakan’s voice sounded to his warriors like a war drum. That leader was no hollow man who stirred his warriors to fury and then withdrew himself. Sword raised, he strode toward the English troops surrounding him.

The forest became a battlefield. Vanderi Berg ordered the cannon that had come with his forces to be fired. Countless warriors of Pandarakan’s army

<!-- source boundary: scan 459 → scan 460 -->

fell victim to those cannon that rained shells. Smoke enveloped the entire region. In that thick smoke, which did not clear quickly, no one could tell what had happened to Pandarakan.

As the smoke slowly began to thin, the English soldiers kicked and turned over the bodies of the warriors lying on the field. Angered by the sight, Vanderi Berg and Edward Madge shouted aloud at the same time.

“Hey, you foolish soldiers! How arrogant are you? Are you kicking and turning over with your feet those lamps of sacrifice who gave their lives for their country? If Pandaraka Vanniyan’s body is among these corpses, do you know what a disgrace it would bring upon us to have insulted so great a warrior?”

Seeing the anger of their commanders, the white soldiers stopped their degrading act and began using their hands to touch and move the bodies instead, searching to see whether Pandarakan’s body lay among them.
