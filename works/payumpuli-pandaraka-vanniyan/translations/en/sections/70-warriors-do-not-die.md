# Warriors Do Not Die!

On the very day Kakkai Vanniyan's fort was being charred into ash—on the very day the leaping tiger Pandaraka Vanniyan was shedding tears for his beloved sister Nalla Naachchi—the white Major Bannerman was conducting a summary judicial inquiry on the soil of Kayathar in Tamil Nadu. The petty rulers of several palayams were seated in that inquiry hall. The Company men, crazed with domination and eager to present themselves as men of justice who punished only after a thorough inquiry had proved guilt, had placed Veerapandiya Kattabomman in a prisoner's dock amid those petty rulers. Major Bannerman asked arrogantly whether he knew that they had placed him in their dock.

Veerapandiyan roared like a lion.

“Would I not know even this? My friend, the king of Pudukkottai, competed with Ettappa, the king of Ettayapuram, and carried off first prize for treachery! That is why I stand in this dock!”

“That is not the reason!” Bannerman said, pounding the table.

<!-- source boundary: scan 347 → scan 348 -->

“There are a few other reasons too! If there is any people in this world who have failed to prosper because they lack unity, it is the Tamil people! They are also a people who, without enemies even having to labour to divide them, reach the very heights of bitterness, jealousy and rancour and split themselves apart! The result is what you see now: Veerapandiyan standing in a dock before you while so many Tamil petty rulers stand watching the spectacle!”

“Oh! Is it our job, then, to unite your people, strengthen them, organize them and raise them into a great world power? If you are a sack of gooseberries, are we responsible for that? It is because we have learned that there are thousands of educated ignoramuses among your people who cannot clearly decide who is fit to lead them that the work of men like us becomes easy!”

“Bannerman! Say it well! I am supremely satisfied! Look at these great men dressed in glittering clothes and adorned with green gems, emeralds, diamonds, hessonites and cat's-eyes—men who have sold themselves for office and pomp and lie prostrate as servants of servants. Say it so they can understand! Ancient Tamil literature speaks of valour—honour, love—fame—generosity! Is it merely literature? No! Emblems of culture! Pearls of history! Gem-stones that reveal tradition! But these men have let their valour go astray! They have put honour to death and temporarily saved themselves from death! Even if fame moves beyond reach like an unattainable fruit or an inaccessible flower, they do not care; they love money and office, and are great philanthropists who offer up even their feeling for their people as a sacrificial gift for them! If only these men had understood, at the proper time, the true meaning hidden in your—

<!-- source boundary: scan 348 → scan 349 -->

—words, could you and your band of officials now be standing on the soil of Kayathar, crowing while Kattabomman stands in a cage? What can be done? Can everyone be an Oomaithurai, a Vellaiyathevan, a Sundaralinga warrior, or a Vairamuthu of my friend Pandaraka Vanniyan who rules Mullaitheevu in Lanka? In the cultivated field of the Tamil people, crops heavy with grain are few! Weeds are many! Here I stand amid those very weeds!”

“Enough!” Bannerman shouted wildly.

“These are all people dear to me! Loyal friends! Foundation stones for the rule of the English Company that is about to dawn!” Bannerman cried loudly.

Kattabomman laughed aloud and mocked him. “Why so many phrases to praise them? You could say it more elegantly in a single short expression—handles of the axe!”

“No more arguments and counterarguments like this! Let us come to the end of the case. I will now read the charges against Kattabomman!” Bannerman said angrily, unfolding the charge-sheet in his hand and reading it.

Veerapandiya Kattabomman, ruler of Panchalankurichi, had committed an offence by refusing to pay tax to the East India Company; an offence by insulting Collector Lushington when summoned for an interview; an offence by killing many Company soldiers. As Bannerman continued piling up charges in this way, Veerapandiyan interrupted him.

“If we live by paying tax, it means the land of Panchalankurichi—

<!-- source boundary: scan 349 → scan 350 -->

—has submitted to the Parangis! Therefore we refused to pay tax! We are people accustomed to receiving with affection those who come seeking us; we are not tailless creatures who must run in search of our enemies merely to bow and submit to them! They besieged Panchalankurichi fort. After the enemy's feet had trampled our soil, are we mere lumps of flesh, mere sacks fed with rice, that we should value life like jaggery? The Tamil people lived with heads held high—would such a people bend before deceit? Would they be bewitched by blandishments? Would they succumb to temptation? What wonder is there that we rose saying we would give our precious lives to protect this Tamil land? How can that be a crime? Because you surrounded me and my army after we left the fort, with the help of those who betrayed us, you now heap charges before me! Why this sham inquiry when you have already written the verdict and kept it ready? Shall I myself tell you the verdict you are going to give?”

Bannerman laughed terribly. “That saves me the work! You may certainly say it!”

“Why should I say it? Say it with your own mouth! Let the distinguished connoisseurs of the palayams seated here listen to that sweet music, nod their heads, clap their hands and cheer!”

The smile did not leave Kattabomman's face; its brightness only grew. Bannerman looked all around him, checking whether his guards were alert. Then he read the verdict: that at the tamarind tree of Kayathar, that lion with the proud gait, that swimmer against the current who never faltered before anything, that man with a heart capable of bearing anything, was to be hanged—

<!-- source boundary: scan 350 → scan 351 -->

—and once the sentence was pronounced, those who had betrayed him, those who had committed treachery against him, those who had gained the pleasures of office because they opposed him—even their eyes filled with tears. Perhaps they were already troubled by the thought that future history would describe them as degraded beings.

“I do not care about the verdict! I have only one regret. If I had died at the gate of my fort fighting the enemy, my history would have been even more glorious. Without receiving that fortune, I am going to hang. You who wash the enemy's feet and drink the water—are you happy now? Live! Live! Live well! I take my leave!”

He raised his hand and waved, strode forward like a warrior, and touched and kissed the hanging rope. He himself placed the noose around his neck and, with a smiling face, underwent the punishment imposed by the English zealots.

Having lost both his sister and Thanigaimala, Pandaraka Vanniyan returned to his palace in anguish. When he heard of Veerapandiya Kattabomman's end, he was shaken.

Kuruvichchi Naachchiyar, thinking that the news from Tamil Nadu must not cause him to lose heart, gently placed a hand on his shoulder and shook him. “Warriors do not die! We too are not going to die!” she said.

Pandaraka Vanniyan clasped her hand tightly.
