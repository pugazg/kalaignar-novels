“On behalf of the freedom fighters of Tamil Nadu, I convey my heartfelt thanks to Naachchiyar for saving the king from danger,” Sundaralingam said. Kuruvichchi Naachchi bowed her head with great respect and, by way of answering his praise, let a smile curl across her crimson lips.

“Kuruvii is extraordinarily lucky today! Praise is pouring in from Tamil Nadu!” said Pandaraka Vanniyan, looking at her from the corner of his eye.

“Is it proper to keep him standing on the road while we talk? Wouldn't courtesy mean taking him to the palace, offering him at least a meal, and then sending him on his way?” Kuruvichchi said without turning her face toward the king, her soft voice resonating like a veena.

However much the king pressed him, Sundaralingam would not agree.

“If not for my sake, at least come to the palace for my Kuruvii's sake before you leave!”

“Forgive me! Without me, Vellaiyathevan's work simply will not move! Kattabomman will be eager to know whether I met you, whether I reached you safely, and what your answer is to the matters in the letter. So the proper thing is to give me leave at once and send me back.”

Even as they were speaking, a warrior went away, cut a cluster of tender coconuts and brought them there. Sundaralingam noticed that the order had been carried out through one of Kuruvichchi's gentle gestures, and inwardly

marvelled at her hospitality. Pandara Vanniyan took a tender coconut, cut it himself with his own hand, and gave it to Sundaralingam. Sundaralingam was simply overwhelmed by the affection the two of them showed him.

“What, finally, shall I tell the king of Panchalankurichi?” Sundaralingam asked eagerly. Patting him reassuringly, Pandaraka Vanniyan said, “Tell him this Vaira Muthu is always ready to stop British domination from taking root!” As he said it, his palm-trunk, diamond-hard shoulders swelled.

“I am very happy! I shall take my leave!” Sundaralingam said, bringing his palms together.

Then Kuruvichchi Naachchi whispered something in Pandaraka Vanniyan's ear. The king's face brightened. Looking at Sundaralingam, he said, “Kuruvii can sometimes give me good advice too! I had forgotten. She is the one reminding me. My warriors will accompany you as far as the seashore!” One warrior dismounted and offered his horse for Sundaralingam to ride. But Kuruvichchi Naachchi did not like that. She felt it would be disrespectful to send Sundaralinga Kudumbar away on an ordinary warrior's horse. So she asked to ride that warrior's horse herself and have her own horse given to Sundaralingam.

“When you came here from the coast, you took the roundabout route through all the villages. On your way back, you can go by the shortcut,” Pandaraka Vanniyan said. Sundaralingam did not accept the suggestion.

“My king! I do not wish to take the shortcut. Do not mistake me for rejecting your suggestion. I want to pass through the villages again. Do you know why?” Sundaralingam asked, leaving the question hanging.

Neither Pandaraka Vanniyan's lips nor Kuruvichchi's moved to ask, “Why?” Instead, their eyes themselves became question marks.

“I want to see Sangili Thatha, whom I met on the way, once more before I go. Who knows whether I will ever have another chance to see him? In the heroic struggle to the death that we are waging in Tamil Nadu, that flower-bed may come to me soon enough! So I want to see this warrior-maiden's grandfather once more, speak to him, and then leave,” said Sundaralingam.

Pandaraka Vanniyan looked meaningfully at Kuruvichchi. She understood and moved her lips to say, “When you go to grandfather's house, shouldn't his granddaughter be there too?”

Sundaralingam could not understand how that warrior-maiden, who fought carrying spear, sword and lance, could bend with such shyness only before her beloved.

“No need! I am not going to stay at grandfather's house! I am only going to see him in passing and then leave—that is all!” he said. Taking leave of the two of them once more, he set out overflowing with happiness.

He and the mounted warriors who had come as his escort sped toward the Mullaitheevu seashore.

On the way, they reached Sangili Thalapathi's village.

“Do not forget—stop at that house!” he had told the warriors accompanying him.

Accordingly, the warriors pulled their horses to a halt before Sangili Thatha's house. Sundaralingam eagerly looked toward the veranda where the old man had sat. He was not there. Before Sundaralingam could finish saying, “Is he inside the house? Go and see,” four or five elderly women from the street came running toward them, wailing. They were weeping and lamenting.

“Where is Sangili Thatha?”

“Alas! Ten or fifteen foreign soldiers carried him away!”

“Why? For what?”

“Kuruvichchi is said to have killed a soldier—so, in retaliation, they carried him away!”

That was how the elderly women explained the matter to the warriors who asked for details.

“Those wretches! That elderly man with the crippled leg?” Sundaralingam cried out involuntarily.
