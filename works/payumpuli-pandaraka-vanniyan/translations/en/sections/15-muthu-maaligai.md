# Muthu Maaligai!

The people dispersing in all four directions from the Visaka festival at the Vattrappalai Kannagi temple could hardly have known anything about the events that had taken place a little distance from Vattrappalai. They did not know that Sundaralinga Kudumbar, who had come from Tamil Nadu, had met Pandaraka Vanniyan; that before meeting him he had fought an elephant; that Kuruvichchi Naachchiyar had killed one of the white men who had come to spy; or that the remaining white soldiers had carried away Kuruvichchi's grandfather, Sangili Thalapathi, to Kaakkai Vanniyan's palace. Unaware of all this, the people returned to their respective villages, enthusiastically talking among themselves about the splendour of the Kannagi Amman festival, the unwavering love that Kuruvichchi Naachchi, who had led the festival, bore Pandaraka Vanniyan, and Pandaraka Vanniyan's affection for her.

Along the route taken by some of those people, they saw Pandaraka Vanniyan and Kuruvichchi heading toward their palace surrounded by a few guards, and cries of “Long live Pandaraka Vanniyan!” rang out.

As the people stood on both sides of the road, cheering and making way, the king bowed respectfully to his beloved subjects

<!-- source boundary: scan 73 → scan 74 -->

and, together with Kuruvichchi, reached the palace.

Without gaudy decoration, modest in appearance yet rising with a lion's majesty, that palace did not occupy a vast tract of land; even so, it was four times larger than Kaakkai Vanniyan's palace. Though it had no ornate upper storeys or grand halls, it stood with an air of spirited dignity. Within the palace walls stood an unusual mansion. It looked novel, but without glitter or ostentation. The imagination of the person who designed it was remarkable. The outer form of the mansion resembled an open shell; the inner form was like the pearl within that shell. Open the door set into that pearl and there was a spacious interior. It had a sleeping room, a resting room, a conversation room, a dining room and a cooking room—everything. This unusual pearl mansion had been created with great simplicity.

Pandaraka Vanniyan and Kuruvichchi entered the mansion. Only when Pandaraka Vanniyan went into the Muthu Maaligai alone was it his custom to bolt the entrance door. When Kuruvichchi came with him, they did not close the entrance door.

Their hearts were united, but the love that had blossomed between them had not taken them to the stage of a physical relationship. They felt that even creating the appearance that they were alone inside the mansion would be wrong, because their subjects should not be given room for such a doubt.

Kuruvichchi delighted in every movement of Pandaraka Vanniyan. She had surrendered herself to the grace,

<!-- source boundary: scan 74 → scan 75 -->

refinement, steadfast courage and spirit of sacrifice woven through his speech. This was not something new. Five years earlier, when he was sixteen and she was fifteen, while the two were studying Tamil grammar and literature under Thanga Naachchiyammai, they had already become bound in a love that would never part. Rather than say that he was infatuated with Kuruvichchi's beautiful form and wide eyes, the truth was that he had been won over by her pure love. Above all stood the feeling that they must guard the flame of Mullaitheevu's freedom and never let it die—a great force capable of binding their hearts together. By the strength of that feeling, they had resolved not to marry until their ideal was fulfilled, but to live as lovers and friends.

They would sit close together and talk, eat, and stroll; they would think about daring ways to face British domination, point out one another's faults sharply, and even shower one another with teasing words. But the thought that pleasure required their two bodies to unite never arose in either of them. They regarded their duty to keep the land from falling into the hands of those crazed with domination as a joy greater than physical pleasure.

They had spoken at length and seriously about that duty, and before Kannagi Amman had sworn and affirmed that they loved one another with all their hearts. Kuruvichchi's pure love, founded not on lust but on duty, had thus become firm. In the joy of that certainty, she had also conducted the Visaka festival grandly for the deity of Vattrappalai. Now, with delight leaping within her that her

<!-- source boundary: scan 75 → scan 76 -->

five-year dream had been fulfilled, she hurried into the inner rooms of the Muthu Maaligai.

They had been delayed by several unexpected incidents and had been unable to return as soon as the festival ended. Worried that lunchtime had slipped past, and knowing that Pandaraka Vanniyan could not bear hunger, Kuruvichchi hurriedly gathered the food and rushed into the dining room.

“Why are you flying like this, Kuruvii?” he teased.

“You are the one who tied wings on me—that's why!” she answered mischievously.

“Don't rush; serve calmly! I am not that hungry.”

“When you are hungry, you won't say it! But your face will.”

“You sit and eat too! We can satisfy our hunger while serving each other.”

“It's all right. You finish eating first.”

“I'll eat everything without leaving even one thing!”

“Even the leaf?”

The two laughed aloud. His laughter had the sound of a waterfall; hers, the chime of anklet bells.

Her red-lotus cheeks glowing even more deeply with fullness of joy, Kuruvichchi laid a leaf before Pandaraka Vanniyan and began serving him. Pandaraka Vanniyan was exceedingly fond of porridge made from raw rice and milk. She filled a vessel with it and gave it to him. “You drink a little first!” he said, holding the vessel toward her mouth. With her beautiful hand she gently pushed it back and made him drink first. She watched without taking her eyes off him, delighting in the way he drank.

<!-- source boundary: scan 76 → scan 77 -->

Then she placed palmyra tuber and *avvai kizhangu*, dipped in honey, on the leaf. She watched closely to see whether he was enjoying the tubers, and blossomed with happiness. Next came rice and meat curry; Pandaraka Vanniyan became absorbed in the taste of the meal.

Kuruvichchi, who was serving him, turned her gaze toward the door. She understood that a warrior had arrived in agitation to report some news. Pandaraka Vanniyan did not see him. He was seated facing another direction and eating, so without letting him know, Kuruvichchi Naachchi gestured to the warrior, asking what it was, and came out toward the doorway.

He was the warrior sent by Sundaralingam. “Mother! British soldiers have carried away your grandfather, Sangili Thalapathi! Sundaralingam sent me to tell you at once, and he himself is still there!” the warrior reported.

“All right! All right! Don't let the king hear of this. I will take care of everything,” Kuruvichchi said. She went back inside and resumed serving Pandaraka Vanniyan.

“What is it, Kuruvii? Who was that? Where did he come from? Anything special?” Pandaraka Vanniyan asked casually as he ate.

“Nothing! I had told him to buy sea fish and bring it. All the fishermen have gone to the festival, and this clever fellow has come only now to tell me there is no fish!”

Kuruvichchi managed the situation with that reply.

At last Pandaraka Vanniyan finished eating and insisted that she eat. He stubbornly declared that he himself would

<!-- source boundary: scan 77 → scan 78 -->

serve her. On one side, anguish over what might have happened to her grandfather tormented her. Even so, she decided it was right not to reveal it.

Her thought was that the king, who stood in the field grappling with the country's problems, should not be given a new worry because of a problem in her own household. She resolved inwardly that she herself would go to learn what had happened to her grandfather and rescue him. So she hardened her heart, made herself appear immeasurably happy that her beloved was serving her, and ate.

Among the countless problems Pandaraka Vanniyan already carried, why should Sangili Thalapathi's problem be added too? She could take charge of it herself. What could equal such a spirit of sacrifice in Kuruvichchi?

“You rest. I shall take the offerings from the Kannagi Amman temple to grandfather, give them to him, receive his blessing and come back,” Kuruvichchi said, straightening the bed in the sleeping room.

“Can't I come to receive grandfather's blessing? We could have gone together from Vattrappalai itself and seen him then!” Pandaraka Vanniyan asked.

“Have you forgotten that I already told grandfather, ‘From now on, when the two of us come together to see you, it will be in wedding attire’? Give me leave. I will go and return. You rest!”

“As you command! In any case, take warriors with you for protection. Don't set out alone.”

“I won't be alone. I will take the guards with me.”

<!-- source boundary: scan 78 → scan 79 -->

“Don't go with just two or three guards as usual. Let at least ten warriors come with you. Well? Will you take them yourself, or shall I arrange it and send them?”

“I'll take ten men, as you wish! You rest. If I tell them, won't they come?”

“Kuruvii! Kuruvii! I don't know what reward to give you for your mischief!”

“You've given me yourself—isn't that enough?” Kuruvichchi said, letting a smile shine even through her sorrow. She left the Muthu Maaligai and shut the door behind her.

Pandaraka Vanniyan lay turning on the bed with pleasant thoughts of Kuruvichchi and closed his eyes, trying to sleep. Then the pounding of a hundred horses' hooves sounded in his ears like a trumpet blast. The man who had been trying to sleep started up in alarm.
