# Kuruvichchi Naachchiyar

Taking leave of Sangili Thalapathi, Sundaralingam also learned clearly the route to Pandaraka Vanniyan's palace by way of Vattrappaalai and set out from there.

Once he passed that small village, the route continued through forest, along a single-foot path—and in some places there was not even a path, so he had to place his feet among stones, thorns, shrubs and creepers. Because the feeling that he had to complete his duty kept driving him onward, he walked quickly without even minding the exhaustion left by his storm-tossed boat journey at sea. As he travelled, enjoying the beauty of the densely blooming yellow wild flowers along the roadside and the brilliant white clusters of *erukku* flowers, Sundaralingam felt as though he were in the southern country of Tamil Nadu.

Had not the land of Tamil Nadu and the land of Sri Lanka once, long ago, been joined together and then been cut apart by the force of the sea? That, Sundaralingam felt, was why he could see the character of Tamil Nadu there too.

<!-- source boundary: scan 35 → scan 36 -->

Though his mind was intent on his duty, only the final answer Sangili Thatha had given kept surging through it.

“My granddaughter's name is
Kuruvichchi Naachchiyar!”

A modest house in that small village—and the woman shining like its lamp was Kuruvichchi Naachchiyar. Her grandfather had once been a commander in that region and had lost a leg on the battlefield. His granddaughter and the reigning king were in love. Yet that love had not reached marriage—the bud had become a flower, but had not yet given off its fragrance! For the young king who always had to stand on the battlefront, perhaps Kuruvichchi Naachchiyar was like a patch of shade in which he could rest now and then. She might live in an ordinary house, but she was no ordinary man's granddaughter! Whatever else might be said, she was fortunate indeed—for if she had as her beloved the leader of a warrior clan praised by warriors, was she not fortunate?

There might be any number of women of striking beauty in the Vanni-Mullai region, easily found even in prosperous households; yet if the king had left all those women aside and loved this one woman alone, she must surely possess some special greatness.

Was it beauty? Or exceptional intelligence? Was she a woman of surpassing courage? Or a sacred lamp of sacrifice?

Thinking in this way about Kuruvichchi Naachchiyar, Sundaralingam reached a path deep in the forest. The place he had reached might have been only a stone's throw from the Vattrappaalai Kannagi Amman temple. Yet even in broad daylight, the place where he stood

<!-- source boundary: scan 36 → scan 37 -->

was wrapped in darkness. The forest was so dense that even the sun's light could not show its face!

The sound of the many instruments resounding at the Vattrappaalai Kannagi temple echoed even through that forest. The thunder-like beat of the *murasu* and the great clamour of the *ekkalam*, *tharai*, *kombu* and *thappattai* seemed to make even the branches of the forest trees tremble. The entrance to the Kannagi temple had been decorated with beautiful arches made of thousands of flowers. A kind of reed-grass had been spread all across the ground; the instant people's feet stepped on it, it burst with a crackle like popping corn, and boys and girls clapped their hands, watched the spectacle and delighted in it.

Banana and *kamugu* trees had been tied around the temple as decorations. Large trays were filled with water at the bottom and ghee was poured over it; thick wicks were placed in them and lit, and hundreds of women carried those trays and danced before Amman's shrine.

At the centre of those women danced one who looked like a figure fashioned from sandal-coloured wax, as though half the moon had been split off to form her forehead, with eyes like black bees circling blue flowers. In bearing and in her proud, graceful gait, she seemed to have no equal but herself, and she danced carrying a tray of ghee in her hand.

“Our Kannagi Devi, queen among women in chastity!
The spirit of the noble women of the Tamil race!”

More than a thousand people of the region—men, women, young and old—were held spellbound by those emotion-filled lines of the dance-song. Among the dancing women

<!-- source boundary: scan 37 → scan 38 -->

were four or five Sinhala women as well. They too sang that stirring song and danced just like the Tamil women.

While song, dance and offerings to Kannagi Amman were proceeding with great splendour, an elephant suddenly burst out of the forest in fury and ran toward the temple entrance. Seeing the elephant's sudden arrival, the gathered crowd began to run in all directions. Kuruvichchi Naachchiyar understood at once that the elephant had been terrified by the music and dancing of the Kannagi temple's Visaka festival and by the sounds of the *tharai*, *thappattai* and *murasu*. She leapt onto her horse, which was standing near the temple, taking up at the same time the spear that had been kept beside it. The instant she mounted, she struck the horse firmly and cried, “Kanna! Run!”—and the horse began pursuing the maddened elephant, seeming to tear through not only the wind but the forest itself.

Realizing that it was being attacked, the elephant ran wildly this way and that through the forest. Seeking a suitable place to stand and turn upon the horse and Kuruvichchi Naachchiyar and crush them, its trunk, tusks, head and legs all seemed filled with the urge to fight; it battered, shook and wrecked every tree that came in its way.

Kuruvichchi Naachchiyar drove her horse toward the place where the elephant stood. Seeing the horse coming very close, Kuruvichchi Naachchi upon it, and a spear in her hand, the elephant grew still more terrified

<!-- source boundary: scan 38 → scan 39 -->

and rammed and smashed several trees there, trampling shrubs and creepers as it trumpeted in frenzy and charged off in another direction.

Sundaralingam was coming from that very direction. Thinking that the elephant rushing uncontrollably toward him must be a wild elephant, he drew his sword to protect himself from it. The sword-stroke he aimed at the raging elephant proved useless, because the elephant held a stout tree branch in its trunk. Using that branch as a weapon and wielding it with its trunk, the elephant fought Sundaralingam. Sundaralingam was determined somehow to cut off the elephant's trunk with the sword in his hand. He understood that if he subjected it to a major attack, his own life would no longer be safe.

His brave fight against the elephant did not bring him victory. The elephant dropped the tree branch it had been holding and, with that same trunk, snatched away his sword. Before he could think what to do next, Sundaralingam was caught in the elephant's trunk.

Realizing that his end was near, he cried aloud, “Alas! I am not going to be able to deliver this letter to King Pandaraka Vanniyan!”

Even as he was entering the gateway of death, what tormented that loyal warrior was that he had not been able to complete the task entrusted to him. Sundaralingam, caught in the trunk, became like a tiny insect trapped in a spider's leg

<!-- source boundary: scan 39 → scan 40 -->

and helpless! If the elephant were to whirl its trunk once more, lift him high, dash him to the ground and trample him underfoot, he would be flattened into the earth like a strip of banana stem!

Sundaralingam, that warrior among warriors who stood upright like a lion without bowing his head as one of the commanders of the Panchalankurichi army, was about to be felled and killed by a frenzied elephant. Yet he did not grieve that he was going to die; he grieved only that he could not deliver the letter in his possession to the person for whom it was meant.

A tremendous trumpet! The elephant lifted Sundaralingam very high. If it dashed him down from such a height, he would be smashed to pieces!

Suddenly the spear hurled by Kuruvichchi Naachchiyar struck the elephant's trunk!

The raised trunk fell powerless. In that instant Sundaralingam freed himself from it and got to his feet.

Blood gushed from its trunk, and the elephant, now boiling with still greater rage, turned and ran to attack Kuruvichchi Naachchi on horseback. Thinking that the enraged elephant would ram her and bring her down, Sundaralingam picked up his sword from where it had fallen and stabbed the elephant alternately at the back of its neck and its belly. Already writhing with its trunk wounded, the elephant could endure no more.

At that moment the sound of the great bell of the Kannagi temple reached Kuruvichchi Naachchi's ears. That great bell was rung only on the final sacred day of the Visaka festival. When it rang, it meant that the king had arrived at the temple. So the sound of that great bell

<!-- source boundary: scan 40 → scan 41 -->

made Kuruvichchi Naachchi start in the saddle. “Oh! Has he arrived?” her lips murmured. Not wishing to remain there even for a moment, she turned her horse onto the road to the Kannagi temple and rode away. The horse seemed to fly with her.

“Who is this woman? She saved us from danger—and we saved her from danger! Yet she left without asking even a word about us!” Sundaralingam said to himself as he looked around. At that moment ten warriors carrying spears, swords and lances came toward him. Before Sundaralingam could wonder, “Who are these men all of a sudden?” the warriors had surrounded him. Their leader looked at the others and ordered, “Arrest him!”

“Why should you arrest me?” Sundaralingam asked irritably.

“You have killed a wild elephant belonging to the state! That is a very serious crime! That is why we are arresting you!”

“Listen to what I have to say!”

“No need for talk! Come with us quietly!”
