61

# Smoke Before War!

One by one, the ideas Marthani gave while reclining on Pilimathalavai's lap began to be put into action. Pilimathalavai had once planned to use the English to crush Pandaraka Vanniyan and Vikrama Raja Singan and then become the crowned ruler of Kandy. But after the brutal death of his daughter Piyaseeli, he swore instead to provoke the English into open conflict, drive them out and avenge himself on Lord North. From then on he approved Marthani's suggestions without alteration and kept issuing orders to carry them out.

One evening, in a town called Puththalam—an area under English authority—English traders were carrying goods in numerous carts through the market streets. The townspeople competed to buy those goods at the prices the traders demanded. Only the wares in some carts were sold retail. The English answered arrogantly that the goods in the other carts were meant for wholesale markets in cities such as Kolumbu. As those carts moved along conducting business in the streets—

small groups of ten men each followed them, their bodies wrapped in shawls so that only their faces showed. The white men driving the carts paid no attention to those groups.

People crowded around one retail cart and bought various goods from it. Among them stood a boy, perhaps eight or nine years old, with a dry, bare-looking head. His ribs showed through his body like the teeth of a comb. His belly was sunken and stuck inward like a hollow in a tree. His thin legs looked as though they had been fixed onto a scarecrow in a millet field—and his arms were no different. One might have said that the shadow of poverty itself had taken the shape of that boy and come to stand there.

The boy stared without blinking as people kept buying goods and putting them into baskets or tying them into the folds of their clothes. Watching prepared foods being sold in the English manner seemed only to sharpen the starving child's hunger. It seemed to burn him from within. Believing that the English traders, busy attending to sales from their cart, would not notice him, the boy slipped a hand inside and took a few tubers. Driven by hunger, he bit into one and jumped down to run away.

No one knew how the white trader's eagle eye caught him. The trader leapt from the cart, ran after the boy, seized him by his dry hair and struck him in the face with all his strength.

<!-- source boundary: scan 379 → scan 380 -->

The boy screamed. He cried out for his mother. His hunger vanished; the pain was now enough to make him forget it. His face looked as though it had been bathed in blood. The English merchant snatched back the goods from his hand and turned away.

By then the shawl-covered group had surrounded the Englishman. Blow after blow fell. The Englishman cried, “God!” The men flung aside their shawls and revealed themselves as Kandy warriors.

Those groups of ten who had arrived in different places now blocked cart after cart.

“See this cruelty! They did not even spare a child!” The Kandy warriors lifted the boy up and showed him to the people of Puththalam.

A widowed mother pushed through the crowd and came running. Her legs had little strength, but a mother's love drove her breathlessly toward that place.

“Ayyo! He is my boy!” she screamed, taking the child into her arms.

Now the poor boy had no pain. No hunger. He had gone to a place without pain, hunger, suffering or sorrow.

“You sinners! You have unjustly killed my child!” the mother cried. But before she could finish, the Kandy warriors had surrounded the English carts and begun looting the goods. Terrified by the violent attack, the white traders abandoned the carts and began fleeing Puththalam itself.

The Kandy warriors who had entered Puththalam looking for a way to drag the English into a deliberate quarrel had found a convenient pretext. Poor boy! After that, no one paid attention either to him or to his—

<!-- source boundary: scan 380 → scan 381 -->

pitiful mother who had lost him. The Kandy warriors and the people of Puththalam turned their full attention to stripping the goods from the ownerless carts left in the street.

The moment news of this reached Kolumbu, Lord North erupted in anger. To inflame him further, the looted goods themselves were brought to Kolumbu for sale.

At once a message flew from Lord North to the Kandy king:

“Your warriors have not only waylaid and looted goods belonging to English merchants; as though to insult us, they are bringing them into our very capital, Kolumbu, and selling them. It is therefore unavoidable that we take action. Yet this is our first warning. The Kandy government must immediately pay suitable compensation for the loss.”

The message came into Pilimathalavai's hands. He drew the sword that had lain buried against Marthani's breast and laughed aloud. It was a grotesque laugh, but Marthani praised it as the laughter of Lord Indra and embraced him.

“What reply shall we write?” he asked her.

She picked up a sheet nearby, placed it against Pilimathalavai's back and wrote on it. Then she handed it to him and told him to sign. He signed on behalf of the Kandy king.

“Do not regard the Kandy kingdom as a land that pays tribute to the English. Compensation is out of the question. Thus, on behalf of Kandy King Vikrama Raja Singan—Pilimathalavai!”

When Lord North saw the reply, he was shaken. All his officers were summoned for consul—

<!-- source boundary: scan 381 → scan 382 -->

tation. Major General Macdowall gave Lord North strong support in that council. Even so, he expressed the view that they should not rush into invading the Kandy kingdom. He was, after all, the Major General, and Lord North could not immediately disregard his opinion.

Pilimathalavai, who had expected the English to invade Kandy, was disappointed. He was frustrated that his desire to avenge Lord North for the torture suffered by his daughter had not yet been fulfilled.

He began addressing the people of Kandy as though he were opposing the very penetration of English domination. In many places he raised the heroic cry that the Kandy kingdom would bow to no one. The people of Kandy did not know that he was staging a drama of freedom-fever for his own selfish scheme. Vikrama Raja Singan was astonished by his sudden change, yet he was pleased that his chief minister had become an enemy of the English.

Then, one night in the capital of Kandy, a great uproar and confusion broke out in the quarters where the English lived.

Kandy warriors had been unleashed on Pilimathalavai's orders. Doors of white households were pounded. Doors that did not open were broken down. English women became prey to the fierce lust of the Kandy warriors—to such an extent that scarcely a woman escaped being dragged into their embrace. Men who saw their wives and women being seized and mauled before their eyes tried to resist in anguish, then collapsed and fled in confusion that night. The whole white settlement became a hall for the frenzy of lust, filled with shouting and wailing.

<!-- source boundary: scan 382 → scan 383 -->

Not only did the news reach Kolumbu; the affected Parangiyar families themselves took refuge in Lord North's mansion.

“Still more patience?” Lord North asked Major General Macdowall.

Macdowall drew his sword, raised it above his head and trumpeted, “Ready to invade Kandy!”

A plan was drawn up for two divisions of three thousand soldiers to attack Kandy.

When Pilimathalavai heard that the English army was moving toward Kandy, he was delighted. Saying that the time had come to answer revenge with revenge, he embraced Marthani tightly. On her advice, he obtained an urgent letter from Vikrama Raja Singan asking Pandaraka Vanniyan for help and sent it toward Mullaitheevu. Marthani herself set out carrying that letter to meet Pandaraka Vanniyan.
