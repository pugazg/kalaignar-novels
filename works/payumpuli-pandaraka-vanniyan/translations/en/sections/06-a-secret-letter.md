## 2

# A Secret Letter!

The year 1798!

Like trying to hold back the flood of a wild river with one's hands—such were palaiyakkarar rulers like Kattabomman. At the same time that they were fighting the authority and domination of the British, who had arrived in India as traders, the realm of Pandaraka Vanniyan and the kingdom of Kandy in Sri Lanka were also sounding the call to battle, refusing to make room for British domination.

One night that year, three men were walking swiftly along the Rameswaram seashore, looking all around them. One of the three wore a turban of thick silk cloth. An upper garment with a gleaming zari border fluttered on his shoulder in the sea breeze. His tightly wrapped veshti ended below the knee, gripping the calf, and gave his figure a stately bearing. His slightly long moustache and large eyes revealed his valour. At his waist, on one side, was a sword in its sheath—and on the other, a rolled-up letter kept securely!

The men walking with him, however, wore ordinary clothes. They followed close beside him with humility, showing him respect.

<!-- source boundary: scan 21 → scan 22 -->

Though it was night, darkness had not spread completely and swallowed the world. Stars shone in the sky like grains of cooked rice scattered over a leaf. Moonlight, from the moon moving along the avenue of the sky while hiding half of itself and showing the rest, spread across the shore and across the sea that longed to embrace the beach with its wave-arms. The three men walking along the shore could therefore be seen clearly. After walking some distance, they approached a boat lying near the shore and stopped beside it. All three put their hands to the boat and pushed it from the shore toward the sea. Though the boat did not immediately enter the sea completely, it began to float in the water as though yielding to the strength of the three men. Even then they kept turning their eyes anxiously over the shore. When no one came into sight, all three let out a deep breath at the same time.

One of the two men in ordinary clothes climbed into the boat first and took up the oar lying there. For a second there was a question of who would get in next! Before that could be decided, the man in the boat looked at the other two and warned softly, “Hush! Hide yourselves for a moment!” The two quickly moved to the far side of the boat and hid themselves while standing in the seawater. The man who had climbed into the boat also concealed himself inside it and watched the shoreline intently.

The hoofbeats of four or five horses disturbed the stillness of the night all at once, as white soldiers passed

that place while surveying the shoreline. Guns stretched out in the hands of those white soldiers. The three men remained hidden until the soldiers had passed beyond the place, then returned to what they had been doing. The man in the boat rose slowly, stretched out his head, and gestured to the other two, who stood with their legs wet in the water, to climb aboard. When both tried to leap into the boat, the man already inside looked at them and said, “One at a time! Let Sundaralingam get in first!”

As if that itself said without saying that the man in the silk turban was Sundaralingam, he alone climbed in and sat down! Finally the third man also boarded and took an oar in his hand. Though those two were boatmen, they had come as Sundaralingam's protectors! And though they had come to protect him, they must have been friends who could move with Sundaralingam as equals. That was why, soon after the boat left the shore, Sundaralingam loosened his lap-cloth, took out betel leaves and areca shavings for himself, and also spread lime on betel leaves, placed the shavings inside, folded them and handed them to the two men who were rowing the boat—then pinched off the tobacco they needed as well.

Three men leave the Rameswaram shore in a boat—travelling at night—climbing aboard in fear that someone might see them—hiding when they spot the horses carrying the white soldiers—who are they?

Certainly those two—yes, those men in ordinary clothes who wield the oars—are not merely boatmen,

<!-- verified source continuation: scan 23 → scan 24 -->

but Sundaralingam's companions as well! His comrades as well! One is named Maruthan! The other is named Bharathan! Both are fishermen! Owners of that boat! Why, owners of this soil too!

Then who is this Sundaralingam? Is it enough merely to say that he is a friend of Maruthan and Bharathan? No! Certainly not!

Sundaralingam, a close friend of Veerapandiya Kattabomman's beloved commander Vellaiyathevan and a warrior like him, was one of the lions of the Panchalankurichi battlefront! Born into an oppressed community, he was a great warrior who flexed his broad shoulders so that foreigners should not occupy this Tamil soil!

Why did that Sundaralingam undertake this overnight boat journey from Rameswaram with the help of Maruthan and Bharathan?

The reason can be understood only if the letter tucked into his waist is opened!

Like children slipping from a father's shoulder and leaping into a mother's lap, the boat climbed the waves, fell, rose again, and continued its journey.

“The sky gives rain,
the earth gives yield;
for the king's tribute,
why this kisti, man!”

Maruthan and Bharathan sang the song loudly, taking turns. The sound of that song, and the question it raised, went unheard across the vast stretch of sea! But Sundaralingam's heart alone was seething. For was he not one who, together with the Panchalankurichi army,

<!-- verified source continuation: scan 24 → scan 25 -->

raised that very question before the Parangiyars at the points of sword and spear?

He had to travel through the whole night and reach Mullaitheevu in Sri Lanka! From Rameswaram he had to pass Paruthithurai, turn south and reach Mullaitheevu. He had to complete the task for which he had come and immediately return to Tamilakam. His eagerness and concern for that task kept urging him onward.

Had not the stars in the sky shone like grains of rice scattered upon a leaf? It seemed that arrogant black clouds had picked them up and eaten them! The maiden moon, which had roamed while hiding half of herself and showing the rest, seemed to have fled in fear and hidden herself too! Then came, first, a drizzle! Next, wind! Then heavy rain! A storm!

The boat struggled in the storm and rain. Maruthan covered Sundaralingam with a bamboo mat that was in the boat.

“You two are getting soaked, my friends!” Sundaralingam said with concern.

“It's all right! Don't worry about us! We are kattumarams that soak in the sea itself! Don't think I covered you with the bamboo mat because I was worried about you! It's only so that the letter you are keeping in your lap doesn't get wet!” Maruthan said mischievously.

“Yes, yes! That's true! A very important message! It has to reach a very important person!” said Sundaralingam.

“You came in this boat trusting us, didn't you?

<!-- verified source continuation: scan 25 → scan 26 -->

What would you do if we grabbed you, threw you into the sea, took that letter and read it?”

Bharathan asked as though threatening him!

Sundaralingam knew that Bharathan was joking and that such joking was his habit, so he burst out laughing. But in the roar of the storm and the heavy rain, even inside the boat the sound of that laughter could not be heard properly.

“Isn't one Ettappan enough for King Kattabomman? Are you now going to sprout up as another one for me?”

Sundaralingam asked, gripping Bharathan by the shoulder and shaking him!

At that moment Maruthan and Bharathan were struggling desperately to control the boat. Both had become afraid that it might capsize at any moment.

It seemed as though the storm, with its cruel hands, was scooping up the whole sea and hurling it upon the mass of clouds—and as though a little coconut-shell bowl were tossing helplessly up and down in it: such were that scene and that boat!

The same condition continued until dawn! It was only many nazhigai after daybreak that the storm and rain subsided, and the three men realised with joy that they had drawn close to Mullaitheevu.

Sundaralingam could not contain his excitement! Sitting in the boat, he stretched his arms around Maruthan and Bharathan on either side and embraced them. “Comrades! I can never forget your service! Nor can I forget your courage!” he praised them.

“Sundaralingam! Can't you tell us at least now about that letter?” Maruthan asked, and Bharathan nodded in agreement with the question.

<!-- source boundary: scan 26 → scan 27 -->

“This is a letter that Vellaiyathevan, commander of Panchalankurichi, wrote in his own hand. If I reveal what is in it, I will become not only a traitor to the country but a traitor to my friend as well,” said Sundaralingam.

“Don't reveal the contents! You mustn't! But can't you at least tell us, your friends of so long whom you trust, who the letter is addressed to? The reason we ask is this: if something suddenly happens to you, shouldn't we deliver the letter to the proper addressee?”

said Maruthan.

Sundaralingam smiled. Then he took the letter tucked into his waist, held it in his hand and said, “There's no need for me to tell you! Look—the address is written right here on the envelope!” He showed only that much to the two of them.

“Don't! Don't! Why should we know such a great secret—especially a state secret?” Maruthan and Bharathan said, turning their faces away.

Smiling, Sundaralingam said, “Hey... hey... don't put on airs! Even I don't know what Vellaiyathevan has written inside the letter! It's all right—the whole household won't drown just because you read the address! Read it!” He lifted the envelope as though to thrust it against their faces. Both narrowed their eyes and read the address written on the outside of the letter.

“To King Kulasekaram Vairamuthu, known as Pandaraka Vanniyan” was the address written there.
