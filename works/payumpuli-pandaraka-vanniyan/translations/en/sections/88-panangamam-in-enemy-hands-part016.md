As Kuruvichchi approached the border of Panangamam with her forces, she seethed at the sight of the English flag flying over Panangamam fort. But, quite contrary to anything she had expected, the English troops let her and the warriors who had come with her pass without the slightest obstruction.

However power-hungry the English might be, she felt a certain pride as she reflected that they had not prevented her from paying the final rites to the dead Oomaichchi Naachchiyar, and inwardly praised their sense of civility.

On both sides, as though forming a guard of honour for Kuruvichchi, the white soldiers sat on horseback with their guns raised in their hands while at the same time bowing their heads in salute.

The moment Kuruvichchi entered Panangamam fort, an English officer looked at her and said, “I request you to wait here for a moment.” Kuruvichchi did not refuse. She stood there. She watched. Four or five British soldiers came and stood before her, carrying white trays heaped with flowers.

Kuruvichchi looked at them in silence, as though asking, “What for?”

Meanwhile the English officer who had respectfully escorted her there said, “To pay the last respects to Queen Oomaichchi Naachchiyar!”

Kuruvichchi scooped the flowers from those trays into her hands.

“Which way must I go to see Oomaichchi Naachchiyar?”

She walked down a long corridor in the direction the officer pointed.

Nowhere inside the fort did it appear that Oomaichchi Naachchiyar’s body had been laid out. Kuruvichchi went along the fort corridor and through several other narrow, cave-like passages, then suddenly realized that she had reached the rear entrance of the fort.

“Here—this is Oomaichchi’s tomb!” Kuruvichchi looked at the tomb, which had been built up into a high mound of earth, plastered and sealed, and without realizing it her eyes became waterfalls.

An oil lamp burned atop Oomaichchi Naachchi’s tomb even in broad daylight. In its light, Kuruvichchi stood before the tomb with the feeling that she was looking upon Oomaichchi’s face. When her eyes closed of their own accord, the drops of water struggling to escape from within them broke past her lashes and leapt down onto the soft, beautiful, deep-red mounds of her cheeks. She scattered over Oomaichchi Naachchi’s tomb the flowers she had gathered in her hands and bowed in homage.

Then, following the English officer, Kuruvichchi entered the fort again by the rear way.

It was a small, attractive chamber. Kuruvichchi was told that it was in that very chamber that Oomaichchi Naachchiyar had given her life fighting the English. She noticed dried drops of blood on the wall.

“This is the blood that spattered from Oomaichchi Naachchiyar’s body when she fell, cut down by a sword!”

The officer gave the information like an utterly emotionless block of wood.

<!-- source boundary: scan 452 → scan 453 -->

Kuruvichchi went to the wall, scraped off those drops of blood with her own hands, and placed them upon her forehead as a warrior’s tilak.

“Sit here in this chamber. The food will be ready soon,” said the officer, pointing to a chair.

“That is all right. Thank you very much for your hospitality. I am leaving,” said Kuruvichchi.

“When you heard the word ‘food,’ perhaps you mistook it for a feast! From now on, you are to be our permanent guest!”

From the officer’s arrogant words Kuruvichchi understood the truth: they had trapped her by cunning.

“What are you saying? Are you calling my arrest a ‘permanent feast’?”

Without even finishing her words, Kuruvichchi drew her sword, raised it, and faced the officer.

Not only that officer but all the English soldiers there burst into guffaws loud enough to shake the chamber.

Through the sound of their laughter came, terrifyingly, the clash of swords and the thunder of cannon outside the fort.

Kuruvichchi eagerly turned her face toward the directions from which those sounds came.

The British officer said mockingly, “There is nothing to be afraid of! The warriors who came with Kuruvichchi have been caught by our men like mice in a trap and are struggling. It is taking our men only a little time to deal with them. Poor innocent fellows!”

Kuruvichchi Naachchiyar stood in that chamber with her sword raised.

<!-- source boundary: scan 453 → scan 454 -->

Yet, without the least concern about the sword she held aloft, the English soldiers closed in around her.

“What do you say? You are our prisoner now!”

Hearing that, she lowered the sword she had been holding high and kissed its hilt.

The next instant, a storm began to rage inside that hall of the fort. Before the whirl of her sword as she seemed to fly through the air without setting foot on the ground, the English soldiers faltered. They suffered.

Yet she was only one woman—and there were thousands of Englishmen to oppose and bring her down. Once again the battle at Panangamam fort reached its fiercest pitch.
