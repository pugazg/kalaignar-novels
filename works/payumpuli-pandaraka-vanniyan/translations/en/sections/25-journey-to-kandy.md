# Journey to Kandy!

Whenever shocking news struck him, whenever confusion surrounded and besieged him, Pandaraka Vanniyan had a habit of taking up whatever lay to hand and scribbling something. Those scribbles never failed to shine as lines of poetry—often to an extent he himself had not expected, enough to astonish him. For a while, the crises and confusions he faced would then withdraw from him, leaving behind a sense of relief.

He was already suffering under the grief of having quarrelled with Kuruvichchi Naachchi and parted from her. While that pain pressed and kneaded his heart, he learned—from the mouth of his own sister—the news, more poisonous than poison itself, that his sister Nallanaachchi was in love with Kaakkai Vanniyan. It was as though thousands upon thousands of sledgehammer blows had struck his head at once.

He stood silent for a while, grinding his teeth. Then he left the place and entered his private chamber. Nallanaachchi and Oomaichchi Naachchi looked at one another in trembling distress. They had come thinking they were bringing good news, and now matters had turned out like this. Neither spoke. Exchanging looks, and deciding that they would talk later, the two sisters went into the inner part of the palace.

Pandaraka Vanniyan stared at the sky through the lattice window of the palace and dropped himself into the chair in his room with a thump.

A collection of palm-leaf sheets filled with the verses he had already written lay spread out before him and drew his attention.

He brought his gaze back from the sky, picked up a leaf and began to write. How many problems! How many confusions! How many pressures! How many expectations! How many shocks! To write in the midst of all this? Yes! Writing alone had always been the medicine that relieved his weariness, turmoil and pain. And so he sought the help of that great medicine which dispelled fatigue and drove away worry.

What he wrote were indeed lines of anguished verse. Yet because they were a child born from the womb of his imagination, giving birth to them brought him pleasure, consolation and joy.

The man who makes life itself a struggle
lives on,
finding delight in a flash of lightning
in the pitch-dark night.
And bound up with that lightning,
even when thunder crashes upon his head,
he survives without dying
and lives on, feeding upon fire!
Some who came beside me, vowing to give their lives,
remain beside me only to dig my grave!
Some born beside me as my own kin
have become diseases born with me only to kill!

<!-- source boundary: scan 130 → scan 131 -->

To stand against all this,
who will give me a heart that can bear anything?
Even if a crore of vultures
try to tear a hole in the sky, I say they cannot!
Even if a crore of foreign enemies
come to plunder Tamil honour,
they will only be defeated!

Kulasekaram Vairamuthu Pandarakan read over the verses he had finished writing once or twice. The weariness that had been weighing upon his heart now fled. A new strength arose in his body. He stacked the leaf he had written with the old ones, shook out his arms and legs, and rose as though newly invigorated. He struck the bell in the room. Before its sound had died away, two warriors raced one another to stand before him and bow.

“I leave for Kandy in the morning. A force of one hundred warriors must come with me for protection. Make the arrangements!”

Pandarakan gave the order in a majestic voice. The warriors flew away like the wind.

But the relief he had gained by turning poetry into medicine would not remain. The thought of the relationship between Kaakkai Vanniyan and Nallanaachchi tossed him about again.

“How was my sister deceived? Has she never seen that scoundrel Kaakkai Vanniyan all these days? He must have trapped her by calling himself a merchant! Love—for a man like him? Love! What a sacred word it is! A man who stains even the purity of that word—how

<!-- source boundary: scan 131 → scan 132 -->

treacherously he has deceived Nallanaachchi! Behind the curtain called love, Kaakkai Vanniyan has drawn my sister into his hands only to carry out some great political conspiracy! How am I to tell Nallanaachchi this truth? If I tell her, will she be unable to bear it and take her own life? Then how am I to rescue her from this web of intrigue? Very well—let what will happen, happen! I am here! There is a sword in my hand! And even if that is taken from me, there is a fearless heart that no one can seize!”

Thinking thus, he tossed sleeplessly upon his bed, somehow passed the night, rose at daybreak and prepared for the journey to Kandy.

At his command, one hundred powerful warriors sat mounted on horses, strong weapons in their hands, and formed ranks before the palace. Dressed suitably both for the journey and for facing any enemy who might come against him, Pandaraka Vanniyan came to the palace gate carrying a gleaming sword. His two sisters followed him like mute women, saying nothing.

Before leaping onto his decorated warhorse, he looked once at his sisters. Unable to bear the light in his eyes, Nallanaachchi and Oomaichchi Naachchi lowered their heads and moved slowly. As though expressing the grief heaped within his heart, he gave a small clearing of the throat. It sounded softly like the first murmur of thunder among clouds gathering for rain. The two sisters understood that some turmoil in their brother's heart was dancing Bharatanatyam with anklets tied on, stamping its feet again and again.

Then he thrust the palm-leaf scroll into Nallanaachchi's hand. As she received it, pearls rose in her eyes, rolled down her cheeks, and she looked at him.

“You drew a picture and showed it to me, did you not? Here—I have drawn a picture too! Read it! This is the picture of my life! I am leaving!”

Nallanaachchiyar began to read it. Oomaichchi Naachchi moved close beside her and stared at it too. They were the verses he had written.

Once again the sisters looked at one another with pale faces.

“I am going to Kandy—urgently! Stay here until I return!” Pandarakan told them, then sprang onto his horse.

Who held the right to Kandy's throne? Kannusami? Muthusami? The moment news reached the British in Colombo Fort that Pandaraka Vanniyan was setting out for Kandy to intervene and decide this great question, they made plans to stop that intervention by any means.

Kannusami did not wish to lie at the feet of the British; therefore, he must not be allowed to assume royal authority. The British officers gathered in Colombo decided that Muthusami—accustomed to bowing to the enemy and exceptionally skilled at betraying his own people—should instead be crowned. They therefore considered how to prevent Pandaraka Vanniyan from reaching Kandy. A few officers proposed going with troops and driving him away. But such a plan now,

<!-- source boundary: scan 133 → scan 134 -->

some officers argued, would lead to a great war. If the kingdom of Kandy and Pandarakan's kingdom of Mullaitheevu fought together, defeating them would be difficult. They must therefore be faced separately in war. First, the conduct of the two rulers should be watched; only after that would severe action succeed. And so the council of British officers gathered in Colombo reached its final decision: let Pandarakan come to Kandy—then let us trap him in our web of conspiracy.

Untroubled by any of these things, Pandaraka Vanniyan's journey continued toward Kandy.
