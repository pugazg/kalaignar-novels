23

# The Grammar of Friendship!

“Bravo, my king!” said Pilimathala, shaking his head, which resembled a collection pot, and moving aside to give Kannusami room to spring upon Pandaraka Vanniyan.

But contrary to what both he and his bewitching daughter Piyasili expected, Kannusami—Vikrama Rajasingan—swung the raised sword straight at Piyasili's neck. Shocked as though a thousand lightning bolts had struck at once, Piyasili ducked suddenly, clasped his legs and wailed:

“My lord! Have you gone mad? Are you determined to make true the saying that the blame falls on one person and the punishment on another? Can you suspect me, when I have built a temple in my heart for your image and worship it there? I lie thinking that the mere touch of your majestic body is bliss equal to drinking the nectar of heaven. Is this the gift of love you give me?”

Her husband did not melt. He laughed aloud, and sparks seemed to fly from that laughter.

“What are you acting at, woman? I have heard plenty of stories about false chaste wives like you, who keep lakes and ponds behind their eyes and open them whenever needed, letting the water run out through canals. What flows from your eyes now is not tears—it is a spring from a poisonous pool!”

“You speak to me like this? You, who used to savour me again and again, saying, ‘This pleasure is mine alone’—you speak about me this way?”

“I know you, woman! I know very well that you seek a new variety every day! I know that only a few men remain in this Kandy palace who have not become slaves to your bewitching display and lost their senses. What can you do? You grew up that way—or you were raised that way! You have one nature: whatever man you see must grow feverish with desire at the movement of your body and stand yearning for you. You must torment him more and more. That madman must circle you again and again, his very self, body and heart melting for you. You take a special pleasure in making many men wander after you like this. Each day, choosing one man from that lust-maddened crowd, becoming intoxicated and revelling with him—that is your pastime!”

“Alas! You are pouring molten lead into my ears! I adorn myself and dress in finery only for you, my lord, only for you! If crows and eagles watch from afar the sacred offering prepared for God, does the offering lose its sanctity? I am the offering prepared for you—yes, for this god!”

“An offering is precisely something shown before God and then divided among the devotees! And you have so many devotees!”

Pilimathala, who had not interrupted their exchange until then, suddenly thrust himself in. It seemed an idea had sprouted briskly in his furnace of a brain.

“My honoured son-in-law! You are also my king. I shall forget for the moment that Piyasili is my daughter and speak to you now as a minister speaks to a ruler. Let this woman Piyasili be whatever she is—a demon of seduction, a lustful maiden who loves every man she sees! I myself am ashamed to hear a husband speak so degradingly about his wife. Never mind. I have only one question now. Piyasili is a demon of lust! A reveler of the bedchamber! An Urvashi who ruins the town! Heap as many such epithets upon her as you wish. But however bad she may be, what of this sinner who, without even the thought that she is his friend's wife, dragged this fallen woman to his bed as though saying, ‘Come, fallen woman—let us fall a little further’? How can you forgive him? Can there be a greater betrayal of a friend? Even if she herself came here and tormented him, what should he have done? Should not this Pandarakan have said, ‘Do not remain here! Leave at once! I will not betray my friend!’?”

Having at last deployed that weapon, Pilimathala stroked his bald head in expectation: surely Kannusami would now be struck where he could not evade the blow.

“Minister!” Vikrama Rajasingan said in a firm, weighted voice as he turned toward him.

“Do you know an important episode from the Mahabharata? Duryodhana and Karna were friends dearer than life to one another. One day, while Duryodhana was away, his wife Bhanumathi and Karna

were seated playing sokkattan. The game reached a point where Bhanumathi seemed about to lose, while Karna played in the delight of approaching victory. Just then Duryodhana returned. Bhanumathi saw him coming. Out of respect for her husband, she rose. Karna, who had not noticed Duryodhana's arrival, mistakenly thought she had stood up merely to avoid defeat, and he pulled at the pearl necklace she wore. The string broke and the pearls scattered across the floor.

“Duryodhana then came and stood beside them. Karna was shaken. But Duryodhana, knowing there was not a speck of stain in Karna's heart, asked with a pure mind, ‘Shall I pick up the scattered pearls? Shall I gather and string them?’ Minister! In friendship, I am that Duryodhana, and Pandaraka Vanniyan is Karna. But your daughter Piyasili cannot be compared with that golden embodiment of chastity, Bhanumathi!”

Kannusami had scarcely finished speaking when Pandaraka Vanniyan, his whole body thrilled with emotion, ran to his dearest friend and embraced him.

Seeing that the conspiracy-drama had ended in a way entirely different from what he had intended, Pilimathala placed a consoling hand on his daughter Piyasili's shoulder and led her away.

“Come, child! God is watching everything. He will take care of it!” he said movingly as they left the room.
