# Foreword

Muthamizh Arignar Kalaignar M. Karunanidhi has created this historical novel, _Payumpuli Pandaraka Vanniyan_, which kindles the yearning for freedom, and presented it to the Tamil people.

Though creating a historical novel is not easy, it is an art he has mastered. He gathers various sources concerning the historical background required for it—the country, the period, the political setting, the situation in neighbouring lands, the people's way of life, religious customs and practices, the nature of the places through which the characters move—studies them and gains clarity, sees them as scenes, and paints them as a beautiful picture so that they unfold, layer after layer, in the reader's mind.

The artistic sensibility that created _Romapuri Pandiyan_, which evokes the proud setting of Sangam-age Tamilakam; _Thenpandi Singam_, which portrays the condition of Tamilakam when British domination was spreading across Tamil soil; and _Ponnar-Sankar_, which displays Tamil valour through folk tales handed down from generation to generation, has painted this work as well.

We know that Thanjavur Tamil University presented the Rajaraja Cholan Award to _Thenpandi Singam_, and that Vice-President Shankar Dayal Sharma, speaking while presenting it, praised and congratulated Kalaignar as a Muthamizh Arignar.

Vairamuthu, guardian of Adangapattru in Vanni Nadu, a part of the Tamil Eelam land, became historically renowned under the distinguished name Pandaraka Vanniyan. Like Veerapandiya Kattabomman, who fought the British with the aim that British rule and domination should not be allowed to take root in Tamil Nadu,

Pandaraka Vanniyan fought in Sri Lanka during the same period to defend the rights of the soil. He was keenly interested in the political developments taking place in Tamil Nadu and was also a friend of Sundaralingam, one of Kattabomman's military commanders.

Like a black cobra coiled around the leg of Pandaraka Vanniyan, who fought with valour and determination to defend the rights of the soil, there stood Kakkai Vanniyan, guardian of Karikkattu Moolai in Eelam—the man who ought never to have betrayed him.

Pandaraka Vanniyan's beloved, Kuruvichchi Naachchi, and his dear younger sisters Nalla Naachchi and Oomaichchi Naachchi are warrior women of noble character.

Kannusamy, who assumed the responsibility of rule in Kandy in central Sri Lanka and, when crowned, took the name Vikrama Raja Singan, was a close friend of Pandaraka Vanniyan. He was committed to protecting the rights of the country.

The minister of that country was Pilimathalavai, a man of treacherous and poisonous thought—a human spider. He was of the Sinhala people. Supporting the aims of the British, he planned to lay out the carpet for their domination and, driven by the ambition to seize power for himself through it, spun a web of conspiracy.

Pilimathalavai's daughter was Piyasali. Piyasali, a maiden graced with youthful beauty, was skilled at bestowing that beauty without restraint upon anyone—a bewitcher of people, a destroyer of people. Pilimathalavai was prepared to use his own daughter Piyasali as a pawn, helping make her a feast for many, and through that to fulfil his craving for office.

The British commanders' scheme to bend and plunder the country; the domestic conspirators who aided it; and the heroes and heroines who sought to cut through the conspiracy-net they had deliberately woven, dedicated themselves to protecting the rights of the country, fought, and gave their lives—this epic, radiant with such characters, resounds as a _parani_ of Mullaitheevu.

<!-- source boundary: scan 7 → scan 8 -->

Although historical events form its focal points, Kalaignar's imaginative power adds flavour through the force of his writing: in arranging the thoughts, actions, events and consequences of the characters; in developing them with anticipation; in describing them stage by stage; and in creating wonder.

Human life takes form in “love” and shows its colours in life. How many kinds of love, how many states, how many flavours! All of them are portrayed in ways suited to the nature of the characters.

The pure-hearted love of those who do not live for themselves; the love that trembles when a woman who gave her heart to a scoundrel who concealed himself behind the guise of goodness learns the truth; the love staged as a daily drama to satisfy one's desire and achieve one's purpose; and frenzied love that revels in lust, a distortion of love—the manner in which these are described makes both their nobility and their baseness clear.

Tamils have lacked interest in recording historical events or in honouring and learning the history of their ancestors. As a result, their history is available only as a shattered picture. Because of this, Tamils have also failed to know and value themselves as a people with historical greatness. In a society where ethnic consciousness has faded, unity becomes rare, concern for the common good and for safeguarding the rightful life of one's own people weakens, and betrayal raises its head. Those who look closely at Tamil history will understand that the failure to possess ethnic consciousness paved the way for their decline.

Nalla Naachchiyar, betrayed, turns to the scoundrel Kakkai Vanniyan, who says, “Am I not a Tamil too?”, and angrily says:

“You are a Tamil! By race, by language, by the blood that runs through your body, you are a Tamil! But a Tamil with neither fire nor shame! A Tamil who worships at another's feet for money! A Tamil who pawns his honour for office!”

One is made to wonder how many people in today's Tamil land those words might fit. Above all rises the yearning: may that thought never be lost. Another arena. In relation to Pandaraka Vanniyan, the following is placed as Kattabomman's speech in Tamil Nadu, on the soil of Kayathar, when the white Major Bannerman accused Veerapandiya Kattabomman:

<!-- verified source continuation: scan 8 → scan 9 -->

“If there is a people anywhere in the world that failed to prosper because of disunity, it is the Tamil people! Without enemies having to struggle to divide them, it is also the Tamil people who, of their own accord, go to the very height of bitterness, jealousy and rancour and stand divided! The result is this: Veerapandiyan stands in the cage before you, while so many Tamil petty rulers stand watching it as a spectacle.”

Is this not the political spectacle of Tamil Nadu today!

Kalaignar skilfully conveys that the grandeur of Pandaraka Vanniyan shines precisely in this feeling.

“The greatness of womanhood”  
“Love of country”  
“Ethnic consciousness”

“Tamil culture” — all of these are fashioned in an unwearying, honeyed Tamil style so as to impress themselves upon the reader's mind. This novel is a feast for the reader; a medicine that instils self-confidence in the Tamil people.

A master of many fields, a genius of the world of art, a sovereign of the world of letters, and a Kalaignar who adds distinction to Tamil in the twentieth century—he has also made the heart of a writer and the heart of a leader, as feelings he himself has experienced, find a place in the expression of Pandaraka Vanniyan's thoughts. That is Kalaignar.

My congratulations to him!

My praise for his writing!

My tribute to his service!

Chennai.  
5.5.1991

With affection,  
K. Anbazhagan
