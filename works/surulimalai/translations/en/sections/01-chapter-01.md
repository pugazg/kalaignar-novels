# Surulimalai

## 1

As usual, Surulimalai sat on the platform beneath the neem tree, playing his flute.

In the mornings that platform belonged to him alone. In the evenings it became the villagers' place for catching the breeze, passing the time and exchanging village gossip. Whenever necessary, the village panchayat itself met on that platform. During the annual arrow-shooting festival, they would decorate the deity upon the horse-vahana, bring the image there, set it down and conduct the worship.

So, beneath that neem tree which held such importance for the village, Surulimalai spent his mornings. That luxuriantly grown neem was a widow! Yes; within a day or two of the marriage, the neem's husband—the arasu tree—had withered away! Bereft of its arasu, the neem grew alone, savouring Surulimalai's flute every day. It would be no exaggeration to imagine that even the bitterness in its trunk, branches, leaves, fruit and flowers had disappeared, so sweet was the music it had drunk! Now that it has been discovered that trees and plants breathe, perhaps Surulimalai wondered whether some researcher would one day announce that they could hear sounds as well—for whatever reason, beneath the neem he would lose himself completely in his bamboo flute. He did not know even the alphabet of the musical grammar that trained musicians had devised for flute-playing.

He would not understand the special rule Ilango Adigal lays down for the flautist in the *Silappatikaram* in those lines. He was, after all, a Tamil youth who could not even understand what the word *Silappatikaram* meant.

We say the cuckoo sings—the peacock dances—and describe a waterfall as singing a *sindhu*. Neither cuckoo, peacock nor waterfall studies musical grammar in order to shower us with musical delight. Yet imaginative people turn the cuckoo's song and the peacock's dance into comparisons for the artistic feasts offered by human beings. Surulimalai's flute had just such an attraction, and just such a power to captivate those who heard it!

Does anyone listen to an owl's song, keeping perfect beat, raga and sruti, and exclaim, "Ah! What musical grammar!"? Even if a turkey danced without slipping from *laya* or losing *bhava*, would anyone praise it?

The natural distinction possessed by the peacock and the cuckoo somehow belonged to Surulimalai's flute as well. That sweet note mingled with the soft, cool breeze beneath the neem tree and, in the morning, gave fresh strength to those heading toward the fields and those hurrying out to labour.

There were some who did not fail to mutter when they saw him. "Look at our great Krishna Paramatma—playing his flute in Gokulam!" How could he possibly earn praise from such great men who mocked him this way?

<!-- source boundary: scan 15 → scan 16 -->

"A fine son Ponnaiya and Pooncholai have produced—forgotten lineage, gothram, family, everything, and all he does is play the flute, the flute!" People would pass by taunting him like this, and Surulimalai was not unaware of it.

He treated all such talk with complete indifference. Perhaps he did not want to answer them and cultivate enmity; he lived as though there were only himself and the neem tree. The agitation and restless excitement natural to hot young blood and a throbbing age—none of it existed in him!

A fifteen-year-old youth, he appeared as calm as a ripened old man. Is not the beauty of the face a reflection of what lies within? There was no one in that village to recognize his quiet, deep nature. So some even called him *sonippillai*.

When he was still at the crawling age and his first teeth had appeared, the family cooked milk-kozhukattai and offered it to the deity. His mother would boast to others that even then she had discovered what sort of character he would have. Before the family deity they placed an offering of kozhukattai; then, on a winnowing tray, they set a toy, a one-rupee coin, a knife and one kozhukattai. They let the child Suruli crawl toward them to see which object he would eagerly take. The child crawled straight over and picked up the kozhukattai. From that moment the neighbours and his mother decided he was destined to be a real *Saappaattu Raman*—a lover of food. They began raising him according to that verdict. And he grew up worrying about nothing and refusing to let himself be caught by any responsibility.

The young man, whose face always carried a budding smile and whose eyes seemed to reveal deep thought, was lost in his flute that morning too, as on every other morning.

At dawn, before he left the house, his loving elder sister Ponmani—who had risen ahead of him, swept the house and threshold, drawn a kolam in the street, and begun the work of making pots—said, "Don't go anywhere without eating! I've kept good leftover rice, buffalo curd and kidarangai pickle to go with it. Eat before you go!"

"I'll eat after a little while, Akka!" he said and started away. She stopped him. "Thambi, sit here for a while and play the flute!" she requested.

"Can't do that!" he said as casually as could be, and came away to the neem tree. There he filled the place with music. From time to time he looked up into the neem tree.

The first time he looked up, surprise appeared on his face.

The next time, a little fear raised its head and vanished at once.

The third time, he looked with indifference.

After that he did not look up again; he gave his full attention to playing the flute.

"Ah! Bale! Bale!! Magnificent!" cried Theechatti Singaram as he came there clapping his hands.

"Come, Anna! Haven't seen you for so long—where've you been?" Surulimalai asked eagerly.

"Oh, leave it, thambi—why ask me that? Fire-pot dances in fifteen villages! Every fellow in every village wants only Theechatti<!-- verified source join: scan 17→18 --> Singaram! I'm one man from one village—how many people can I dance for? So off I went. Only got back today. You know something, thambi? That's why I was looking for your house," Singaram said, laying out his preamble.

"You came looking for our house and landed under the neem tree! Leave that aside—what is it, Anna?" Seeing the eagerness in Surulimalai's question, Singaram continued with a long introduction.

"I'd gone to the Kali festival at Kattur. Aiyo! What a crowd—no room even for a sesame seed to fall!"

Surulimalai interrupted. "Why shouldn't there be room for sesame to fall? If it fell on people's heads, would they be unable to bear it?"

"Hey! Don't interrupt! Listen to the matter, thambi! After Kattur I went to Kaniyakurichi. Went to Samayapuram. Everywhere, huge crowds! Tremendous respect for our fire-pot dance! Four days in a row, from morning, take the kapprai pot in your hand, walk ten miles around and dance—is that easy work? On the fourth morning I was dancing with the fire-pot and got completely exhausted! Dizzy too! Right in front of me was a girl—a pretty young thing! I kept looking only at her, forgot myself, and went on dancing..." Singaram drew the story out.

Surulimalai snapped, "Aiyo! What is this, Anna? Coming and telling me all this..." and got to his feet.

"All right, all right, listen, boy...... Somehow, while I was dancing, my hand slipped and the fire-pot fell down and shattered! Along with it went my honour!"

"Why should your honour go just because the fire-pot broke?"

"You thick-headed fellow! Don't you know? We carry that pot in our hands with all that fire in it, and it doesn't burn the hand—what did you think the reason was?"

"I thought it was the power of Kaliamman!"

"However much power Amma may have, we need a little care of our own too, thambi!"

"How, Anna?"

"We spread cottonseed under the fire-pot so too much heat doesn't come down to the bottom. Otherwise—the hand is finished!"

"Cottonseed?"

"Yes, da, yes! That secret was exposed there! That pretty little woman who had been looking at me with such pride and affection, melting like the fire inside my pot, gave a little *kaluk* laugh!"

"Then?"

"Then what? I spun a story that some danger was going to come to this village, that the fire-pot had slipped from my hand because Amman was showing a bad omen. Everyone believed me and got frightened! Only my own Amman showed me a good omen and disappeared!"

Though Surulimalai felt increasing distaste at his talk, he could not show it. The smell of arrack coming from Singaram also told him that the man was in a mild morning intoxication.

<!-- verified source join: scan 19→20 -->

"All right, all right... then?" Surulimalai said, rising.

"I need a fire-pot urgently. Go tell Amma I said so and ask her to make one quickly and give it to me within two days!"

"You go ask Amma yourself—go!"

"Why? Are you off to some great Collector's job?"

"Yes; I've got a job bigger than a Collector's. I'm going to attend to it!"

"What kind of job is that, thambi?"

"Just tilt your head up and look at the tree branch," Surulimalai said calmly, pointing above.

Singaram looked up. He started. Without realizing it, he cried, "Ah!" A young woman was hanging by the neck from a branch.

"What is this, you sinful fellow? You didn't tell me all this time! And me—I'm a fool, standing here all this time talking nonsense to you without noticing it. What kind of boy are you? A woman has hanged herself and is dangling there, and you sit playing the flute without caring... what is this... Aiyo, who knows if she's still alive!" In a panic, Singaram began climbing the tree.

"Right, Anna! You take care of this. I'll go bring the panchayat elder," said Surulimalai. He left the neem tree and headed toward Panchayat Paramasivam's house.

Panchayat Paramasivam was sitting on the outer veranda of his house, enduring the antics of his precious son<!-- verified source join: scan 20→21 --> with one hand on his head and muttering, "Oh, Easwara!"

"Appa! Appa! Who's holding up the world, Appa?"

"God's holding it up, da... keep quiet!"

"Doesn't his neck hurt, Appa?"

"When it hurts, he'll change shoulders—keep quiet!"

"What'll happen, Appa, if one day God just drops the world with a thud?"

"If only he did! My troubles would end—I'd go in peace, without your nuisance!"

"Appa! Tell God to lift the world and put it on my head!"

"He's already put the world on your head! What else do you think he's put there—a brain?... All the soil in the world is inside your head, isn't it!"

With that, the panchayat elder struck his own head. Whenever he was at home, the whole day was one headache for him.

He had just heaved a sigh—"God, the torment has begun at daybreak itself today!"—when Surulimalai arrived and told him that a woman had hanged herself from the neem tree. Paramasivam hurried toward the tree. As the news spread, the villagers followed behind him.

Meanwhile Singaram, who had climbed the tree, found that he could not see the woman's face because it was covered by the cloth drawn over it. Hurriedly he loosened the noose and lowered her to the ground.

<!-- source boundary: scan 21 → scan 22 -->

By then Panchayat Paramasivam and the others had reached the spot. Singaram pulled aside the cloth covering the woman's face. That was enough.

"Aiyyo!" he screamed.

Before the villagers could ask him what was wrong, Singaram cried aloud, "Aiyyo—you?" His head spun, and he collapsed to the ground.
