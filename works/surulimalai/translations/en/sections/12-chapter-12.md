## 12

At the cries raised by Ponmani and Pooncholai, a few people from houses farther away came running with lamps. Theechatti Singaram, who had only just left the house, heard the commotion and turned back in alarm. Even so, he hid behind a tree and watched what was happening. He feared that perhaps Pooncholai had discovered that *he* had run away and was shouting because of it. He had no intention of going back and getting trapped among them again to suffer more misery.

By then people from the neighbouring streets had gathered in front of the house.<!-- verified source join: scan 91→92 --> The bull did not stop its attack. The man it had set upon lay pressed against a thick log on the ground, hiding his body behind it, and because of that the bull's horns could not pierce him. The deep gouges made in the log would have torn his body to pieces beyond recognition had they landed on his flesh. The people of the street ran up and shouted as much as they could, but the bull would not leave him. They were afraid to go near it. Nor were they prepared even to stand at a distance and throw stones, because it was a temple bull. God might become angry. Worse still, the bull itself might become furious. They trembled at the thought that it would remember their faces, mark them one by one and take revenge later.

Ponmani's eyes filled at the thought that the man might simply have to die there. "Won't somebody run and save him?" she pleaded. No one dared.

At last she herself ran toward the bull. She went straight toward the man under attack and stood against the animal.

Whether or not Ponmani actually thought, "Are you all men? Haven't we heard that in olden days women would say they would marry only the man who could subdue a bull, and warriors would compete for that honour, treating life as no more than a straw as they fought bulls? Looking at all of you makes those stories seem invented"—we do not know. But her act of running straight against the bull showed that she might well have thought so about the men of her village.

The moment the bull saw her, it turned its attack toward her and glared terribly.

Seeing this, Pooncholai screamed, "Aiyyo! Ponmani! Ponmani!" and ran toward her daughter. The shouting and confusion only made the bull more frenzied. It gathered all its strength, preparing to charge Ponmani savagely.<!-- verified source join: scan 92→93 -->

Pooncholai cried, "Aiyaiyo!"

The bull drew back a few steps, snorting with fury before its charge. At exactly that moment, the sound of a flute came from somewhere. Into that cruel, shuddering scene entered a cool stream of music, making the moment seem stranger still. But that sound had the power to control the temple bull, as could be seen from the sudden change in the animal.

The bull stood looking toward the direction of the flute. Everyone else turned that way too. A boy was approaching through the darkness, playing as he came. As he drew closer to the lamplight it became clear that the boy was Surulimalai.

He came and stood beside the bull. The animal became calm. It licked his hand with its tongue. He gave it two pats on the back, climbed onto it and sat down.

Carrying him, the bull moved away from the place. Everyone watched in amazement.

Pooncholai embraced Ponmani. "My girl! You're alive?" she cried, kissing the crown of her head. "All the gods I worship are good gods," she said, congratulating herself on her skill in choosing them.

<!-- source boundary: scan 93 → scan 94 -->

The people of the street lifted up the man who had been tossed by the bull and had curled beside the log, and sat him upright. There was no major wound on his body. The bull's horns had not found their mark. If they had, his stomach or chest would have been ripped open. His body was covered with bruises, however, and from those blows he had lost the strength to speak and had fainted.

One man held his lamp directly before the stranger's face. He was a young man with a calm, handsome face. A budding moustache. A civilized appearance. Perhaps around twenty-five years old. His eyes were closed. If not for his cropped hair and moustache, he might almost have looked like Gautama Buddha; such serenity rested upon that youthful face.

Ponmani hurried away and brought water. Pooncholai took it and sprinkled some on his face. At Pooncholai's request—"Lift him carefully and bring him inside!"—several men carried the young man into the house.

Only when Pooncholai looked around for a place to lay him did she realize that Singaram had escaped. "Ada, amma! In the middle of all that commotion he threw dust in our eyes and got away!" she exclaimed, both surprised and annoyed. She told them to lay the young man on the cot where Singaram had been lying.

All this caused Ponnaiya great irritation. "Aiyo! What kind of cursed business is this? Is this a house—or a hospital? Is every man who gets beaten or kicked in the street going to be brought here?" he muttered. Fortunately, at just the right moment his cough stopped him from continuing. "Go and ruin yourselves any way you like! What do I care?" he concluded through the coughs.

<!-- verified source join: scan 94→95 -->

Pooncholai, Ponmani and a few people from the street began giving the young man whatever care they could. Singaram, who had been hiding and carefully observing everything, left the place and quickened his pace.

After a long while of treatment, the unconscious young man opened his eyes slightly, looked in surprise at everyone around him, then rolled onto his side. "There's no danger now. Let him sleep from exhaustion," they said, and everyone left Pooncholai's house.

Ponmani sat at a distance where she could see his face in the dim light of the lamp. For the first time in her life, she found herself enslaved by a new feeling. Without realizing it she kept looking at his face. A thought would arise that she was doing something wrong—and disappear as quickly as it came. This itself felt strange to her. Until then no one had managed to steal her heart. Now she herself seemed ready to come forward and offer her heart to this young man.

Her eyes advanced upon his face like the dark *kiruttina* birds that dive slantwise, never missing their aim, to snatch fish glittering and floating in clear water. An indescribable feeling she had never known before began to toss her about.

<!-- source boundary: scan 95 → scan 96 -->

Whenever he groaned from pain, she felt as though she ought to sit beside him and stroke his body. She imagined, with longing and embarrassment, that if she sat near him and kept passing her soft fingers over his handsome forehead, he might fall into deep sleep.

"Ponmani! Go and sleep, amma!... I don't know what has happened—the whole village seems to have gone wrong. Look at this! That Singaram has run away too. The possession came and said there was danger! He has left the ghost here and gone... who knows what will happen! Gods! Keep this family from any harm!" Pooncholai prayed fervently, told Ponmani to sleep, and herself dozed for a while.

By the time Ponmani could tell that dawn had come, her wandering eyes, eager to see the young man's face clearly in daylight, had already pushed her awake and drawn her toward the hall.

Pooncholai too had risen by then. "Thambi! Thambi!" she called, waking the young man.

The young man opened his eyes. He did not ask, "Where am I?" He understood only that he must be in some safe place, and that kind people had rescued him.

"How is your body now, appa?" Pooncholai asked.

"Not too bad. Thank you very much, amma," the young man replied.

<!-- source boundary: scan 96 → scan 97 -->

Ponmani stood hidden behind a pillar, enjoying the beauty visible in the movement of his lips and the music woven into his voice.

"Lok! Lok!"—a cough. Ponnaiya came slowly toward the hall.

"Where has that boy gone? Useless donkey! If he keeps roaming the village like this, he'll end up holding a begging bowl!" Ponnaiya had just finished when Surulimalai entered, chanting in a tune: "Harahara Mahadeva! Charity for Annamalai, mother! Arohara for Annamalai!"

"Where did you go, da? What do you think you're doing? If your mother and sister struggle and earn food for you, is your plan to eat without a word and roam the village—lazy donkey! This place is running as both choultry and hospital now. Nothing will prosper! Nothing will prosper! Cursed house! Cursed house!" Ponnaiya said, heading toward the backyard.

Noticing that his father was walking unsteadily, Surulimalai said, "Slowly! Slowly! Don't fall down in your anger, appa!" He took Ponnaiya's hand and led him toward the back.

Ponmani saw a change on the young man's face after hearing her father's words and became deeply upset. Pooncholai too was shaken and for a moment did not know what to say. Then she recovered.

"Thambi... my husband is always like this. A sour-tempered man! His heart is good—only his mouth never stays quiet. Some people in this world speak like sugar and act like bitter neem. But my husband has no malice in his heart. Only outside<!-- source boundary: scan 97 → scan 98 --> he talks like this and earns himself a bad name. He truly loves helping people. Don't take it to heart, thambi," she said, soothing him.

"It's all right! It's all right! I owe you very much for your kindness. I think I can slowly make my way out now. Please give me leave to go," said the young man.

By then Surulimalai had returned.

Pooncholai immediately introduced him. "This is my son—the younger brother of the girl standing over there," she said.

The young man turned and looked. It became very difficult for him to draw his eyes away again.

Pooncholai continued: "He's the one who saved you, thambi. If he hadn't come, both you and my daughter would have been sacrificed to that temple bull's horns!"

"Is that so?" the young man asked in surprise.

"Yes. The bull knocked you down and you were lying there without breath or speech. The whole village came running, but not one person went near. Then my daughter Ponmani ran bravely and stood against the bull. The bull tried to kill my daughter too! Just then my boy Surulimalai arrived. Thambi—you won't believe it. The moment he took out the flute and played, the bull was enchanted by him and knelt right down!"

"It's a bull that has long been used to hearing the flute, isn't it? A bull the boy already knows—right?"

<!-- source boundary: scan 98 → scan 99 -->

Saying this, the young man looked back and forth between Surulimalai and Ponmani.

Surulimalai stood without speaking. Ponmani spoke with her eyes, then hid herself behind the pillar. The young man remembered a moon-song he had heard somewhere and hummed:

> Hiding your body beneath the garment of the blue sky,  
> you show only a moon-bright face!  
> If you displayed your whole beauty,  
> would not the world die in love's plunder?

Pooncholai did not stop talking. "You look like a golden-hearted boy. Who are you, thambi? Which village are you from?" she asked.

"I'm an elementary-school teacher. My name is Aravazhi. I've been transferred to the school in this village. Until now I was in a village in Salem district," the young man replied.

"Why did they transfer you here?" Pooncholai asked.

"For my sake, of course," Ponmani answered silently within herself.
