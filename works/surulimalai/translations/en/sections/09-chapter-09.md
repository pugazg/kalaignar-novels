## 9

If Karpuram was to feel Kannan's love and desire for her swelling like a flood that had burst its banks, Ponmani must not stand in the way between them. Ponmani had to become another man's so completely that Kannan would cease even thinking about her. There was nothing strange, then, in Karpuram thinking that such an eye-catching beauty might as well become her own brother's wife. Her longing for Kannan knew no measure. Karpuram, who had been doing penance as though to offer herself up to his majestic good looks, now felt a new hope dawn within her.

A suggestion made by some of the villagers for driving out the ghost said to possess her brother began to work on her mind. They had proposed bringing Karagam-dancer Kannan to exorcise the ghost so that Singaram might recover.

Questions followed: "How long have Kannan's family and yours been enemies? If you go and call him, will he come to drive out the ghost?"

She wanted him *not* to come. The reason was simply that he and Ponmani must not meet inside the house. But after thinking deeply, she decided that the excuse that her brother was possessed by a ghost would at least give her a chance to see Kannan once and speak with him. She gained both clarity and satisfaction from deciding that her plan was sound.

There was no ghost in her brother, nor anything of the sort. Even if invited to exorcise one, Kannan was not likely to come. So she could go and invite him freely. Under that pretext, she would get the chance to talk to him.

Karpuram's decision was put into action at once. Leaving Pooncholai's house, she walked straight toward Kannan's home. Since their fathers' time the two

households had not even exchanged words. Now, however, a flood of affection that no dam could hold back made her forget the hereditary feud entirely.

Kannan was sitting on the front veranda reading a Mariamman lullaby when he saw Karpuram coming toward his house. He was surprised. He was confident she would never actually enter his house, and with an air of indifference—why should he even notice her?—he pretended to look at his book while casting a sidelong glance toward the street. Karpuram crossed Kannan's threshold without hesitation and came toward the veranda. Kannan could no longer remain still. He stared at her in agitation. Sweat poured all over him. He made a great effort to turn his head back and bury his eyes in the book again, but could not. Karpuram herself began to speak.

"Shouldn't you at least ask a visitor to come in? Shouldn't you ask why I've come?"

Kannan was jolted. Was *Karpuram* speaking like this? He could hardly believe it and remained silent.

"There's great danger! The ghost trouble with my anna has become very severe..."

"What cursed business is this? If someone gets possessed by a ghost, what is that to me?"

"Even if there are a thousand quarrels between us, shouldn't people help when there's danger? Don't they say there is no sin in helping someone in danger?"

"Fine justice! Have you people no shame or self-respect? With what face did you come here to call me?"

<!-- source boundary: scan 74 → scan 75 -->

"With this face right here! Why—don't you like this face?"

Karpuram tried to pull the conversation in a direction favourable to herself. Without taking the trouble to understand what lay beneath her words, Kannan replied:

"Some wretch killed an innocent woman and hanged her, and now he's suffering the result. You've come to call *me* to drive a ghost out of the fellow lying in the police station—tch! Shame..."

"In the police station?... No... no. Anna is at Pooncholai-amma's house. Don't tell anyone! You're the only one I've told this secret to. Because I have so much trust in you!"

The moment Kannan heard that Singaram was in Pooncholai-amma's house, the thought of Ponmani flew into his mind. Now the idea of agreeing to exorcise Singaram's ghost took hold of him. Wouldn't that let him feast his eyes on Ponmani in her own house? If possible he could speak to her, draw closer—why, perhaps even settle the marriage itself. In an instant he had built an entire palace in his mind.

Looking at Karpuram, he said, "Hm! What can one do? The elders say we should do good even to those who've done us harm. All right, all right—I'll come and drive out the ghost!"

Karpuram felt no delight. Yet she made herself appear happy. She had thought he would not come—and that it was better if he did not. Kannan's decision threw her into confusion. What<!-- verified source join: scan 75→76 --> could she do? Could she now say, "Don't come"? Karpuram composed herself. In a tone of gratitude, opening her heart and mixing shyness into her words, she said to Kannan:

"I never believed you would agree! Ah... do you know how long I have suffered and suffered for that one word, 'yes'?"

He had no patience to take in the meaning of her words. A chance had come to him of its own accord to go to the house and see the woman he desired. His whole attention was fixed on not losing it and leaving at once. He ran inside, changed his clothes, took his udukkai, and set out toward Ponmani's house.

Surulimalai welcomed Kannan, who came with Karpuram, and led him inside. Kannan let his eyes roam all over the house searching for Ponmani. She had hidden herself somewhere. Karpuram took some satisfaction in that.

No sooner had Kannan reached the place where Singaram lay than Singaram somehow found a new strength. He tore the bindings on his hands to pieces and sprang up in a single leap. But the binding on his legs would not let him rise any farther.

"Hey, traitor! What are you doing here? Come to drive out a ghost? Can *you* drive out the ghost that's got hold of me? What are you—a great sorcerer? A magician? Idiot! Fool! Don't stand there—run!" Singaram began shouting.

Kannan, determined to turn the situation cleverly to his own advantage, said, "Aiyo, appa! This is a powerful demon! First, all of us together must tie it down. Then I'll begin my work!" At that, Singaram, trembling with agitation, began trying to undo the bonds on his feet.

But before he could, Kannan seized him and tied him tightly to the cot. Deciding this was the moment to vent his anger against Singaram, he took a large cane in his hand and struck him all over the body. The cane blows rang out—"veer, veer." Singaram collapsed in exhaustion.

Karpuram could not bear it. Thinking of the pain her brother was suffering because of the castle she herself had built in imagination, she began to cry.

"Stop! Don't hit him! He can't bear any more!" Only after Karpuram ran forward and caught Kannan's hand did the cane blows stop.

After that, Kannan began the puja. Before it ended, he was desperate somehow to see Ponmani, but he could not. Not only that: suspicion of Singaram had now sprouted in him too. If Singaram stayed there, might he perhaps charm Ponmani? That fear began to trouble him as well.

When the puja ended, Kannan said he would return the next day to perform another puja and took his leave. But he did not go straight home. He hurried to the outpost police station. Only after telling the officer there that Singaram was at Pooncholai's house did he sleep peacefully.
