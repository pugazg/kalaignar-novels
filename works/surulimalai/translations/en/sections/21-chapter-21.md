## 21

Ponmani stood motionless, exhausted and bewildered, staring at the shattered pots. Aravazhi too came to the doorway and looked closely at her with sympathy.

"Tch! Tch! Have all the pots broken?"

His words of concern gave her comfort. What did it matter how many pots had broken? If only the fortress of affection that had arisen in Aravazhi's heart for her on the first day had not broken down, that was enough.

But in every shard lying there, she seemed to see the figure of her father Ponnaiya and the form of her mother Pooncholai, standing in pain with tears streaming from their eyes.

It was with the money she expected to earn by selling those pots at the market that day that they could prepare an important medicine prescribed for her father. The moment she remembered this, tears poured from her in a river despite herself.

Her mother would be waiting eagerly for her return that evening. Her father, thinking of the medicine he was going to take, would have been freed at least a little from anxiety about his illness. But now everything had been smashed to pieces. What was she to do?

The young woman sighed deeply. Just as a straight, graceful line gives beauty to the shape of the body, so came that deep sigh—but aiyo, how much sorrow, pain and anguish it carried!

Aravazhi could not understand the meaning of it. He thought Ponmani was only afraid that she would be scolded at home for breaking the pots.

"Don't be afraid! You're this worried because they'll scold you at home, aren't you?..."

<!-- source boundary: scan 156 → scan 157 -->

Ponmani gave no answer. She gathered the broken pots together.

Only one small jar with beautiful workmanship had survived. She picked it up.

Aravazhi came nearer and tried to take the jar from her. She handed it to him.

"Ah! What a beautiful jar! What fine workmanship! I'll take this. How much does it cost?"

Ponmani did not reply.

Aravazhi tried to make her speak.

"All the pots have broken. What would they have been worth?"

"If I sold them at the market, I'd get seven or eight rupees," Ponmani said.

"All right. Tell me the price of *this* jar."

"No! I've fixed a price for this. Its price is ten rupees. One could value it even higher—but in the present circumstances, that's as much as an elementary-school teacher can afford!"

"What do *you* need this earthen jar for?"

"Don't you know? My teacher's job is about to go 'Arohara'! After that I'll need this to go from house to house collecting rice and gruel to eat. That's why I'm buying the basic tool of my future profession!"

Aravazhi spoke with a mischievous smile.

Ponmani's face grew even more troubled than before. She understood that Aravazhi might have formed a wrong opinion of her.

<!-- source boundary: scan 157 → scan 158 -->

"I came looking for you precisely to explain that," Ponmani said, wiping away the pearls spilling from her eyes before they could roll down her cheeks.

The rain began to fall a little harder, so Aravazhi invited her inside. She entered the room.

The wind too began blowing more strongly. He gave her a small towel and told her to wipe the drizzle from her face.

These courtesies did not fully satisfy Ponmani. She longed painfully, thinking he was treating her with a changed heart—a heart that had discovered some great fault in her.

They had not known each other long enough to speak freely about everything. One day's meeting—a gaze full of meaning—that was all. Their two hearts had not yet been rubbed together hard enough to strike the spark called love and throw it onto the bales of cotton called opposition.

So she hesitated.

Even so, she resolved that she must remove his mistaken belief about her.

"They've sent a petition from the village..." she began.

"Yes. I've heard. My thanks to everyone who signed it!" he replied.

Ponmani struggled, unable to say more.

Aravazhi himself continued.

"If they would free me not only from this village but even from this job, I'd be very happy!"

"I too... in that..." Ponmani began.

<!-- source boundary: scan 158 → scan 159 -->

"You too?... You too what? What is it? Say it so I can understand!" Aravazhi asked mischievously.

"After everyone else had signed, whether two or three people from our house signed or not, the petition was going to gain life anyway. We were afraid to stand against the discipline of the whole village, so we signed it at home. Apart from that, there was no hostility toward you at all!"

"Chi, chi! Who said there was? Who in this village is my enemy? They all want to drive me away only because they want the village to prosper, don't they?"

"You shouldn't say that. Don't put a stain on what we did. Once the whole village had joined together, my family thought, 'Why should we alone stand apart?' And that wasn't all. My thambi and I had decided to tear up and burn the petition so it would never reach the post. I signed without fear only because I believed the petition would come into my brother's hands."

"So what? Never mind. Did you have to come searching for me just for that? It's a trivial matter."

"For *you* it's trivial. But for me?"

"For you too!"

"If it were trivial for me, why would I have come here?"

After saying this she lowered her head.

Aravazhi too felt that her statement made sense. Her family had lived for years within the discipline and collective expectations of the village. There was no reason they should suddenly create a situation in which they became enemies of the whole village for the sake of a man they had met only one day earlier.

<!-- source boundary: scan 159 → scan 160 -->

But Ponmani had ignored that village unity and shown her affection for him through action, coming all the way to where he stayed.

The thought brought Aravazhi a new happiness.

Eagerness and affection took shape as words.

"I've never seen such a strange village. Within just a few days of arriving, I've met people who hate me, people who want to drive me out, and people who show affection toward me!"

"You must have come to a separate conclusion about each of them."

"Certainly!... Whose conclusion do you want to know?"

She grew shy.

"The conclusion you've formed about me..."

"About you?..."

She looked straight at him.

He too stood looking only at her.

The rain came down very heavily. The wind grew harsher and harsher. Signs of a storm appeared in the sky.

But a revolution greater than the one in the heavens was taking place here, inside two hearts.
