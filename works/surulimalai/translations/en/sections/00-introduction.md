# Introduction

There are many ways to tell a story!

Suddenly, in the middle of the night, a terrifying sound is heard in a house. Everyone in the street rushes there in alarm. The door of the house stands open. A lamp burns dimly. Someone's moaning reaches the ear. A little later even that sound stops. When the lamp is turned up, a beautiful woman is seen lying there dead. The people of the village know her. She is the woman the whole village praises as its very Mahalakshmi. Now she is a corpse. Her husband is nowhere to be seen. Where has he gone? Why was she killed? From these question marks the complications of the story begin.

A fine moonlit night! The sky shone over the whole world as though silver had been melted and cast into light. Birds rested in their nests. The entire village lay swooning, senseless in the embrace of the maiden called peace. Kumkum-coloured pathiri flowers lay scattered in the streets. In the blue moonlight a beautiful woman walked slowly and all alone. Who was she? Why were lines of pain spread across her face? Stories sometimes begin this way too.

When a story begins at the centre of its action and draws readers backward and forward from there, there is no need to announce beforehand the particulars of the people who move through it.

The story of *Surulimalai* does not take shape from the centre of its events. It arises instead from the historical centre of a small village. The events that whirl around the important people of that village form the substance of the story. So is an introduction to them not essential?

We call every tiny hamlet a *sirur*, a small village. But the small village in this story is itself named Sirur!

Let us now enter Sirur. You can enter that village only in a bullock cart—or on foot. The road is that full of jolts, with great pits running this way and that. Sirur is a village of beauty and abundance, as though proving the rule that one must cross many hardships before reaching pleasure. Right at the village entrance stands a coconut grove rising toward the sky, its palms heavy with clusters of red tender coconuts! Sit within it and a cool breeze gently strokes and lulls the body. Go farther in and there stand an imposing Siva temple, a Kali temple and a Mari temple! A temple tank—with four streets around it! Green lawns spread out like silk! Beside them lie lovely fields where golden grains of paddy nod and sway!

The peal of the Siva-temple bell—the swell of music—the resonant pambai and udukkai at the Kali and Mari temples—the sound of the oil press—the sales-cries of basket women—the racket of children playing marbles—the multiplication lesson<!-- verified source join: scan 6→7 --> shouted in chorus by the boys at the school in the middle of the mango grove!! The whole village would seem alive like this. Let us seek out the chief people in that village who concern our story. The first to catch our eye is—there—that temple bull!....

● If it was the Siva-temple bull, "Siva," no one would even stare at it. It was not merely that staring was a "sin"; the villagers were that frightened of it! When it strode majestically down the street with a kingly gait, the villagers would shrink against houses and fences, their bodies damp with sweat. A well-fed body—horns stronger than iron—a thick hide of glossy blue-black hair—the villagers said that only in the eyes of that fierce bull could a certain tenderness be seen. Yet they were afraid to go near it. Are attachment, affection, bonds and belonging the private property of humankind alone? Do cattle have none of them? If anything, they labour more than human beings do; they show greater loyalty than humans to the one who raised them.

Raise a cow for many days and then sell it to someone else for money—does it ordinarily leave that house so easily? How many times does it escape from the buyer and run back to the place where it first lived! The seller drives it away again and again; at last the animal seems to reach a decision of its own... perhaps deciding that the one who paid money because he wanted it is better than the one who rejected it and valued money more... and it begins to show affection to its buyer. Born into a distinguished and prosperous family of that bovine race was the temple bull Siva! People honoured Siva as the vehicle of the Lord. But one impudent fellow made it his own vehicle—and that fellow was............

<!-- source boundary: scan 7 → scan 8 -->

● At the name "Surulimalai," one might imagine some great robber or hardened thief. Devotees, on the other hand, might feel like singing, "O virtuous one who dwells upon Surulimalai! I praise you, Subramanya of the spear!" People of Madurai district say that the slopes of Surulimalai even today offer thieves and criminals places to hide and live. Our Surulimalai, however, dedicates himself to the effort of bringing thieves and criminals out into the open.

He was born into an ordinary family. His mother, father and one sister were his only support. Apart from Siva, the temple bull, he had no friends at all in the village. After Siva, the thing he loved most was a bamboo flute. If he had Siva and his flute, he would not even notice hunger. Though the poor family did not wish to place its entire burden on a boy not yet even quite eleven, it expected at least a little help from him. But he wandered as carefree as the temple bull. His father would scold him—run to beat him—his elder sister would stop him and weep—his mother would become a vessel of anguish, a storm of sorrow. Yet her name, of all things, was Pooncholai—"flower grove"!....

● She had married into a family that made its living by treading clay and making pots and vessels, and had passed more than forty years believing labour alone to be the joy of the world—a grand woman, the very embodiment of love, a mountain of virtue, a queenly woman who shone as the steady lamp of the household! If you asked for Ponnaiya's house, nobody would understand; in that village the house itself was known only by the name of his wife, Pooncholai. If anyone in any house fell ill,<!-- verified source join: scan 8→9 --> Pooncholai would be there. Childbirth? Let Pooncholai merely step in, the elders would say, and it would be an easy delivery. Throughout the area people said that for Pongal a new pot and new cooking vessel had to be bought from Pooncholai's own hands.

A striking kumkum mark on her turmeric-tinted face—a broad gaze—a calm face—and, within, a storm from the worries of the family! The ache that Surulimalai had become an unruly boy—the sorrow that no suitable groom had been found for her daughter—the unappeased desire to adorn that beautiful young woman with jewels. If ornaments could have been made of clay, she would have piled them up by the lapful! But they had to be made of gold. Perhaps simply to satisfy her heart, she had at least given her daughter the name Ponmani—"golden jewel"..........

● A young peahen from a flowering grove! A sculpted beauty with surging youth spilling from every limb! Teeth like jasmine buds yet to open—a radiant face like the round moon come down to earth! That statue of beauty had to labour upon the earth and beside the fierce heat flung by the blazing kiln, so that her natural loveliness remained hidden while she pined in the courtyard of a poor home. To her it seemed that the world ended with her house, the pot-firing kiln beside it, and the villages to which she walked to sell those pots. A father in failing health—a mother altered by endless labour—a younger brother roaming the village—between them she spun like a top wound tight by the cord of family affection, that maiden, that crystal statue!

Only one kind of joy could sometimes seize her so completely that she forgot every worry: the sweet sound of the flute when, after being asked many times, her younger brother finally agreed to play. The pullaan-<!-- verified source join: scan 9→10 -->kuzhal would pour out its delight! She would then become all grace; watching her, her mother's eyes would turn into pools of tears, and she would ask, "Who will be blessed enough to win my darling girl?"

Then from inside would come Ponnaiya's "lok, lok" cough—the deathly torment he suffered—and send them running into the house!.... Through the coughing, the father would begin scolding his son. The cough would worsen. Thinking that his father's very life was in danger because of him, Surulimalai would leave. Ponmani would run after him and return in tears! If Theechatti Singaram failed to make one sort of criticism or another, again and again every day, about this strange family, he could not sleep at all!....

● It was not merely his name. To look at the man himself was like looking at fire. Whoever he spoke to, his words came out blazing. The villagers could not help feeling a little fear at his imposing appearance and brisk gait. Yet no one should decide from his speech, appearance, walk, dress and manner that he was a wicked man. His heart held no deceit. He simply did not worry about what he was saying, why he was saying it, or whose feelings his words might wound. Whatever entered his mind had to be spoken before the thought was even complete. Otherwise he felt as though some great weight were pressing on his head and heart!

At the annual festival of the Kali temple, the hero of the event was none other than Theechatti Singaram. Not only at Sirur's Kali temple: during festival seasons in every village within fifty miles there was enormous demand for Singaram to carry the fire-pot and dance.

<!-- source boundary: scan 10 → scan 11 -->

It brought good income—he had work almost every month. Even when he was not actually carrying the fire-pot, he had an uncontrollable desire to wear the costume of the fire-pot dance. Sometimes the villagers teased him by calling, "Kattavaraya! Madurai Veera!" If he himself was fire in gaze and manner, his younger sister was camphor—Karpuram—ready to flare up with a *kubir!* the moment it touched that fire!....

● It was enough for Karpuram to see an emotionally charged scene—or even hear of one; divine possession would seize her. Then the udukkai and pambai had to be brought out! She would dance for at least half an hour before calming down. By then people with wishes to ask about would come running in haste for an oracle, and she would give them answers while still dancing. Karpuram's fame had spread through the whole region. She was probably not yet eighteen. One could not call her a great beauty, but she lacked nothing in attraction. There was a captivating power in her eyes. A permanent dark mole on her flushed face gave her a charm of her own. Like her brother, whose words leapt like sparks, she too spoke in sharp, snapping bursts. Everyone worshipped her as an incarnation of a goddess; no one came forward to examine whether she was good or bad. Would it not be a sin to examine a divine aspect? Perhaps that was why! A young man who wanted society freed from that "divine aspect," that cloak of divinity, arrived in the village. One might say that after his arrival a revolution began there...

<!-- source boundary: scan 11 → scan 12 -->

● There was nothing in his appearance or face that looked capable of producing a revolution! Must a revolutionary look like Theechatti Singaram? Did not Gautama Buddha, with a face filled with peace and words gentler still, set in motion a great revolution in the world? When a wild river is going to overflow its banks, how quietly, without anyone noticing, does the first revolution that produces the flood take place! How quietly the sun turns sea-water into vapour, and the cool wind meets the water in the clouds! Until rain falls and the world becomes a sheet of floodwater, no one notices the silent revolutionary meeting conducted by sea, sun and wind!

The result of that silent revolution ends by giving great life to the world and causing a little destruction as well; that cannot be avoided. In just that way a spark of revolution emerges from a gentle heart. Such a heart now stepped into Sirur for the first time. The villagers welcomed Teacher Aravazhi with some surprise, for until then they had never seen a young man as a teacher. Having seen only grey-haired old men with their hair tied in knots, they had decided that this was what a teacher had to look like. They gazed at Aravazhi in wonder. On the very day Aravazhi came to the village there was a small quarrel—and the man responsible for it was Karagam-dancer Kannan...

● A handsome young man—with a budding moustache and a lion's gaze—a deeply devout man—renowned as an unrivalled expert at dancing with a flower-karagam. He had an unshakable attachment to hereditary custom and the old ways. A child in anyone's hands! His nature was to listen to whatever anyone told him and act accordingly. Between him and Theechatti Singaram there was a hereditary feud in that village. From the days of their grandfathers and great-grandfathers, the two families had competed in fire-pot dancing and karagam dancing, quarrelled, and lived apart. Panchayat Paramasivam, who settles every quarrel, finds himself unable to settle theirs...

● Hot-tempered and credulous—anything white was milk to him! His household was one of hereditary wealth in the village. Yet he was a thorough miser: he would put out a cigar again and again and smoke the same one for three days. Though he looked like a poor farmer, he owned more than seventy acres of fields, gardens and other property.

A man who uttered the words "Everything is given by God" at least a thousand times a day had been given, by God, a mad son. Why should I explain that mad son to you now? He will appear at many places in the story. You may savour his deeds and his speech then!

The introduction is, to some extent, over. From time to time many others will come and go.
