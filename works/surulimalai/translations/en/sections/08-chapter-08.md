## 8

Surulimalai answered with his usual indifference. "Yes. It would have been one thing if the dead woman's mouth had spoken; they say the end of her sari spoke instead!"

Still astonished, Ponmani poured out her impatience. "What is all this you're saying, thambi? Tell it so I can understand!"

"Wasn't it enough that the dead woman died? She has gone and landed the people still living in the village in danger too!" Surulimalai muttered to himself.

"Are you going to tell me properly or not?" his sister chided him, with a touch of affection.

"The police came through the panchayat and took away the body of the woman who had hanged herself. They examined the body there, it seems. They call that an inquest. They decided it was suicide. Just then, they suddenly noticed a knot in the end of the corpse's sari. When they untied it, there was a letter inside. They read it."

"It said, 'The reason I killed myself is that sinner Theechatti Singaram'—that's what she had written, it seems."

Before he could finish saying this, Ponmani's whole body began to tremble and her tongue faltered.

"Aiyaiyo! What new trouble is this? Thambi, he's here in our house, isn't he..." she said in terror. Surulimalai rose and went inside without showing any agitation.

The moment Singaram saw him, he shouted, "Surulimalai! Where is Maina? Where is my Maina?"

<!-- source boundary: scan 65 → scan 66 -->

"Maina, sparrow—what next!" Surulimalai muttered.

"Don't say anything, kanna! Anna is being tormented by a ghost! The woman who hanged herself has turned into a ghost and clung to him!" Karpuram said, trying to soothe him.

"Hm! Has it only clung to him? Looks as if she'll take your anna along with her too! She seems to have written a letter blaming him, saying she killed herself because of him."

Singaram started.

"Ah! Am *I* the reason my Maina died? Aiyyo—God! I don't know of any sin I've committed! Maina! Maina!! Did you die because of me? I don't understand anything! Please untie me!!"

Trying to drown out his cries, Karpuram began shouting, "What is it, anna—have you gone mad? Don't babble! Maina, dirt, whatever!... God! God! Could you write such a fate on our heads? We went to save someone in danger and ended up with slander upon us!... And now anna's mind is ruined too! Hey, demon!... Are you going to leave my anna or not?"

It became a great task for Pooncholai to restrain her. Hearing the sound of four or five people approaching from the street, Surulimalai turned toward the entrance. He saw the police coming toward the house, their heavy boots striking the rough ground with a frightening thud. He hurried out into the street and stood waiting for them.

The police asked Surulimalai, "Where is he?..."

"Who?" he said.

"That man—Theechatti Singaram!"

<!-- source boundary: scan 66 → scan 67 -->

"Singaram? Ghost and madness have both got hold of that fellow. He just ran off this way. Go quickly—you can catch him!" Surulimalai said as casually as though it were nothing.

The police hurried in the direction he pointed. Once they had gone, Surulimalai went inside and bolted the door. He called his mother and sister.

"Danger! Be careful! We must not let him go outside at all. If he goes, that's the end of it!" he warned.

Ponnaiya did not like deceiving the police in the least. Coughing "lok, lok," he called Surulimalai and snapped, "Hey! Why do we need all this trouble and police business?" Surulimalai gave his father no answer. As though his words did not count, he turned to Karpuram and said, "You go home! We'll take care of everything. Don't blurt any of this out to anyone! If you're not at home either, the police will grow even more suspicious."

Karpuram was caught between satisfaction that the opportunity she had hoped for had come and distress at the unexpected accusation now falling on her brother. She took leave of everyone, prayed to her chosen deity, took some sacred ash and smeared it on her brother's forehead, then left for home by the backyard route.

Surulimalai sat silently beside Singaram for a while. Singaram, tormented inwardly by the thought that his words and cries carried no weight in that house and that they took his lamenting for the shrieks of a ghost, softly and affectionately called, "Thambi!"

<!-- source boundary: scan 67 → scan 68 -->

At once Pooncholai panicked, caught Surulimalai by the hand and pulled him toward the kitchen. "Aiyo! Don't sit here! It'll talk softly and then the ghost will jump onto you!"

Singaram fell silent. Tears welled from his eyes and ran past his ears. Unable even to move his hands to wipe them away, he lay in misery. Ponmani saw his distress and came near. She was afraid to approach him alone; even so, compassion overcame her fear. "Don't cry," she said, and wiped his tears with a cloth.

For some reason, Karpuram, who had gone home, returned just then. She saw Ponmani wiping his eyes and, filled inwardly with delight, slipped away again without anyone noticing. Hope had dawned in her. She calculated that affection would somehow ripen between Ponmani and Singaram, and congratulated herself on her own scheme. She did not want her brother to become husband to Maina, a *vazhavetty*—a separated woman. And on the very next day after she had thought so, Maina had left this world without obstructing her family's honour. That gave Karpuram great satisfaction.

Why was Karpuram so anxious that Ponmani should become Singaram's wife? Ponmani was a great beauty. Did she want that lovely statue of a woman as a bride for her brother because of her beauty? No—not for beauty! Ponmani was a woman of rare virtue. Did Karpuram think she ought to enter their family as a bride because of her character? No—not for character either! Then why? Karpuram wanted, somehow, to tie Ponmani to her brother; but it was not really her brother's marriage to<!-- verified source join: scan 68→69 --> Ponmani that she longed for. In short, all her concern was about *Ponmani's* marriage, not about her brother Singaram's!

Why should she care so much about a friend—more than about her own brother? Another man desired Ponmani. He was wasting away for her. If she so much as moved her eyes in his favour, he was ready to lie at her feet and serve as her slave for the rest of his life. Karpuram's thought was that he must not obtain Ponmani. As for him, he had sworn that either he would obtain Ponmani or he would die. Karpuram had personally seen the scene in which he begged Ponmani for the alms of love. He believed no one had seen him pleading with her—but through a crack in the ruined mandapam, Karpuram had fixed both eyes on that begging for love without so much as blinking.

At dawn Ponmani left home to sell pots and vessels in the neighbouring village. With a large basket full of pots balanced on her head, one hand steadying the basket and the other swinging gracefully, there was a beauty all its own in the way she walked. In a world where a customer might tap and inspect everything from a water pot down to a tiny mustard jar, finally buy a pot for three-quarters of an anna, and then grow angry when she tapped the suspicious quarter-anna coin he handed her—"Look at her! Testing it as if it's a bad coin!"—Ponmani dealt well with everyone without surrendering her dignity, and successfully carried on her earthenware trade.

<!-- source boundary: scan 69 → scan 70 -->

That day too she set out for business with the same enthusiasm. As usual, she did a good trade at the neighbouring village's Friday market, tied up her earnings, and, unable to contain her happiness, started home with the empty basket.

Strangely, there were very few people on the road that day. Wanting to reach home before dusk, she walked quickly. Before she had covered half the distance, the sky darkened. Thunder followed, then a drizzle.

Ponmani walked still faster. The rain soon came down heavily. Her clothes were soaked through, and her ivory-like limbs showed through them, spilling a beauty that could slay the beholder. With one hand she held her garment above her knee, while she turned the basket upside down over her head and tried to cope with the rain by half running, half walking.

Then she heard a cart coming behind her and moved to the roadside. The cart stopped. "Ponmani! Get in the cart. Aiyo! You're soaked through..." said Karagam-dancer Kannan sympathetically as he got down.

Ponmani was overcome with embarrassment. Holding the basket so that it hid her from face to waist, she walked on without speaking.

Kannan followed her. "Aiyo! Why are you acting mad? Just get into the cart! If you want, I'll shut my eyes. Why suffer, using the basket as an umbrella?" Saying this, he took the basket from her hand, put it in the cart and gestured for her to climb in too, pretending to close his eyes.

<!-- source boundary: scan 70 → scan 71 -->

Ponmani did not want to get into the cart. She looked around. There was a ruined temple mandapam nearby. She darted into it. Though old and dilapidated, the temple gave some shelter from the rain. But Kannan would not let it go—he too ran into the mandapam. Now Ponmani's face changed sharply. What she had failed to discover in his speech and behaviour until then became clear the moment he entered the mandapam after her.

"Ponmani!" he said.

"Enough! Enough! Please go away!" she snapped.

"You don't know anything about me! Night and day I think of you and melt like a worm in fire. Look here—so much rain is pouring down! Even in all this wet my body is burning! I've been doing penance for so many days just to have your darshan. Only today has the deity shown grace..."

"Don't drag God's name into this for no reason! Go without saying anything! None of this is proper!"

"Not proper? Look at me, Ponmani! They say there isn't a handsome man in this whole village to match me... Look how my budding moustache quivers... Does it sound right if I say all this myself? You're the one who should say it! Say one word. If I marry you, why would I dance with a karagam balanced on my head? I'll lift *you* up and dance—like Paramasiva dancing with Ganga upon him!"

"Don't talk nonsense! Otherwise I'll turn nasty—yes, I will!"

<!-- source boundary: scan 71 → scan 72 -->

"Hey—'Nasty? What will you do?... You worthless wretch!'—you'll say, then throw your arms around me and pinch my cheek..."

"Chi! Chi! Chi! Stop it! Speak with some respect!"

"Forgive me, Ponmani! You're the one who has to save me! Either I marry you, or I remain a bachelor for life. This is an oath! What do you say?"

Ponmani was silent for a while, looking up at the sky. The rain had stopped. Only a light drizzle remained.

"There's no one here! Don't be shy!" Kannan said, moving closer. He did not know that Karpuram too had taken shelter from the rain in the mandapam and was watching the whole drama closely.

As Kannan came near, Ponmani looked at him angrily. "Talk about marriage to my parents! What use is there in talking to me?" she said, then hurried away.

Kannan did not wish to follow and trouble her further. He was delighted with the thought that Ponmani did love him but was simply refusing to show it.

His cart too began to move slowly along the mud-filled road.

With a long sigh, Karpuram came out of the mandapam. She made her decision then and there: somehow, she must prevent Kannan from getting Ponmani. The reason was that Karpuram herself had lost her heart to Kannan's beauty.
