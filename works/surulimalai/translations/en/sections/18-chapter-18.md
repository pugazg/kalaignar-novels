## 18

Kannan stopped beating Singaram only after his own hands began to ache and exhaustion overcame him.

Blood was seeping from Singaram's lips. More blood flowed past his ear. His cheek and forehead had swollen rapidly. He had been standing without moving. Now he let out a hot sigh that stirred the dust on the ground and tried to move a little. His legs could hardly take even one step.

But he did not want to show that weakness in front of so many people. He had faced hardships like this many times in his profession. When he danced enthusiastically carrying the fire-pot, blisters would rise on his hands, sparks would fly, his body would burn. He had grown used to enduring all of that with determination.

So he gritted his teeth and began walking away.

A single silence settled over the crowd. Only Karpuram continued sobbing. Everyone expected the possession to seize her. Fortunately, nothing of the kind happened.

<!-- source boundary: scan 136 → scan 137 -->

Only Chiranjeevi's cry tearing through the silence made the panchayat elder furious.

"Bring a garland! Bring a garland! Shout victory for Karagam-dancer Kannan! Victory to the greatest of heroes who beats a dead snake! Victory! Victory! Victory!"

For this, Chiranjeevi received a rough knock on the head from his father as a prize.

Aravazhi, who had watched all these painful absurdities, found his mind naturally beginning to follow Singaram. The character suggested by Singaram's appearance and the character revealed by his conduct clashed with each other and plunged Aravazhi into confusion. He decided it was essential to learn more about this remarkable man.

He waited until Singaram turned at the end of the street. Once he had turned, Aravazhi began following him. He was overwhelmed by the desire to obtain some explanation for the strange scene he had witnessed.

Singaram stopped nowhere in the street. He entered no house. He sat down nowhere. He simply kept walking slowly toward some unknown place. Aravazhi had to follow at the same pace.

What terrifying whirlwinds raged in Singaram's heart! A cyclone—thunder and lightning—torrential rain—the shock of an earthquake—and amid all of them his heart cried like a motherless child. It whimpered. It screamed "veer, veer." It choked back sobs. It wailed "ko!" With every kind of crying, his inner being throbbed and suffered.

That very sister whom Kannan had stained with humiliating words had now dared to surrender herself to that same Kannan. Not only dared—she had gained the courage to argue that the scoundrel mattered more than her brother. Not only that—she had hardened her heart to the point of saying that her brother must not strike Kannan, while Kannan might beat and even kill her brother as he pleased. She had dismissed sibling affection altogether.

How could Theechatti Singaram bear such an unjust transformation, such a sudden shock?

<!-- source boundary: scan 137 → scan 138 -->

He had guarded his sister Karpuram like his own eyes. At the moment of their deaths, his father and mother had taken a promise from him: he must never cause Karpuram distress, and must let her live according to her wishes.

And his father had secretly taken another oath from him as well. The more Singaram thought of it, the more his chest felt like a wound touched by fire. He pressed his chest with his hand. He feared that, despite himself, his heart and lungs might burst like a balloon unable to hold any more air.

Where, finally, would his feet stop?

Aravazhi followed with that question in mind.

There—the neem tree.

Singaram went and stood beneath it.

Aravazhi watched from a distance.

Singaram lifted his head and stared up at a branch. That was enough. All the pain pressing down upon his heart began pouring out like flame bursting upward from beneath the earth.

"Aiyyo, Maina!" he cried, striking his own face. With a great "ko!" he began to weep.

He sobbed like a small child. Embracing the trunk of the neem tree, he lamented.

<!-- source boundary: scan 138 → scan 139 -->

Aravazhi's astonishment only grew; he still had no explanation. He decided to go near and question him fully.

He came to the tree.

Singaram, crying without awareness of anything around him, noticed only after a long time that someone had placed a hand on his shoulder. Perhaps he did not even think to turn and see who it was. Perhaps he concluded it must be Kannan. Or perhaps the sudden sensation made him lose all thought.

Whatever the reason, the instant he wheeled around he punched Aravazhi in the cheek.

Unable to bear the pain, Aravazhi covered his face and stepped backward.

Only then did Singaram realize he had made a mistake. Even the pain from the wounds Kannan had given him disappeared from his awareness. He stood staring at Aravazhi's face.

Aravazhi could see in that look the gesture of a plea for forgiveness.

"Don't worry! This village and I are a perfect match. The moment I arrived, I got a proper welcome! Excellent beating—kicking—punching—butting! Magnificent hospitality! Grand hospitality!" Aravazhi said with a laugh.

Singaram did not understand every detail, but from the gentleness and sweetness in Aravazhi's voice he understood that the younger man was not angry with him.

He went near, stroked Aravazhi's cheek and pleaded in a trembling voice, "I hit you by mistake, in anger. Forgive me, thambi!"

Tears rose in Aravazhi's eyes. He embraced Singaram and sat down with him on the raised platform beneath the neem tree.

"You are a remarkable man!" Aravazhi said.

<!-- source boundary: scan 139 → scan 140 -->

"You're the greater man, thambi," Singaram replied, patting him affectionately.

"Then may I understand the reason for all these strange things that have happened?" Aravazhi asked eagerly.

Singaram could not speak. Yet he felt as though he had to unload the burden of his heart onto someone at once.

"Have you been watching me from the beginning of the fight in that street?"

"Yes. I've heard a great deal about you. But I simply couldn't discover the reason for the strange way you behaved today."

"The man who beat me until his own hands hurt—that was Karagam-dancer Kannan!"

"I know him very well. I can tell *you* a story about him."

"He is my mortal enemy. The feud between his house and mine goes back to before I was even born in my mother's womb—to the point that the two families would have nothing to do with one another in life or death! And now my thangachi says she's going to marry him. Is that just? You tell me!"

"They say love is blind."

"Looking at my sister's love, it seems we could also say it has no brain, no affection, no attachment, no honour, no sense of shame!"

"Can we keep carrying an old feud forever simply because it began long ago? In the puranas we read that even a tiger and a cow drank from the same watering place at the same pond!"

<!-- source boundary: scan 140 → scan 141 -->

"Oh, be quiet! It must have been a lame tiger—that's why it couldn't pounce. Otherwise it must have quenched its thirst by drinking the cow's blood!"

"Aiyaiyo! You're even speaking against the puranas. Isn't that a sin?"

"Puranas or mud-clods—I will reject everything, and I will celebrate everything. I will never be afraid to say whatever seems right to my mind. I'm no great devotee—and I'm no supreme sinner either!

"My very livelihood is a livelihood of deceiving the village. Even so, I have an unshakable belief that the reason that livelihood runs properly is God's grace, Kali Devi's mercy. The deity shows only a little of the road. After that, isn't it our duty to walk it ourselves? Does the signpost that shows the road to the village come along with us for company?"

Aravazhi saw Singaram piling out whatever was in his mind without any order. He gained hope that by keeping the conversation going he could learn more about him.

"In your life, God has at least stood like a signpost and shown you a road. But for many orphans, I think He has taken the form of a hanging tree and shown only the road to the other world."

Aravazhi said this calmly.

Singaram became confused. The moment the words "hanging tree" reached his ears, Maina and the branch from which she had hung appeared and vanished before his mind's eye.

"Maina!" he said once.

<!-- source boundary: scan 141 → scan 142 -->

"Who is this Maina? What connection does she have with this tree? Could she perhaps...?" Aravazhi let the question trail.

"She? She was the first and the last woman to enter my life—and she is the one who has ruined me like this!" Singaram began weeping again.

"Was she your lover?"

"Don't talk about that and make me cry more. I'm going. If you can, come and see me when this frenzy has died down a little. Right now I don't even understand what I'm saying. Look—I can't even hear with my own ears what my own mouth is saying!"

Singaram rose as he spoke.

Aravazhi understood that a heart turned into a field of pain was making him lose his balance. He stopped him from leaving and seated him beside him again.

"I won't ask anything about Maina! That's what upsets you so much, isn't it? Then tell me this instead: what objection do you have to Kannan marrying your sister?"

"You've seen with your own eyes what kind of man he is. Would any brother give his sister to such a low creature—to such a complete scoundrel—and ruin her?" That was Singaram's answer.

"All right. What if she marries him despite you?"

"I will keep the promise I gave my father that I would never go against my sister's heart. But by doing that,

<!-- source boundary: scan 142 → scan 143 -->

I will throw another promise I gave him into the wind. And after throwing that promise away, why should I live? I too will go to my Maina!"

"What promise did you give your father?"

"Karagam-dancer Kannan's father—or, if that failed, his son, this Kannan. I swore that one of the two would be marked with black and red spots, mounted on a donkey, and paraded through the village! His father escaped. For a long time I've been waiting for the right moment to conduct that ceremony for the son at least. And before I could, this has happened!"

"Aiyyo, appa—what a terrifying oath! Why did you have to swear such a thing? What crime did they commit?"

"Aiyyo! My tongue itself shrinks from telling that crime. Please don't trouble me by asking!"

Aravazhi's curiosity would not subside. The oath was terrifying. The urge for revenge was boiling over. How could he leave without understanding its cause?

He himself was hardly an acquaintance, and he was a little afraid to press too hard. Even so, he did not give up immediately.

"I'm not someone you've known for long. Even so, some feeling has drawn me toward you. I feel I must hear your story. My heart aches to know the reason for your tears. Just thinking of your oath makes me shiver. Yet you refuse to tell me how that oath came into being. That isn't fair.

"You can trust me. Please tell me. However great the secret may be, protecting it

will be my responsibility."

With those words, Aravazhi decided to draw him out.

Singaram did not open his mouth. He sat looking only at Aravazhi.

From far away, the sound of a flute floated toward them on the wind.
