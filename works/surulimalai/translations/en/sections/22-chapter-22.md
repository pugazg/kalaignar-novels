## 22

The thunder and the howling wind drove Ponmani and Aravazhi into still deeper silence. The wind gathered up the heavy rain and joyfully poured it into the school.

She stood motionless.

Rainwater had been flung all over her as though by the handful. More than half her body was soaked.

Aravazhi ran near her.

"Ada-da!" he said sympathetically.

<!-- source boundary: scan 160 → scan 161 -->

She looked at him once as though she would swallow him with her eyes.

"I'm going home," she said, and hurried toward the outside.

"What? In this rain?" Aravazhi came in front and stopped her.

"No! I have to go. The moment Amma sees the rain she'll worry terribly. She'll set out looking for me!"

"What are you—a tiny child? A baby? Don't you even know how to shelter safely somewhere in wind and rain?"

She laughed at the teasing. But tears peeped out immediately after the smile.

"Ah! You smiled like lightning—and at once shed tears like rain! Very good, this too!" Aravazhi teased again.

"All right. I'll go. If you have an umbrella, can I borrow it? I'll send it back with my thambi," Ponmani asked, wiping her eyes.

"An umbrella? I have one. I have no objection to giving it either. I'll give it if *you* bring it back yourself."

"Don't play. If you have one, give it. If not, never mind. I'm leaving."

Ponmani walked toward the doorway. Aravazhi caught her hand and pulled her back.

"What is this stubbornness?" he chided affectionately.

Ponmani said nothing. Nor did she pull her hand out of his.

<!-- source boundary: scan 161 → scan 162 -->

Aravazhi came closer. He lifted the hand he held and pressed it to his forehead.

Though wet with rainwater, her hand felt warm.

"Ponmani! Have I done something wrong to you? Why are you trying to run away like this—in such rain?"

"I'm the one who has done a great wrong. I intended one thing and something else happened. Only after I understood that there could be no forgiveness for this fault did I lose all peace!"

"Don't cry, Ponmani! Don't cry! I no longer consider what you did a fault at all. I've understood you and the generosity of your family."

"Do you truly mean that? Or are you saying it only to console me?"

"Ponmani! When I came to this village, all my thoughts were about Karagam-dancer Kannan. I thought he would be waiting to take revenge. The first stage of that has already happened.

"But I've gained a new courage: whatever troubles he brings me one after another, I will face them by thinking of you. From that, understand how I regard you. You are the lamp of my life! You are the deity filling my heart, kanne!"

"I don't understand anything. Look at that rain, roaring terribly and pouring down with a 'sho!' My mind is being tossed about like the trees and plants in it.

"I'm a young girl. You know the world. If I make a mistake, correct me. Don't join me in it and ruin me too!"

"Ponmani! Foolish girl!"

<!-- source boundary: scan 162 → scan 163 -->

"Uh-uh! This isn't right! Yes—I feel this isn't right. My appa and amma think I'm a very proper girl. I won't betray them!"

"You are betraying no one. Two hearts have become one. This is the path nature has laid down, not a road against it.

"I would never, even for a moment, dare destroy you by becoming a slave to mere frenzy and emotion. I want to learn the language of love that youth gives us without violating the bounds of its grammar.

"I will not step onto the path of men without limits who stain women's chastity, held dearer than life, and play with their lives, even if I become a blind pigeon.

"You can trust me without fear, Ponmani!"

"I never said I don't trust you! I never said you'd abandon me in midstream either. I only meant that we shouldn't do something childish and then regret it ourselves afterward."

"Look at the water running like a river through the street in this rain. When I was a boy, I used to make boats and float them in water like that.

"I'd fold a square sheet of paper into four, bend one corner, make it into a little boat, and set it afloat in the water running down the street. Watching that boat, I'd walk a long way in the rain. My whole body would get soaked. That night I'd get a fever. My parents would suffer.

"Why did I make a boat I couldn't ride in? Why did I soak myself in the rain just to watch the beauty of it sailing? Why did I then grieve when it sank in the rainwater?<!-- verified source join: scan 163→164 --> Those questions occur to me now. They don't occur in childhood.

"But I can say one thing with certainty: I would not make such a boat now. I would not run after it in the rain either.

"The boat I'm building now is the boat of life. My queen and I will board it together. Do you understand, Ponmani? Please understand that building *this* boat is no childish game!"

"Don't even ships built by grown men sometimes sink?"

"They say even the world itself will break apart one day. Even the sun may one day shatter into dust like pieces of broken glass. If human beings thought only about such things, they could never live!"

"What am I beside all your learning? Whatever you say, I simply have to believe it, don't I?"

"Aiyyo! These days it's the educated man who cheats the village most. That's exactly why scholars say everyone must be educated!"

"Why? So everyone can join together and cheat the village?"

"No! If everyone becomes educated and capable, then no one will be able to cheat anyone else, will they?"

"Our village Kannan came to your village and fooled everyone by saying he would take out buried plates. Was he highly educated?"

"Well, he has at least read the old puranas, hasn't he? Leave that aside—our investigation has wandered somewhere else entirely!"

With that he put a full stop to the conversation and embraced Ponmani affectionately.

<!-- source boundary: scan 164 → scan 165 -->

"Uss!" Ponmani made a scolding sound and moved to pull away.

At that exact moment one terrible peal of thunder followed another.

"Veer!" she screamed and ran into his arms. Her body trembled violently.

"Don't be afraid! Say the mantra 'Arjuna! Arjuna!' The thunder will run away!" Aravazhi laughed, holding her tightly.

"Why should I say *his* name?" she asked mischievously, looking at Aravazhi as she wound her soft arms around his neck.

"Shall the two of us sing:

> Let us praise the great rain!  
> Let us praise the great rain!

?" Aravazhi asked.

Affectionately she pressed her cheek against the line of his jaw and closed her eyes in bliss.

Aravazhi too had lost himself in the pleasure of the touch. Then his eyes turned toward the table.

Rainwater was dripping onto the copy of *Kalithogai* lying there; the whole book had become soaked.

He ran over and picked it up. The cover came away in his hand. The wet pages too began coming loose in his hands. Only the *Neithal Kali* portion remained together.

There his eyes caught the beautiful line:

> வாடை தூக்க வணங்கிய தாழை!

He savoured the meaning of the line. He delighted inwardly, telling himself that little by little he must give *her* such turns too.

"Do you know what's in this entire book?" he asked.

<!-- source boundary: scan 165 → scan 166 -->

"Rainwater," she answered sharply.

"Rain, yes! All of it pearl-rain! Sugar-rain! Honey-rain! A rain of delight that never cloys! Kanne, this book is called *Kalithogai*. It's all about love! The love of the cowherd girl who sells milk! The love of the fisherwoman who catches fish!... Ada-da..."

"Then... isn't the love of a girl who sells pots and vessels in it?"

"Everything is in it! This book is a mirror to the loving hearts of Tamil women!"

"Leave that aside. It's getting late. I have to go."

"Does even a thought like that occur to you?"

"See? Only just now you said we mustn't become slaves to emotion. Can we forget the whole world? Can we push aside mother, father, family and everything else?"

"True, Ponmani. Not only mother, father and family—the country, which stands above all of them. If harm comes to these, I will not forget that love is the feeling that should deepen these bonds, not destroy them.

"You may go. I'm not stopping you. But one thing: have we finished saying everything we need to say?"

"Are we never going to meet again?"

"If you wish—if your heart wishes—we can always meet.

"The rain and wind are unbearable. I'll take you home myself."

<!-- source boundary: scan 166 → scan 167 -->

"If the two of us go together, what will the villagers say if they see us?"

"No one will come outside in this rain. Only the two of us will be walking under one umbrella through water running knee-deep in the street.

"Who knows whether this rain will give us such a wonderful chance again soon after we're married? So let's enjoy it now. Come!"

Aravazhi took the umbrella and opened it.

A scorpion dropped out from inside and looked for somewhere to run.

The moment Ponmani saw it, her eyes met Aravazhi's without her intending it, full of mischief.

"I'll catch the scorpion for you. Shall I make it sting your hand?" he asked.

"No! No! The mantra you blew into my ear before is what has brought me to this state. Don't keep blowing in my ear again and inflate my heart like a balloon until it bursts!"

Ponmani rested her face on his shoulder and let out a long sigh.

The wind only grew stronger. The rain continued pouring.

Ponmani became increasingly desperate to reach home somehow, so Aravazhi set out with her.

They pushed open the door against the wind and stepped outside. Aravazhi had to grip the umbrella with great force. Ponmani walked close against him beneath it.

The whole sky had darkened and cast darkness over the earth too. The rain was so dense they could barely see. The wind hurled the rain<!-- verified source join: scan 167→168 --> drops against them like stones.

Trees along the street seemed to threaten that they might fall at any moment.

Aravazhi and Ponmani began to tremble with the fear that coming outside had been a mistake. They had no confidence that they could reach home safely.

At intervals came terrifying thunder that shook the chest, and lightning that stole the sight from their eyes.

Ignoring all of it, Aravazhi held Ponmani tightly and kept walking.

The force of the wind began tearing the umbrella from his hand.

They lost it and took shelter beneath a tree.

Right before their eyes, a tree beside the one under which they stood crashed to the ground.

Now even standing beneath a tree seemed dangerous.

Whatever happened, they decided, they had to reach home.

Holding each other's hands, they walked on through rain pouring like a waterfall.
