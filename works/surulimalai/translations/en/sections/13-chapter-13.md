## 13

"They say my staying there would bring danger to that entire village. So the officials decided in a great hurry that I had to be transferred somewhere else!" Aravazhi said.

"Looking at you, child, you seem as gentle as a cow! What danger could *you* bring a village, thambi?" Pooncholai asked eagerly.

<!-- source boundary: scan 99 → scan 100 -->

"I said the people in the village ought to be intelligent. Apparently that was a very wrong thing to say. I told them to live frugally instead of wasting the money they earned; that was a crime. I tried to make everyone live in a civilized and hygienic way; that was a sin. So they drove me out!"

"Aiyyo! What's wrong in saying all that?... What are those villagers spending so much money on, thambi? Too much drinking?"

"They spend on things worse than drink. Someone comes along, says something, cheats them and takes their money. One day they hold a kumbabhishekam. The next they conduct two festivals!"

"Thambi, thambi—what? Is performing kumbabhishekam and holding festivals bad spending? What are you saying, thambi? Now I can see why those villagers drove you out! Aiyaiyo... are you against the gods? That's why even our temple bull chased you!"

"I'm not against God, amma. I'm the true devotee! I'm against the man who cheats the village in God's name. God has no anger toward me. The man who makes a living out of God is the one angry with me. Why would your temple bull chase me? Had it learned all about me the instant I arrived?"

"Why wouldn't it know, thambi? It's God's bull! It knows everything!"

"There is a great deal of connection between your temple bull knocking me down and that man in your village—Karagam-dancer Kannan."

<!-- source boundary: scan 100 → scan 101 -->

"Who—Karagam-dancer Kannan? Why would he be angry with you? Oh-ho... that's right! Kannan is a great karagam dancer!... And there's no deity he doesn't know. He's a great sorcerer too! Should you go and quarrel with a man like that? That's why he cast a spell on the temple bull and sent it after you."

"In our village I proved that all his magic and tricks were nothing but deception. The humiliation he suffered from that made him try to take revenge the moment I arrived here. That's all," Aravazhi said. He straightened slightly and looked toward where Ponmani stood. She remained there, motionless.

Even in his mind flashed the thought that his transfer away from the other village had perhaps turned out for the best. When he looked at her, Ponmani abruptly dropped to the ground the gaze she had let rest upon him. He did not fail to notice that either. He felt as though he ought to go on talking. Even the pain in his body seemed to have withdrawn. He waited eagerly, wondering whether Pooncholai-amma would ask another question.

Pooncholai, however, had become a little confused. He was talking about ideas she did not understand—new things, things opposed to the old beliefs she had trusted until then. That was the source of her confusion. Even so, she did not come to dislike Aravazhi. She was ready to forgive him as a young fellow speaking out of the hot blood of youth. His affectionate face awakened a new fondness for him in Pooncholai-amma.

<!-- source boundary: scan 101 → scan 102 -->

Surulimalai listened with his usual indifference, though he looked as if he were waiting for something inside Aravazhi's talk. They all remained silent, thinking about Aravazhi, but none of them asked a new question that would give him a chance to speak. Aravazhi too waited for a little while. Then he himself began continuing his story.

"I said I had humiliated your village's Kannan in our village, didn't I? None of you asked how. Here—I'll tell you myself. Listen!" Aravazhi said, bringing a smile to his lips and looking from one to another of the three.

Ponmani's gaze was full of eagerness. Pooncholai-amma looked at him. "What you're saying is astonishing, thambi. Kannan is a great sorcerer. For you to say such things about him..." she let the sentence trail away.

"The people of our village too had made the same mistaken calculation about him. Even I, when I first saw him, thought he would not be a cheat; I thought he must be someone who openly explained the tricks he performed, displayed his skill and earned payment for it. But he tried to deceive me as well," Aravazhi said.

Ponmani could barely contain her excitement. Aravazhi held the very opinion of Karagam-dancer Kannan that she herself did. That gave her enormous pleasure. She burned to know how Kannan had been caught by her beloved Aravazhi.

At the same time another fear came over her.

<!-- source boundary: scan 102 → scan 103 -->

Between herself and Aravazhi stood Karagam-dancer Kannan not only as a lustful man, but also as an enemy waiting for a chance to take revenge on Aravazhi. That was the fear.

Aravazhi continued the story of Kannan:

"For ten days before Karagam-dancer Kannan was due to come to our village, there was nothing but excitement. Everyone in that whole area said no one could dance karagam like him. There was a great festival at the village Mariamman temple. The final day of the festival was to feature Kannan's karagam dance.

"One of the local leading men was not only the trustee of the Mariamman temple; what he said was law in the whole village. By his authority, arrangements had been made for Kannan to stay in our school building. I too lived in a room in the school because there was no other housing in that village. Even if one or two houses happened to be available, they had told me they would not rent to unmarried men. So I had been compelled to stay in the school itself."

"Oh-ho! You still aren't married, thambi?" Pooncholai interrupted. Ponmani overflowed with delight.

"No," he said, turning toward her before continuing.

"Karagam-dancer Kannan, whom the villagers had been longing and waiting to see, arrived at our school early in the morning on the appointed day. His appearance did not make me dislike him. There was something rural in his dress and bearing, and a rural warmth in the way he spoke. His karagam dance was only in the evening.

<!-- source boundary: scan 103 → scan 104 -->

"Until then he stayed at the school. When there's a village festival, can there be school? So I had a good holiday too. During the day Kannan and I had a chance to talk and get acquainted. Within a short time of beginning the conversation, I clearly understood what kind of man he was. He invited me to come without fail to the karagam dance that evening. 'It is an art, after all. I'll come and see it,' I said. 'How can you say that? Art, dirt—it's all divine power,' he argued.

"I went that evening to watch the festival. As everyone in the village had said, he had learned the karagam art magnificently. Even now I am prepared to praise the way he spun like a top with the karagam balanced on his head.

"He danced while winding a spiral hoop around himself. He knelt and crawled, while making only the karagam on his head spin rapidly.

"The knife was blunt at the edge; even so, he stood upon it and danced, answering the challenging thunder of the pambai and udukkai. The performance captivated everyone. The sight still has not left my eyes.

"It was only when the man who had given such a splendid feast of art suddenly began sacrificing the villagers to 'divine power' that the circumstances arose for such a great enmity to grow between us!"

Aravazhi let out a long sigh after saying this. Though they did not grasp the deeper meaning of his words, Pooncholai, Ponmani and Surulimalai listened as though listening to a story.

<!-- source boundary: scan 104 → scan 105 -->

Even Aravazhi himself wondered whether he was speaking as though to people as informed as himself—and whether they could understand. His speech flowed from his natural enthusiasm.

"Hm! Then what happened?" Pooncholai asked.

Ah, they really *are* listening eagerly, he thought, and resumed where he had left off.

"Karagam-dancer Kannan finally reached the temple entrance and suddenly began dancing as though divine possession had come upon him. The trustee and the village elders went near and asked for boons. Kannan told them, 'Everyone's wishes will be fulfilled.' Then, as if throwing a huge bomb among them, he announced a terrifying piece of news to the villagers:

"'A Malayali sorcerer, wanting to bring great ruin upon this village, has buried metal plates in all four directions. If they are not taken out at once, the whole village will be destroyed!'

"The villagers immediately cried out and begged, 'Somehow take out those plates and save us, Mother!' After that the deity departed.

"Karagam-dancer Kannan promised that he himself would use mantras to remove the plates supposedly buried by the Malayali sorcerers. He asked for a hundred rupees as an offering. The trustee agreed to provide it. They decided the plates would be removed the next morning.

"Kannan returned to the school and lay down. He even asked me for my criticism of the karagam dance. I praised it greatly. He was delighted. Then he said he was sleepy and sent me back to my room.

<!-- source boundary: scan 105 → scan 106 -->

"I came to my room and lay down, thinking over one thing and another until I fell asleep. It must have been around two in the morning when I suddenly woke. After that I could not sleep and kept turning in bed. It seemed someone came near my room. I lay motionless.

"'Vathiyare!' came a voice. It was Kannan's voice. I stayed silent.

"Kannan moved away from the room. I slowly got up and came outside. I could not see him. I went to his bed—the bedding was empty. I came toward the street. Kannan was walking along it. I felt I had to follow him. So I followed..."
