## 5

"You poured all that salt in because you don't want me to eat even this, didn't you? Never mind—there is a proverb, 'Remember as long as you live the one who gave you salt.' I'll remember you forever," Peethambaram went on, needling her with mischievous humour. Maina had no idea how to manage this awkward situation.

"Why are you keeping a face like a lump of manure? Your husband has come after so many days—shouldn't you welcome me with your mouth all teeth?" Peethambaram asked.

Without realizing it, she snapped back:

"Only if there were teeth in the mouth could I welcome you like that!"

Peethambaram understood what she meant. But without showing it, he laughed and asked, "What, *you* have no teeth? They're beautiful, like rows of mullai buds!"

"These are all false teeth! Bought in a shop to deceive the village!" Maina said angrily.

Peethambaram was pleased. Somehow, all he had wanted was to get her talking. Conversation began to grow between them.

"Have you seen the play *Nalayini*, Maina?"

"No. I've only seen *Alli Arjunan* and been deceived by it!"

"*Alli Arjunan* is a love story. *Nalayini* is the story that explains true devotion to a husband!"

<!-- source boundary: scan 44 → scan 45 -->

"Have you performed the play *Valli Thirumanam*?"

"No..."

"You're lying! Haven't you performed *Valli Thirumanam* upside down?"

"Upside down? When? Where?"

"When? Have you forgotten? In your own life! In the old *Valli Thirumanam*, Velan comes as a *viruthan*, an old man. In the version you performed, the *viruthan* came as Velan—isn't that so?"

"Maina! Lord Muruga did not come directly and marry Valli. He came disguised as an old man. Why? It is a philosophy explaining the truth of human life! Youth does not last forever—one day old age surrounds a man. The disguise of the old man was meant to show that truth and strengthen Valli's mind!"

"Valli didn't think, 'Youth will disappear one day anyway,' and ask Muruga to remain an old man, did she? She married him only after he came as a young man!"

"He was God—that is why the old man could turn into Velan!"

"Then don't tell stories about gods and use them as examples for ordinary human beings!"

"Enough circling around it... Maina—why did you run away without telling anyone?"

"You should have understood that without my telling you!"

<!-- source boundary: scan 45 → scan 46 -->

"You were the one who wrote to me. You begged that you had fallen in love with me and couldn't change it. You even sent your picture and bewitched me. Then, after the marriage happened according to your own wish, you ran away. Is that fair?"

"An elephant that falls into a pit thinking it's green grass struggles to escape somehow, doesn't it? That's my story too!"

"Maina! At least speak with some respect for the mangalyam lying around your neck. I've cast aside honour and shame and come begging to you. The name Peethambaram has fame throughout the town—don't ruin it! If people say, 'Peethambaram's wife ran away with someone,' I'd rather hang myself and die!"

"Forgive me! Forget me!"

"Impossible! I simply cannot forget you! I used to weep over my theatrical life, which was like a desert. Then our marriage took place and made me forget all of that. Maina, on the wedding day you must have been happy—you thought I was Manmathan then! But all your happiness could never have competed with the happiness I felt. I was proud that a fruit beyond reach had fallen into the hands of a cripple. I believed that somehow the truth would come to light, and afterward you too would console yourself by saying, 'Whatever else he may be, he is still my husband.' You have thrown dirt over that hope and joy now!"

<!-- source boundary: scan 46 → scan 47 -->

"Are you trying to make *me* the guilty one and escape? You too must once have been a fine young man. Think of that! I'm asking without shame—what kinds of thoughts arise in a young woman of that age! Are you fit for all of those? Think and tell me!"

"What is there to think about? Ask even a blind man and he'll say I'm not fit, Maina! I didn't come to persuade you by talking of fitness and rank. You yourself came and married me. Now you yourself have abandoned me. Is that fair? I came to plead with you and take you back."

"Just live as though no event called a marriage between you and me ever happened!"

"They say some doctor once told a patient, 'When you take the medicine, don't think of a monkey.' This is just like that!"

"What's the use of wasting words?"

"That's exactly what I'm saying. Come with me at once!"

"I can't! I can't! Even if my life goes, I can never live with you again!"

"Then don't live with me! Just come and stay in my house. At least help me shut the village's mouth."

"Has the village's mouth stayed shut until now? By this time they must have said all sorts of things about me. Is it only from now on that they are going to talk?"

<!-- source boundary: scan 47 → scan 48 -->

"The story in the village is that you've gone to Singanallur. I told everyone who asked that the hour of the wedding wasn't auspicious, so you had gone to your native place and would return after two months!"

"Listen to what I'm saying. When a wife says she has no desire to live with her husband, what's the gain in being more and more stubborn?"

"Don't live with satisfaction. Live with dissatisfaction! Somehow, I only need you to be in my house!"

"Am I supposed to sit in your house like a doll?"

"Live like a queen!"

"I may as well live like an animal! Even an animal wouldn't get a bad time like mine!"

"We'll treat you as the mistress of our house. Treat me like your slave! What more can I say, Maina?"

"Do you think I want to come there, make everyone dance to my tune and rule the house? I'll wander as a beggar—I'll even be a servant in your house—but if my mind has no satisfaction, what use is the office of mistress to me?"

"Your mind should be satisfied. Here, I swear right now—on my family deity! On my profession! I will not touch you. I'll treat you exactly like my daughter. Is that enough?"

"If you truly mean to treat me like a daughter, then treat me like a daughter right now. Your daughter has been married and has come to her husband's house. Appa—<!-- verified source join: scan 48→49 -->—imagine that you've come to visit your daughter, and *you alone* go back to the village!"

"Maina! You are my daughter—please forgive me!"

Maina had not expected it in the least. Peethambaram fell at her feet. She could do nothing. Once again she became motionless.

It did not feel to her as though Peethambaram were lying at her feet and pleading. It felt instead as though her husband had assumed a terrifying cosmic form, lifted her lightly in a hand of red-hot iron and crushed her with a *nara-nara*, splintering her ribs, crushing her heart, and making blood burst out. He might have beaten her. He might have cut her. He might have smashed her skull. Perhaps she could have borne all of that. But she could not bear to see him curled at her feet.

Tears poured without her knowing. In a faint voice she said, "Please get up!" and lifted him by the shoulder.

"It's true, Maina! I'll treat you as my daughter. You asked me to forget our marriage—I certainly will. You forget it too. We'll live as daughter and father. I'll say even more, daughter: I'll find a young man suitable for you and marry you to him!"

Before Peethambaram could finish saying this through tears, Maina clutched his feet tightly and sobbed, "Aiyo! Forgive me! Don't say such things!"

Now she trembled even to look at Peethambaram's face. The man she had thought of as a demon that kept her alive only to torture her inch by inch—that demon<!-- verified source join: scan 49→50 --> now seemed, for a moment, to stand before her in a pleading posture, begging for his own life. The next moment, within that embrace of affection, it felt as though the demon's cruel sharp claws had unknowingly torn open her breast and punctured her heart.

She thought of how, on that first night, both her mind and body had been ruined. Sweat poured from her. The fear that she had fallen into a pit of sin from which there was no return shook her still more. She became like a madwoman.

"Don't touch me—I'm a sinner—I'm a sinner!" she screamed.

"Maina! Maina!" Peethambaram called.

Maina began running like a woman possessed. Peethambaram followed, but his age was of no help in stopping her flight.

Maina ran straight into the temple administrator's house. Only the administrator was there.

"Is Theechatti Singaram here?" she asked breathlessly.

The administrator delayed a second before answering. Then he said, "Singaram? Yes, he's inside!"

Maina ran into the house. No one was there. It was completely silent. She turned and ran back toward the entrance. The outer door had been bolted.

How was she to get out?

She looked up.

Laughing *kada-kada*, the temple administrator approached her.

Maina screamed.

No one heard that scream. Maina was trapped in the administrator's hands. Only then was the fire that had been burning for so many days in the administrator's heart extinguished.

For the third time, her chastity was stained!

<!-- source boundary: scan 50 → scan 51 -->

The temple administrator looked at her with a victorious smile. She smiled too.

The administrator opened the large wooden chest in the hall. Inside it was a beautiful little box. He took two five-rupee notes from it and held them out to Maina.

She shook her head as though to say, "Not enough."

The administrator went back and opened the box again.

The moment Maina had been waiting for had come.

She hurriedly lifted the oil-dripping kuthuvilakku beside her. In the same movement she brought it down hard on the back of the administrator's neck.

Even the administrator's cry of "Aiyyo!" was not heard outside.

She did not stop to see whether that wretch was dead or alive. She opened the door and ran out.

From the entrance she stared wildly. In the distance Peethambara Bhagavathar was walking away, carrying his beloved kitten in his arms. She ran after him. He kept walking without turning.

Suddenly, at some thought, she stopped. She no longer wished to follow him.

Like a woman seized by madness, she stared at the wide open space before her. She felt as though her whole body were burning and flames were being poured into her heart. On her own she kept muttering, "Sinner! Sinner!" through heavy breaths.

The young woman who had stepped onto the threshold of life with such pride and joy, delighted that she was entering it with so much enthusiasm, now stood like a crushed flower, a scorched shoot, forced to greet a future that looked like emptiness itself through eyes full of tears.

Who was left to support her now? Theechatti Singaram?

How could she go to him and describe her condition? Was she to say, "I hated my husband. I found you as my lover. I also submitted to the desire of a lustful man"?

Tch! Tch! Better to give up her life. That alone was the proper end.

But before dying, should fate not at least let her see Singaram once? Could she not die with at least the consolation of having seen him?

How?

She made her decision. She would see him.

Her feet moved toward Sirur.

What could stones, thorns and the rough road do to her? She had already decided to die—what did it matter what happened to this body now? A thorn tore near her shoulder and blood seeped out. There, on her cheek, blood had clotted where the temple administrator's teeth had struck her.

There was no stain in the blood flowing from the wound made by the thorn. Even famous experts in blood analysis, if they examined it, would say it was pure blood.

But the blood clotted unseen upon her cheek—the consequence of the temple administrator's teeth—that blood had become sewer water capable of breeding terrible germs. That was why she walked on without caring about the blood flowing from the thorn-cut wound.

<!-- source boundary: scan 51 → scan 52 -->

The rough stones stabbing her feet, softer than a cat's paws, could clearly be seen in the anguish that showed in her eyes. She paid no attention even to that. What did it matter how this body was damaged? Tomorrow morning they would burn it, scorch it, reduce it to ash.

Because it was a suicide corpse, doctors would cut it open and examine it. If those doctors conducting the post-mortem were truly skilled, then—just as one puts a wristwatch to the ear to see whether it is running—if during the post-mortem they took out the heart, held it to the ear and listened, it might surely tell them the true reason for the suicide.

The body they describe as gold, treasure, flower, ruby—they would lay it on the earth, stack dung-cakes over it, light the fire, and erase every sign that it had ever been there.

Until then, what did it matter how many stones stabbed her? What did it matter how many thorns tore her? Even if the whole body became one wound, she did not care. If only her eyes remained so she could see Singaram for the last time, that was enough.

Thoughts, sighs, and a courage that cared nothing for any obstacle before her—all together brought her to Sirur.

She reached a Pillaiyar-temple mandapam at the edge of Sirur. Someone inside was singing:

> Two trees beside the road,  
> trees the Sarkar planted,  
> trees that grew up tall—my golden gem!  
> A hanging tree fit for you—my precious jewel!

A voice kept singing the song again and again. Maina stood there, surrendering her heart to it.

Then another song!

"Who are you, wicked woman...? Speak, you burning-fruit demon!" sang Panchayat Paramasivam's cherished son loudly as he came and stood before her.

How was she to know that he was mad? She herself had now become three-quarters mad!

<!-- source boundary: scan 52 → scan 53 -->

"This is Sirur, isn't it?" Maina asked him softly.

"Yes! This is Sirur! I'm the panchayat elder's son! My name is Chiranjeevi! What do you want?"

The madman began to babble. She did not understand that.

"Where is Theechatti Singaram's house?" she asked eagerly.

"It's in this village. Theechatti Singaram's village is this village. My village is this village too. Singaram's wife's village is this village too!"

The madman's answer stunned her.

"What? Is Singaram married?"

<!-- source boundary: scan 53 → scan 54 -->

"Why—are *you* planning to marry him? Can't do it. Singaram's already married, and a child is even going to be born."

"Really? Are you telling the truth?"

"I'm Harischandra's son. Why would I lie to you? Singaram's marriage is over. Only the funeral rites are left now!"

"Is that so? Oh God!"

"You want a god? There—inside the temple. Go talk to him. I'm leaving."

Then Chiranjeevi ran away singing:

> I'm going, I'm going, Kattabommu!  
> I'm going off to war, Kattabommu!  
> Give me leave to go, mother Vellaiyamma!  
> If I go, I won't return, Vellaiyamma!

<!-- verified source join: scan 54→55 -->

Maina could not bear the shock of that disappointment. The man she had run all this way to see one last time before dying—she decided he too was a cheat who had deceived her.

The madman's babble produced a great convulsion in her heart. Why see Singaram now? Maina's mind went numb.

She herself did not know how long she sat in that mandapam. The world began to darken. She hurried to bring midnight stillness quickly into her own life, which had already gone dark.

She had expected that at least one man in this world would worry for her, would shed tears over her corpse. Now that Singaram too had become a traitor in her mind, she cursed him to her heart's content. She judged him a scoundrel who had treated her as a shop-bought thing to satisfy hunger wherever he happened to find it.

At that very time, while she was cursing him, Singaram was describing his tour to his sister Karpuram. Overflowing with happiness, he spoke to her about the welcomes, hospitality and gifts he had received in every village.

Was not his meeting with Maina itself the foundation of that happiness? He did not know how to tell his sister about the meeting and the decision born from it.

<!-- source boundary: scan 55 → scan 56 -->

She was deeply worried that the fire-pot had broken.

"You must buy another fire-pot the very next day if the family is to prosper. Otherwise a terrible sin will occur!" she warned her brother.

Singaram was desperate to somehow bring up Maina in the course of conversation and obtain Karpuram's consent to begin a household with her. He slowly opened the subject. Perhaps he thought the best way was to begin with Karpuram's own marriage.

"You're getting older. Shouldn't we at least get your marriage done this year, and quickly?" he began.

"So you've started the marriage talk! Fine—shut your mouth!" she snapped. Then, laughing, she added, "What's my hurry? First *you* agree to get married. I've already found a beautiful girl for you!"

"You found a girl? Karpuram, I've saved you the trouble—I found one myself!" Singaram said.

The conversation moved exactly as she wanted.

"Where did you see her, Anna? What village? Whose daughter?"

"On the tour. A very beautiful woman. A heart of gold. Her name is Maina!"

"Mother and father?"

"No mother or father!"

<!-- source boundary: scan 56 → scan 57 -->

"Brothers... younger brothers?"

"No one!"

"Then? An orphan girl?"

"Yes—almost an orphan. She had a husband. He's not there now either."

"What?"

"Yes. She is a married woman!"

"Ah! A *vazhavetty*—a separated woman!"

<!-- source boundary: scan 57 → scan 58 -->

Karpuram became emotional. As always when emotion overtook her, possession seized her too. She began to dance in divine frenzy.

When Karpuram was possessed, that was the end of it—she would leap nearly to the roof-beam, climb onto the loft and sit there. The villagers said it was Bhadrakali's possession. They would run in to ask for oracles.

For Singaram, the great problem now was how to handle Karpuram. What if the villagers gathered? If she blurted out his secret before everyone, it would become a great embarrassment.

So he pulled the door shut, folded his hands before Karpuram and pleaded, "Amma! Mother! Bhadrakali! Forgive me!"

"I forgive you! Hey—will you forget her? Will you forget her?" Karpuram shouted.

After shouting for a while she fainted and fell down.

The deity had climbed the hill—the possession was over, in its way.

Singaram felt some relief. As usual, he sprinkled water on her face and brought her around. Rubbing her eyes, Karpuram looked at her brother before her. Tears flowed from her eyes.

"Don't cry, Karpuram!" Singaram said tenderly.

"Anna! Forget that separated woman. That sort of thing won't suit our family. I've found a wonderful girl for you!" Karpuram said in a trembling voice.

"Who is this miraculous girl?" Singaram asked merely to humour her.

"Our Pooncholai Amma's daughter Ponmani!" Karpuram said with delight.

"I want neither *pon* nor *mani*. I want my Maina!" Singaram said, and strode out quickly.

"Is that so? Your wish! But I swear one thing: I will not stay alive to see your wife!" Karpuram shouted.

<!-- source boundary: scan 58 → scan 59 -->

Singaram walked toward the next street, thinking he might find a little medicine to ease the torment in his mind.

His own sister had sprung up as the first obstacle to beginning life with Maina. Singaram believed he could moisten the heart that had dried up from thinking about it by soaking it in sour liquor.

He drank heavily until he forgot his condition. Singaram was not the only one who forgot that when the intoxication cleared, grief would raise its head again from every corner and begin digging into the brain. Everyone who treats liquor as medicine for sorrow forgets the same thing.

As dawn approached, the intoxication lessened. He remembered telling Karpuram about Maina the night before, and remembered her answer. The infatuation he had developed for Maina felt strong enough to smash every obstacle his sister placed before him. That feeling rose within him and made him stand upright.

Somehow he had to begin a fine household life with Maina in Sirur. Whatever side opposition came from, whoever opposed him, he would not care.

Maina was not merely beautiful. She had affection and good character. She had been stunned by an unexpected deception in life. She had no support in the world except him. If he too abandoned her, she would turn mad. He must immediately attend to arrangements for their household—Singaram thought intensely and came to this decision.

The night before, when he had been thoroughly drunk, he had begun singing love songs about Maina to the tune of the cremation-ground episode. If his companions had not stopped him, he would have staggered and staggered until, by accident or otherwise, he reached the neem-tree side.

<!-- source boundary: scan 59 → scan 60 -->

He would have seen Maina, already prepared for suicide. Her suspicion of him would have been cleared. She would have told him of the temple administrator's crooked deed. Singaram's fury would have turned upon the administrator, leading to many disastrous consequences.

None of this happened. Intoxication held him down.

She hung from the neem like sugarcane hanging from a tree.

She had been born in Singanallur. Rejoicing that she had attained a fine life fragrant to the mind, she had placed her foot on the first step of life.

From that moss-covered step she slipped, fell headlong, and fainted. When she opened her eyes again, Singaram came like a drop of honey falling upon a tongue dried with thirst. Then the temple administrator intervened like scorpion venom poured over that drop of honey.

Maina's mind had been utterly ruined. Now the only support left to her in the world was that heavy neem branch. Her connection with the world would end with those two cubits of rope.

How boldly she climbed the neem tree in that darkness!

What had she to fear? Should she be afraid that she might slip and fall? Even if she fell, the life she wished to end would simply go. So she climbed swiftly onto the branch. She tightened the rope around her neck and fastened it to the limb.

"Appa!" she called once in a sobbing voice.

How much flesh—how much living weight—was contained in that single word! How much her father had suffered with his daughter's happiness as his one aim! How happy he had been to think that they had chosen a bridegroom pleasing to his daughter's heart!

Fortunately, he had closed his eyes without seeing any of these cruelties. A sigh of consolation escaped her.

Perhaps that was beautiful Maina's last breath.

The next instant she jumped from the branch and swung from the rope. After a few seconds of the body's struggle for life, Maina became still.

There was no grief in her heart now. Peethambara Bhagavathar's form could no longer come before her eyes and threaten her. The inward eye that had trembled again and again at the terrible image of the temple administrator had gone completely dim.

There was no longer any space for waves of memory about Theechatti Singaram to rise.

She had released herself—but she had gone after making Singaram writhe.

From the moment he had left her, he had repeated her name like a mantra. The sight struck him like thunder falling on his head. Every plan he had made to begin a household with her shattered into pieces.

Panchayat Paramasivam urgently ordered that the unconscious Singaram be carried at once into some nearby house. A man ran to fetch the doctor.

Meanwhile they carried Singaram to Pooncholai's house and laid him down. Pooncholai Amma panicked and tried all sorts of treatments to bring him out of the faint. Nothing worked.

Hearing of the danger to her brother, Karpuram ran to Pooncholai's house. The moment she saw Singaram lying there motionless, she became overwhelmed by emotion. Possession seized her.

<!-- source boundary: scan 60 → scan 61 -->

For Pooncholai and Ponmani, looking after *her* now became the larger task!

"Aahoy! Hey, people! This is the deity's fault! The deity's fault! Nothing will happen to this life—don't be afraid! Don't be afraid!" Karpuram began shouting.

Old Ponnaiya slowly rose, coughing "lok, lok," and handed Karpuram the bowl of vibhuti from the wall-niche. Karpuram scooped up the vibhuti and flung it about, gave Singaram two or three hard blows on the head, and then, as the frenzy turned into exhaustion, sat down where she was.

By then the doctor had arrived. Medicinal smoke intended to break the faint was administered through Singaram's nose.

By this time Karpuram's possessed feeling had completely subsided too. Yet she sat motionless. Her brain was working furiously.

She planned that she could use the possession that came upon her for a good purpose.

Until then, possession had come without her knowing it. A sudden shocking event, terrible news—such things would make her lose control and begin the deity-dance. Now, however, she deliberately decided to fake possession as a stratagem.

She remembered the argument between herself and her brother the night before. From that she guessed that the woman who had hanged herself must indeed be Maina.

Had Karpuram not wanted to tie a knot between her brother and Ponmani? She decided to create a good situation for that.

<!-- source boundary: scan 61 → scan 62 -->

Just as the doctor's efforts made Singaram slowly open his eyes, Karpuram again shouted, "Hoy!" and began the deity-dance.

"Hey, people! Listen—the danger that has come upon this life isn't an ordinary danger! That woman who hanged herself under the neem tree... her life has turned into a ghost and fastened itself onto this life!..." Karpuram screamed.

Hearing this, Singaram cried, "Yes! Yes! It's fastened onto *me*! Let me go to my Maina! Maina! Maina!!" He tried to get up and run.

The others would not let him. They seized him and made him lie down.

Karpuram continued.

"See? See the ghost's mischief? People! This life must not be allowed outside! For ten days this life must not leave this house! If he goes out, the ghost on this life will attach itself to someone else in this house... For ten days he must be kept lying here, and with mantra—tantra—medicine the ghost must be killed atom by atom. Only then will this life survive! Only then can the people in the house escape!"

Karpuram danced in possession and fell to the floor.

The deity had climbed the hill, in one fashion.

Pooncholai was seized with terror. Fear that if Singaram went outside the ghost would possess someone in the house shook the woman badly.

Karpuram, whose deity had now "climbed the hill," sprang toward Pooncholai as though she knew nothing and began wailing, "Aiyo! What's happened to my Anna?"

<!-- source boundary: scan 62 → scan 63 -->

Singaram repeatedly tried to rise crying, "Maina! Maina!" and the people around him repeatedly seized him and made him lie down. It became a major task.

Karpuram insisted on taking her brother home. Pooncholai absolutely refused. As the family deity had said, she declared firmly that Singaram must remain in her house for ten days and receive treatment there.

Inside, Karpuram was delighted.

The first stage of the plan she had made had succeeded magnificently: her brother and Ponmani would get a chance to move closely with each other; from that they would fall in love and become ready for marriage.

Until now the possession that came without her knowledge had been a great torment to her. Now she felt full of satisfaction that she had used it for her own benefit.

Singaram begged to be let out. He cried. He lamented. No one listened. He tried threatening them. He cursed—he struggled. The people around him used all of it as evidence for their conclusion that it was the ghost's mischief.

Singaram's anger increased. He sprang up, attacked those who blocked him, and tried to run outside.

The resistance became stronger. Everyone together tied his hands and feet. They laid him on a rope cot.

Though it was painful for Karpuram to watch her brother being tormented, she secretly rejoiced at the benefit she expected from it.

At last Singaram realized nothing more was possible and lay down quietly. For the first time in his life, he cursed the possession that came upon his sister.

<!-- source boundary: scan 63 → scan 64 -->

Once Singaram became quiet, people began leaving one by one. Some promised that the next day they would bring a good sorcerer and drive out Singaram's ghost.

Someone offered the opinion that Karagam-dancer Kannan was skilled at driving out ghosts: if he looked at it, the ghost would fly away.

The moment that news reached Singaram's ears, he shouted in fury, "Ha! What did you say?"

He shouted because he remembered the hereditary feud between Kannan's family and his own.

But the others decided, "That is the ghost's nature."

Little by little the crowd dispersed. Only Karpuram remained sitting beside her brother with her face hanging. Pooncholai and Ponmani looked terrified. To Ponnaiya, everything seemed strange; he kept muttering, "Mm... Easwara!... Easwara!"

The sound of a flute came from outside the house.

Ponmani eagerly expected that it must be Surulimalai.

Surulimalai, as unconcerned as ever, sat down on the veranda playing his flute with effortless ease.

Ponmani ran to him.

"Thambi! What happened?"

"What happened? The dead woman came back to life!" Surulimalai said.

"Ah! She came back to life?" Ponmani's mouth fell open in astonishment.
