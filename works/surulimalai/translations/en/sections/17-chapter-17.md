## 17

Kannan had been building castles in his mind: somehow blame must fall upon Singaram; the police must threaten him; he must suffer; the villagers must look upon Singaram with contempt and disgust;

<!-- source boundary: scan 127 → scan 128 -->

and Kannan must enjoy watching all of it. So there was nothing surprising in his deep disappointment when he heard that Singaram had escaped the accusation.

At a time when it had become an ingrained social habit even for people who met face to face and embraced one another warmly to invent stories and speak behind each other's backs, would Kannan—who had become Singaram's enemy from birth, as it were—refrain from slandering him?

The moment he heard the news, he went first to the panchayat elder's house.

Panchayat Paramasivam had grown accustomed to assuming that whenever Kannan appeared there must be some new quarrel with Singaram. So he opened with, "Come, Kanna! Nothing troublesome going on in the village, I hope?"

"Why wouldn't there be? The trouble already here looks ready to grow bigger—and on top of that a new trouble seems to be coming. In fact, you can almost say it's already arrived!"

"What is this, dropping bombs on me all of a sudden? Tell me properly."

"Singaram has become such a great man that he can bribe the police now!"

"What? What?"

"Yes! The police have decided that the letter folded into that corpse's sari-end has nothing to do with Singaram! Look at the way justice is going!"

"All right, leave it alone. Why talk about this? Then Singaram gets angry, and the quarrel keeps growing. Don't keep feeding it!"

"I don't want to go looking for useless trouble either—but will the heart keep quiet? If injustice

<!-- source boundary: scan 128 → scan 129 -->

and wrongdoing happen, I can't bear it. Look at this now—a sinful fellow has come as teacher to our village, a village overflowing with devotion..."

"Who? Has the teacher arrived? They said he hadn't come yet."

"He's been here two days! His name is Aravazhi, it seems. He talks nothing but irreligion—a godless fellow! His very arrival was an ill omen! Even God couldn't bear his coming to our village. He sent the temple bull to butt him. He got a proper welcome the moment he arrived! Then our Pooncholai—you know, the holy woman of the village—apparently he's been staying at her house for two days, getting poultices put on his body..."

"What you're saying sounds strange. A teacher who is supposed to show children the right path talks atheism?"

"Yes! Ninety out of a hundred of these young teachers who come nowadays are that kind of fellow... 'Rationalism,' they call it—rationalism! The schools are all going to ruin!"

"Hm... we'll watch little by little and see what our teacher is like."

"These things shouldn't be given time to grow. They have to be pinched off at the brain itself!"

"You've only just put the news in my ear. I'll begin watching."

Kannan felt a little satisfaction—the pleasure of scratching an itch. Before long, just as the itch of a sore gives way to burning after it is scratched, hatred flared like fire inside him.

<!-- source boundary: scan 129 → scan 130 -->

He felt strongly that the punishment he had already given Aravazhi was not enough. He planned to campaign throughout the village against Aravazhi, create a great conflict between him and the village's religious zealots, and make person after person chase and beat him.

As though drawing the first auspicious curl for his arrangements, he made Panchayat Paramasivam his first target. Even a monstrous liar unpacks his rotten goods believing everyone will accept whatever he says exactly as told. There is great satisfaction in that belief; the wheel of his life turns upon it.

Kannan too imagined that the villagers accepted exactly the opinions he spread about Singaram. Now he made Aravazhi enemy number two and began his work.

Stirring up a muddy pond is no great achievement—even a buffalo can do it and walk away. The difficult task is to set the pond right, preserve clear and clean water, and make it useful to everyone. Kannan was highly skilled at performing the first task without obstruction.

Having planted two big matters in the panchayat elder's ears, Kannan took leave and set out enthusiastically to attend to whatever else had to be done.

Just then the panchayat elder's son Chiranjeevi ran inside shouting, "Appa! A teacher has come to our village, appa! He's come to see you, appa!"

Looking at his son, Paramasivam saw black streaks all over his face. "What cursed thing is this? Your whole face is covered in soot!" he muttered.

<!-- source boundary: scan 130 → scan 131 -->

"That teacher smeared the soot on my face! He's a very good man, appa!" Chiranjeevi cried, hopping about.

The panchayat elder had been eager to see the new teacher, but when he heard that the man had smeared soot on his son's face, he hesitated.

"All right—looks like he's a mischievous fellow. Tell him to come! Tell him to come!" he ordered in a dry voice.

Chiranjeevi bounded outside.

At once Kannan began fanning the fire.

"See that? Here's direct evidence for you. Forget what *I* said—look at what he's done! You're the one big man in this village, like the iluppai flower in a place with no sugar mill! If he's bold enough to play tricks on your son and smear soot on his face, what else won't he do?"

Having poured that incense into the air, Kannan started out.

Aravazhi entered from the opposite direction. Seeing Kannan, he behaved as though he had not, and respectfully greeted the panchayat elder.

Paramasivam looked at Aravazhi with great indifference.

"Who are you, pa? Are you the son of the new teacher who's come here?" he asked.

Aravazhi smiled. "No, periyavare. I myself am the teacher. My name is Aravazhi."

"Oh-ho! You look like a schoolboy yourself. What do you know of the world? What are *you* going to teach and accomplish?" the panchayat elder asked rather insolently.

Those words caused no disturbance on Aravazhi's face. Water in a tank becomes disturbed only if a stone, stick, dust or chaff falls into it. A mere shadow falling upon water does not disturb it. Aravazhi treated the panchayat elder's speech as no more than a shadow

<!-- source boundary: scan 131 → scan 132 -->

falling on water. He remained calm without even having to think, "He is insulting me, but let me endure it."

"Just as I'd expect from a little boy—you've barely arrived and already smeared soot on my son's face!" Paramasivam continued.

"Smeared soot? Me? Not at all. I tried to wipe away the black streaks that were already on thambi's face. He wouldn't let me. Look—my own hands have gone black from trying to wipe them off!" Aravazhi said, spreading his hands.

"Not just the hands—the man's heart is black too. Look at this rationalist!" Karagam-dancer Kannan laughed mockingly.

"Haven't you left yet?" Paramasivam asked.

"How can I feel like leaving? A great scholar has arrived. I feel I could just stand here looking at him!" Kannan said. He glared once at Aravazhi, spat out "Tch!" and left for the second time.

A few seconds after he went outside, Chiranjeevi came running back, crying, "Appa! Appa!" and threw his arms around the panchayat elder. Paramasivam struggled in his grip. Aravazhi watched that strange household in amazement.

"What happened?" the father asked.

"Appa! A huge fight is going to happen in the street! Elephant army, cavalry—everything is coming! Ship battle—cannon battle! Then death—murder! Then peace—cremation ground—mud-clod—Arohara—Govinda! Govinda!"

Chiranjeevi shouted all this while dancing and singing.

<!-- source boundary: scan 132 → scan 133 -->

"He won't babble like this unless something has happened," Paramasivam said, coming outside.

The moment he stepped out, he saw with his own eyes that Chiranjeevi's words had come true. There were no ships, cannon or aircraft, but Kannan and Singaram were fighting a war of words harsher than any of those.

"You worthless scoundrel! Why are you going around spreading lies about me? You and I have nothing to do with each other in life or death—our connection is finished! Can't you sleep unless you talk about me?"

"Why are you threatening me? You're pouring out words like fire. What's the matter? Keep all that with your fire-pot!"

"Don't talk about my profession, you fool! Answer properly for talking about me—or say it wasn't true and apologize. I'm not letting you go this time!"

"Apologize? Not in this lifetime! What great man are you that I should apologize to you? You commit outrages in the village. Some poor orphaned woman—because of a vile sinner like you she hanged herself and died. If I condemn that, your anger boils over, does it? Let it! Let it! I'm not afraid. Do whatever you want!"

Kannan hitched up his clothes, clenched his fist and stood ready to fight. People from the street gathered around.

"Hey! Hey! Hey! Stop talking!" the panchayat elder shouted.

<!-- source boundary: scan 133 → scan 134 -->

"Yes! Stop talking—and start the fight! Let the fight go on! Let the world be destroyed!" Chiranjeevi shouted, clapping his hands.

The panchayat elder gave him a smack on the head, then went and caught Singaram and held him back.

While Singaram stood restrained out of respect for the elder, Kannan sprang forward in a single leap and landed a hard punch on Singaram's forehead. After that Singaram could no longer remain in the elder's grip. He threw down the staff in his hand and leapt at Kannan.

Punches rained rapidly between them. The people of the street and the panchayat elder intervened and held both men tightly so they could not break free. Aravazhi too stood watching in amazement the riot taking place directly in front of the panchayat elder's house.

"He is beating the drum all over the village that I bribed the police and escaped. Can he prove it?" Singaram shouted, wiping the blood running from his lip.

"Was bribing them the only thing you did? Would the police bend for just that? Even your beautiful younger sister..." Kannan had barely said it.

No one knew where Singaram found such speed.

"What did you say?" he roared, and sprang like a tiger.

That was enough. Kannan went down to the ground.

Singaram sat on his chest and punched him again and again, one side and then the other, until some of his fury was spent. Even the people in the street stood frozen for a few seconds. No one could tolerate the filthy way Kannan had dragged Singaram's sister into his speech. So they held back and gave Singaram a little time to vent his anger.

<!-- source boundary: scan 134 → scan 135 -->

"Aiyyo! Aiyyo!" Kannan screamed.

Then Karpuram entered, wailing "Oh!" She caught her brother's hand and shouted, "Stop! Stop!"

"Thangachi! Don't interfere—go!" Singaram snarled.

Suddenly overcome with emotion, he tried to say, "Aiyyo, Karpuram! About you... about you..." but could not get the words out. Sobbing, he began raining punches on Kannan's face again.

"Let him go! Don't do anything to him! Leave him! Leave him!" Karpuram cried.

Singaram would not listen. Karpuram wrapped her arms around her brother's neck and prevented his blows from falling on Kannan.

Singaram pulled Karpuram away and shoved her aside.

"Karpuram! Go home!" he ordered harshly.

"I won't! You mustn't hit him!"

"Hit him? I'll kill him!"

"Listen to me, anna. Don't do anything to him. That's all I'm asking!"

"You're pleading *for him*?... Go home properly!"

"Then kill me right here too!"

"Karpuram—what are you saying?"

"Why should I hide it? I'm going to marry him. I'm his wife... My anna isn't greater to me than my husband!"

Karpuram spoke in anger and wept.

Every nerve in Singaram seemed to collapse. He became like a corpse. His face went pale.

Only one sound came from him: "Ah!"

He stood

<!-- source boundary: scan 135 → scan 136 -->

like a statue.

Kannan got up and struck Singaram with all his strength. Singaram did not return even a single blow. He silently received every strike.

Karpuram came between them crying, "Anna! Anna!!"

Kannan dragged her aside too and continued attacking Singaram.

Singaram stood without moving.
