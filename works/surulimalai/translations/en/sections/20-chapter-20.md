## 20

Aravazhi could say nothing more. He was stunned that the meeting he had kept imagining, soaking his heart in honey, had ended at last in such bitter disappointment.

Even among the many love stories he had read, he had never encountered an event quite so shocking. Writers who create lovers at the tip of their pens generally let them enjoy each other's affection for at least a little while. Only after that do they bring in rupture, separation, longing, pain, torment, and all the rest.

Aravazhi became dejected at the thought that his own story had turned out completely differently.

Why should *she* have signed a demand that he be transferred out of the village?

It was an unsolved riddle.

Perhaps she had feared that the love sown at their first meeting would slowly become a plant, then a tree, and finally fall upon her own head—and had decided to pinch it off while it was still a sprout?

Who was there to answer Aravazhi? There was no one to give him advice or consolation. Only Surulimalai stood there, and he did not show that he had understood the smoke rising inside Aravazhi's heart.

Aravazhi handed the petition back to Surulimalai and left the place quickly.

"Why are you going without saying anything?" Surulimalai called.

"What is there to say, appa? If even your household is against me, what work do I have left in this village?" Aravazhi replied, and walked away.

Surulimalai watched Aravazhi leave without blinking. Then he nudged his mount into motion and went straight to Panchayat Paramasivam's house.

The panchayat elder sat on the veranda.

"What, pa? Did you get the signatures from your house?" he asked.

Surulimalai held out the petition.

Paramasivam looked at it.

"Besh! Besh! Almost everyone's signature is there now. You yourself must take this and put it in the post!" he ordered.

Karagam-dancer Kannan and Panchayat Paramasivam had immediately shown their hostility toward Aravazhi. In great haste they had gone among the villagers stirring up complaints and prepared the petition. They had gathered most of the required signatures from the very people who had come to watch the fight between Singaram and Kannan.

The remaining one or two signatures were left to Surulimalai. He too obtained those—including the signatures from his own household—and brought them back.

When the responsibility of posting the petition was also tied onto his head, he set out in great happiness.

He knew perfectly well that once the petition was sent, Aravazhi would be troubled.

When he had gone home to explain the matter and get signatures, his mother had refused. His sister too had disliked the idea. But after Surulimalai's explanation, they finally agreed.

Three-fourths of the village had already signed. Whether Pooncholai's household signed or not, what was going to happen would happen anyway. Why earn a bad name by refusing, when they could earn a good name by signing? That was his argument.

<!-- source boundary: scan 152 → scan 153 -->

Even after that, when his mother and sister remained worried, he reassured them: in any case, the petition would never be mailed. He himself would tear it up right there.

When Ponmani saw that Surulimalai was using a trick to make sure the petition never reached its destination, she could barely contain her delight.

"Thambi! You fooled the panchayat elder and the others and made sure the petition came into your hands!" she praised him.

It was on the way to the panchayat elder's house, after receiving that praise, that Surulimalai had met Aravazhi.

Everything had gone exactly as he had planned so far. He had even gathered the courage to destroy the petition at the end and lie that he had posted it.

But poor Surulimalai—before he and his mount had gone very far from the panchayat elder's house, Karagam-dancer Kannan arrived there in a hurry.

Kannan himself said the petition in Surulimalai's possession should be handed over to him and that he would take it to the post.

The panchayat elder agreed.

Surulimalai could say nothing.

He had planned to take it away and throw it in the rubbish. But the situation had changed. The petition fell into Karagam-dancer Kannan's hands.

That very day, it was mailed.

When Surulimalai told Ponmani what had happened, she felt as though she had fallen into fire. Through her brother she also learned that Aravazhi had seen her signature. She grieved deeply.

Somehow she had to see Aravazhi and ask forgiveness for the mistake she had made.

She tried for two or three days. She could not manage it.

Two

<!-- source boundary: scan 153 → scan 154 -->

days later, one morning, Ponmani stacked pots and vessels into her basket and set out for the market.

The school lay on her route.

She had gone to the market that way on countless days. But that day alone some magnetic force seemed to pull her to a stop.

It was morning. Workers were engaged in tidying the grounds around the school.

When would they leave the place? When could she go and meet Aravazhi without them noticing?

Thinking like this, she kept walking back and forth nearby. She was firmly determined not to leave without seeing him, and waited for the right moment.

She understood that deliberately going up to speak with a man did not fit the accepted conduct of a village woman. Even so, she told her struggling heart that she somehow had to change the mistaken idea Aravazhi held about her—and that the only way was to speak openly with him.

She could see Aravazhi moving around in a side room of the school.

But he did not notice her.

Ponmani stood looking toward the room. Aravazhi turned the other way and sat down.

As usual after waking in the morning, he sang aloud:

> Awakening feeling across the world,  
> the red-rayed sun has risen.

The moment he finished singing, he felt embarrassed at himself.

<!-- source boundary: scan 154 → scan 155 -->

The reason was that there was no red sun in the east when he sang. The sky was dark. There were signs of rain.

Before long, a drizzle began.

Aravazhi shut the window shutters, sat down again, took up the *Tirukkural* and began turning its pages.

On many mornings he had opened the *Tirukkural* and read. Usually, as he turned the pages, the divisions *Book of Virtue* and *Book of Wealth* would come before his eyes.

But that day, as though arranged in advance, it was the third book that presented itself to him.

> Budding in the morning, growing all through the day,  
> this sickness blooms in the evening!

He read the couplet four or five times, again and again, as though memorizing it.

His eyes moved to the next kural.

Meanwhile, the workers in the street, seeing the drizzle, hurriedly finished their work and withdrew. That suited Ponmani perfectly.

She lowered the pots and vessels from her head and set them down. Slowly, slowly, she walked toward the room.

The sight of Ponmani arriving jolted Aravazhi.

"Who? You?" he asked in surprise.

"Yes," she said shyly.

"Thadaal..."

"Pada-pada!"

A noise came from the street.

It was the sound of pots breaking.

Ponmani ran outside and looked.

A large coconut frond had fallen from the tree at the entrance and smashed the pots into fragments.
