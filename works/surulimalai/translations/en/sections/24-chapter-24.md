## 24

Only while life clings to the body does a human being have worries, desires, games and celebrations. Did the elders sing for nothing that even the breath taken in sleep may turn and be gone? Wife, children, friends, relatives—what among them follows a human being beyond death?

As long as the body remains, there is every comfort. Once it falls, not even the ash remains for long.

While alive, a man refuses to give another person even a place on the veranda of his house. Once dead, even if a thousand corpses are laid one above another upon the ash of his crushed bones, he raises no objection.

Ada-da! While life remains—what rivalry, how much quarrelling, how much arrogance!

Once he becomes a corpse,

<!-- source boundary: scan 174 → scan 175 -->

even an ant plucks at his eyes and eats them. Worms graze inside the cage of his heart.

If people who make plans about whom to destroy and how would sit quietly for a while and think deeply that death will come to them too, there would be no turmoil in the world. Everyone would live happily.

But a human being does not think of it.

He plans instead: live from somebody else's labour; enjoy himself from somebody else's sweat; float the boat of his life upon somebody else's blood.

Only now and then does he remember that this body, like a bag filled with air, will one day burst with a bang and become ash—and immediately he forgets it again.

"Aiyyo—whose hearts have we wounded with our words? Whose homes have we ruined? How many families have we reduced to broken walls while wearing the outward costume of a good man?"

Such calculations have become the mental accounts made correctly by a man lying on his deathbed. They rarely become the inner struggle of a man still alive.

At houses of death, elders wearied by life commonly exchange thoughts like these after offering condolences.

Those who gathered on hearing of Ponnaiya's death did not fail to speak such philosophies either.

In general, the investigation of life's philosophy becomes most intense at houses of death. Every future corpse who comes to offer condolences thinks or speaks about the impermanence of life and the ways one ought at least from then onward to conduct oneself.

But once people return from the cremation ground, everything is forgotten.

Even a man who wept over his wrongs while giving up his life would, if by chance he revived at the cremation ground and returned home, certainly forget everything he had thought while dying. Yet does that stop the customary talk at a house of death?

Not at all.

Pooncholai embraced Ponnaiya's body and wailed.

Women who lose their husbands cry that their forehead mark, turmeric, flowers and thali are gone. But if someone said, "Give all those up and I will return your husband," would they cling to the turmeric and thali? They would say, "Let the turmeric and kumkum go—only let my husband live again."

Pooncholai's greatest longing was that her husband's life might somehow return. She kept expecting it until his body was bathed and placed upon the bier.

Ponmani clung to her father's feet.

"Aiyyo, appa! Who is left now to support me? You've left all of us orphaned and are lying here without moving, appa!" she lamented.

Tears streamed from Surulimalai's eyes. He sat silently in a corner.

Theechatti Singaram attentively looked after everything that had to be done.

Karpuram took charge among the women and managed the tasks there.

Kannan too ran here and there, competing with Singaram in helping.

<!-- source boundary: scan 176 → scan 177 -->

Singaram would smear sacred ash on the corpse's forehead; immediately Kannan would place a sandal mark over it.

When the body was laid on the bier, Singaram took the head end; Kannan supported the feet.

Surulimalai was bathed and the fire-pot for the funeral flame was placed in his hand. Singaram took it from him and began walking with it.

Kannan shouldered one part of the bier.

Aravazhi observed all of this and walked slowly behind the funeral procession.

Leaving behind wife and children, cattle and home, Ponnaiya had begun a journey from which he would not return.

Once the house had been left behind, what more was there? Walking and hurrying, everyone reached the cremation ground.

"This is our fate too," they said, placing Ponnaiya's body upon the pyre and lighting it.

"A man is afraid even to touch the glass above a lamp because it may burn him. And here he is bathing in fire itself," people muttered as they bathed in the canal and came back to the bank.

Meanwhile Singaram and Kannan competed with each other in settling the wages, allowances and other accounts due to the cremation-ground workers.

On the way back to the village, talk arose strongly about the future of Ponnaiya's family.

"It would have been good if he had tied that girl to someone's head before this."

"He has died anyway. Couldn't the man at least have arranged that girl's marriage before dying?"

<!-- source boundary: scan 177 → scan 178 -->

"Is arranging a marriage such an ordinary matter? Doesn't it require money? Doesn't a good groom have to be found?"

Karagam-dancer Kannan joined the talk.

"There was no fault at all in Ponnaiya mama. All the fault was mine!"

Everyone was surprised to hear him.

"What fault was yours?" an elder asked.

"He tormented me, insisting that I must marry Ponmani. I was the one who kept postponing it to tomorrow and let time pass!"

Kannan walked along saying this with a completely casual face, as though it were the most ordinary fact in the world.

"The whole village is making a fuss that you're going to marry Karpuram. How are you going to marry our Ponmani?" another man asked suspiciously.

"Karpuram? Camphor? Who would marry a woman from that fellow's house? She comes and falls at my feet, that's all. Some people are angry, their moustaches twitching, merely because I refuse to accept her!"

As he said this, Kannan glanced pointedly at Singaram.

Singaram too stared at Kannan with sparks in his eyes, then turned away.

"Kannan and Ponmani are a good match. By God's grace, if the marriage happens soon, Ponnaiya's soul too will find peace!" an old man declared through his toothless mouth as he walked bent over.

<!-- source boundary: scan 178 → scan 179 -->

"Enough! Come quietly. What marriage talk is this at a house of death?" Theechatti Singaram shouted in a frightening voice.

For a little while no one in the group spoke. Everyone walked in silence.

Then Kannan began again, his voice full of arrogance and confidence.

"What harm is there in talking about marriage? Only those smacking their lips because *they* didn't get the girl will find it painful—poor fellows!" he laughed.

"Why do *I* need marriage? If I wished, a thousand women would come!" Singaram shouted.

"They'll come! They'll come—and hang themselves!"

"Tell him to speak with respect!"

"If the man says he doesn't want marriage, why should he interfere in *my* marriage?"

"I have a right to speak for Ponmani!"

"How did *he* get a right that I, her future husband, don't have?"

"Husband! Is she dying to put a garland around your neck?"

"What then—is she going to garland *you*?"

The two came face to face as though each would bite and swallow the other.

No one dared separate them.

Aravazhi caught Theechatti Singaram by the hand and drew him aside.

Before he could do more, Kannan shouted and slapped Aravazhi across the cheek—"palaarr! palaarr!"

That was enough.

Singaram sprang at Kannan.

The two grappled and rolled together in the street.

<!-- source boundary: scan 179 → scan 180 -->

Even Ponnaiya's death flew out of everyone's mind. Calming the two fighters became the villagers' greatest headache.

They fought for a long time without either yielding. At last both stood up, growling like tigers.

"I'll take your life—watch!" they shouted at the same moment.

News reached Panchayat Paramasivam, who came running and asked what had happened.

"Ponmani is my wife!" Kannan roared.

Aravazhi's heart grew heavy. He forced himself to remain calm. He understood that in that village Singaram was the one ally available to himself and to his beloved Ponmani.

"Ponmani is not your wife!" Singaram answered fiercely.

"No matter who opposes it—she belongs to me!" Kannan roared.

"Don't dream! She belongs to *me*!" Singaram shouted in fury.

Hearing Singaram's words, Aravazhi felt as though thunder had struck his head. He stood like a tree.

Surulimalai, still grieving the loss of his father, came and stood before them. His eyes were red.

"What right do any of you have to fight in the street while talking about my akka? There is one condition for marrying a woman from our house," he said calmly.<!-- verified source join: scan 180→181 -->

"What condition?" the panchayat elder asked.

"Whoever subdues the temple bull Siva—my akka will garland that man!"

"Phoo! Is that all? I'll subdue that bull! Ponmani will be mine!" Kannan laughed triumphantly.

"Surulimalai! I accept your condition. But one thing, Panchayat elder! Ponmani goes to the man who subdues the bull. If a man fails to subdue it and loses, he must mark himself with black and red spots, mount a donkey, and be paraded through the village!" Singaram said firmly.

"Oh, I'm ready!" Kannan replied.

Aravazhi felt as though his heart might stop.

Without letting anyone notice him, he hurried away toward the school.
