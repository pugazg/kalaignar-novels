## 23

After sending Ponmani to the market, Pooncholai-amma began watching her husband anxiously. For two days Ponnaiya's cough and breathlessness had grown worse. The previous night the coughing had become so severe that one could say a life that had almost gone had returned. A doctor had been summoned at once, and only after treatment did Pooncholai's distress ease a little.

In the morning Ponnaiya had been talking as cheerfully as usual. Both of them were reassured by the thought that Ponmani would use the money she earned at the market to buy the ingredients for the medicine the doctor had prescribed.

<!-- source boundary: scan 168 → scan 169 -->

Even when Ponmani took leave saying, "I'll go to the market and come back, appa!" he still looked strong.

A little while after she left home, the sky began to darken.

Ponnaiya's face began to darken too.

He sat on the rope cot talking to Pooncholai, and every now and then pressed a hand to his chest.

Pooncholai asked fearfully, "Why do you keep touching your chest?"

"Chest? What chest? Where is it? I gave it to you the moment we got married!" Ponnaiya replied with a laugh.

Her husband had often spoken mischievously like this when they were younger, but he had stopped talking that way as they aged. Pooncholai was both astonished and delighted that his youth seemed suddenly to have returned.

Memories of their younger days began running across the screen of her mind like scenes in a film.

The groom's family coming to see her. The groom, Ponnaiya, who had come with them, hiding behind the backyard wall and trying to look at her. Her seeing him do it, leaping in embarrassment and running inside. The two of them at the wedding pavilion stealing glances at each other's faces and smiling whenever no one was looking, imagining their future. Their awkward hesitation on the first night over who would speak first. Then, as time passed, the two of them talking without pause until they earned a reputation as great chatterboxes. Within a few years they became parents and the whole burden of family responsibility fell upon their heads.<!-- verified source join: scan 169→170 --> Through that responsibility, a natural restraint gradually settled into both of them.

One change after another rolled through Pooncholai's memory and became waterfalls in her eyes.

Without showing it, she stroked her husband's chest.

Ponnaiya watched his beloved wife without taking his eyes from her.

"Why are you looking at me like that? Why?" she asked, shaking him with a trembling hand.

"Nothing. I feel like I want to go on looking at you."

Ponnaiya's voice trembled. His eyes too had filled.

"Pooncholai! I have only one worry. For so long I've been carrying the desire to see our Ponmani married before my eyes. I don't think it's going to happen soon."

"Everything happens when its time comes. You stay peaceful."

"Time? What time? Is that time going to arrive before I close my eyes?"

The thunder, rain and wind seemed to worsen Ponnaiya's illness badly. As he spoke, he began coughing violently.

"Will Ponmani come now?"

"Where is this Surulimalai?"

Ponnaiya asked with great urgency.

The rain poured unbearably. Lightning—thunder—wind—and his cough joined them.

"Appadi! It feels as if my chest is hurting," he said, leaning against Pooncholai.

<!-- source boundary: scan 170 → scan 171 -->

She too was badly shaken.

"Aiyyo! What are you saying?"

Pooncholai's tongue faltered. Even so, she did not give up courage. She ran to the kitchen, prepared hot water, and began applying warm compresses to her husband's chest.

Ponnaiya could not bear the pain. He groaned more and more.

"Aiyyo, I can't bear it!" he cried.

There was no one else in the house.

How would Ponmani return in this rain? Where had Surulimalai gone?

Pooncholai's mind floundered.

She lifted the mangalyam and pressed it to her eyes.

"God! Protect my turmeric and kumkum!" she prayed.

For Ponnaiya, seeing Pooncholai's face caused even greater suffering than the pain in his chest.

A sudden doubt came to him: was he about to die?

What troubled him was not death itself. His worry was that after he died, his wife and children would become figures of grief. Who would comfort them then? Who would console them? Would they be left with no one?

Pooncholai stood before him as a married woman in full auspicious adornment, with flowers, turmeric and kumkum. In that village, no good or bad household event took place without her. At weddings Pooncholai had to come and perform arati for the bride and groom. Newborn children had first to be placed in Pooncholai's lap. When anyone set out on important business, they would long for a good omen to appear before them—would Pooncholai come into view, so that their aim might succeed?

The villagers fully valued the lucky face of such a "raasi-raasi" woman.

Why all this?

Because of the source-celebrated power of turmeric and kumkum.

<!-- source boundary: scan 171 → scan 172 -->

If that disappeared, Pooncholai would lose her standing. She would be pushed aside to the extent that people would say she must not cross the path of auspicious ceremonies. Her widowhood would be treated as a poisonous omen. Pooncholai would be shut away in a corner.

Ponnaiya told himself that, at the very least to spare her such humiliation, he must not die.

The sound of his heart weeping did not reach Pooncholai's ears. Her whole mind had gone to God.

She paid no attention even to the rain, which fell as though it had sworn to dissolve the earth beyond recognition.

She set out to call the doctor.

"Don't, Pooncholai. Nothing will help now," Ponnaiya said.

"Don't stop me as I'm going. I'll bring the doctor in one moment!"

"It's gone beyond the doctor. Only a little time remains. After that...?"

"Aiyyo, don't say things like that!"

Pooncholai gripped her husband's feet tightly and sobbed.

"If you too go to call the doctor... must I die alone?"

Pooncholai could not answer.

She cried and cried. She trembled. She suffered.

She tried every treatment she knew.

The pain kept increasing.

Pooncholai understood nothing.

Ponnaiya spoke slowly.

<!-- source boundary: scan 172 → scan 173 -->

"If you want, go and bring the doctor. Why should I spoil your wish?"

Pooncholai set out, gathering the courage to go through the storm and rain without fear.

Ponnaiya called her back and took from beneath his bed, beside the pillow, a sealed paper envelope he had hidden there.

"Read this and act according to it," he said in a voice that mixed firmness with tenderness.

Pooncholai tucked the envelope into the fold of her clothing and set out toward the doctor's house.

Ponnaiya cried out, "Ah!" and clutched his chest.

He fainted and fell.

Blood flowed from his mouth.

Pooncholai lifted him and seated him. He could barely speak now. He leaned against her lap.

Pooncholai decided there must be no more delay and ran out toward the doctor's house.

* * *

On one side, Pooncholai was running through the rain.

From the opposite direction, Ponmani and Aravazhi were coming toward the house.

Pooncholai did not see Aravazhi and Ponmani running and playing through the rain like artists at play as they approached. They did not see her either.

"Appada! We escaped!" Ponmani and Aravazhi said, blessing the rain as they walked a little faster.

Ponmani stepped inside the house in her rain-soaked clothes.

Aravazhi remained outside. Ponmani insistently called him in too.

No one seemed to be moving inside the house.

She looked all around.

"Amma! Amma!!"

<!-- source boundary: scan 173 → scan 174 -->

No answer.

She went toward the rope cot.

"Appa is sleeping. Should I wake him? No..."

At last she decided she would wake him after all.

"Appa! Appa!!"

No answer.

A suspicion.

She touched her father.

His body had gone cold.

His breathing had stopped a long time ago.

Her mother came rushing inside with the doctor.

In the speed of her entry she struck the threshold.

She fell flat to the floor.

A hard blow.

No consciousness.

Without seeing any of this, the head of that family had peacefully closed his eyes.
