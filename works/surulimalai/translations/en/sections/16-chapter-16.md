## 16

After leaving Pooncholai's house, Theechatti Singaram had no idea where to go. Another question kept gnawing at him: why would Maina, who had killed herself, have placed the blame on him? In any case, he decided the best thing was to go in person to the police officers, learn the details and explain the matter to them. He found the courage to think: if we conduct ourselves properly, then whatever is right or wrong beyond that may happen as it will.

<!-- source boundary: scan 120 → scan 121 -->

The police officers showed him the letter that had been knotted into the end of Maina's sari. There was no signature on it.

Singaram grew suspicious. Would she write a letter without signing it? He himself could not recognize her handwriting. The officers asked him, "This is her handwriting, isn't it?"

Singaram explained the situation a little. He wanted to tell the police at least some of the facts about Maina. He also longed for that explanation to free him from the false blame laid upon him.

The police had photographed Maina's body. The tears Singaram shed on seeing the photograph surprised even them. He became desperate to have Maina's handwriting identified and to establish that this was not her letter, so that the accusation and stain upon him might be removed.

He told the police that Maina had a husband and himself gave them the address: Perur, Peethambara Bhagavathar. At Singaram's repeated pleading, an officer set out with him for Perur to ask Peethambara Bhagavathar for the facts. Singaram went to great trouble because he had somehow to prove that he was without stain.

Peethambaram trembled violently when a police officer suddenly arrived at his house. The officer reassured him that there was no need to be afraid, then showed him Maina's photograph.

"Do you know this woman?" he asked.

"Who is that? She looks like my Maina!... Aiyyo, what kind of picture is this? Has Maina died?" Bhagavathar cried out.

Singaram stood there with wet eyes.

<!-- source boundary: scan 121 → scan 122 -->

"Who is Maina? What relation is she to you?" the officer asked.

"She is my..."

Bhagavathar meant to say "wife." But the word would not come out. Had he not promised Maina herself that from then on he would regard her only as a daughter?

The officer did not let the matter go. "Maina is your wife, isn't she?"

Bhagavathar did not deny it. He merely nodded in agreement.

"Maina committed suicide..." the officer began.

Before he could finish, Peethambaram spoke through tears, his tongue faltering.

"I already knew it. She had no other way. If a woman becomes the wife of a sinner like me, this is the only ending she can have..."

He did not stop there. He wanted to open his whole heart before the officer. He told him about the marriage between himself and Maina, the disappointment between them, the longing, their separation, and their final meeting. When he had told everything, the actor sobbed and wept.

It felt to Singaram as though hundreds of sledgehammer blows were falling on his heart. When Maina had told him this sorrowful story, had it not been his duty to correct her course and send her back to her husband's house? He tormented himself with the thought.

The next moment he consoled himself. What happiness would Maina have found in her husband's home? What path was there there for the dreams of her youth to be fulfilled? So he reassured himself that what he had done too had, in one sense, been right.

<!-- source boundary: scan 122 → scan 123 -->

The officer was genuinely moved to see a husband describe his wife's state of mind so openly, recognize his own mistakes, and grieve over them.

"Why? Is there any law under which action can be taken against me because my wife committed suicide? If there is, I'll accept whatever punishment is given. You've heard my whole story. You've understood what kind of relationship there was between me and my Maina. Anyone in that village will tell you that she left me and lived alone in another place. If, even after that, there is some way to place a charge against me, I'm ready to accept it freely and submit to whatever follows," Peethambaram said in a trembling voice.

"There is no need for you to be afraid like that. Do you have any letter written by Maina? We need one to compare the handwriting and identify it. If you have one, can you give it to us?" the officer asked.

Peethambaram thought for a while. Then, as though remembering something, he rose and went inside. He did not come back for a long time. Only the sounds of boxes and vessels being moved around could be heard from within.

With a "meow," Maina's kitten came toward the street side. The moment Singaram saw it, he swept it up and held it tightly against his chest. He felt as though he were embracing Maina herself.

The officer did not notice the streams of tears flowing from Singaram's eyes. Singaram closed his eyes.

The fire-pot dance—Maina beside the veranda of the house—that enchanting gaze—the night meeting—the flood of pleasure—the affectionate parting—one after another,<!-- verified source join: scan 123→124 --> they appeared and changed like scenes in a film upon the screen of his mind.

"Here it is—Maina's letter. The first letter she ever wrote me, and the last. This is the very note she sent with her beautiful photograph and her request that I marry her. This is the letter my Maina wrote me with love!"

Peethambaram came out saying this and, in still greater pain, handed the letter to the officer.

Singaram too looked at it eagerly. The officer compared it with the letter taken from the dead woman's sari.

The two were very different.

A long breath escaped Singaram. "Appada! We've escaped a great false blame!" he said to himself.

Even so, he still did not understand the true reason for Maina's suicide. From Peethambaram's explanation, a suspicion surrounded him: perhaps she had chosen suicide after the Bhagavathar's final meeting with her. Singaram became anxious to send the officer back to the village and somehow learn that truth directly from Bhagavathar.

Although the officer's suspicion of Singaram had disappeared, another strong doubt arose: who had tied that letter into Maina's sari in order to turn suspicion against Singaram? He too wanted to discover the answer.

Singaram himself suspected a few people, but did not dare name anyone specifically.

<!-- source boundary: scan 124 → scan 125 -->

Peethambaram asked for Maina's letter back, wanting to keep it forever as a remembrance. At that point the officer had to explain the matter concerning Theechatti Singaram.

"This letter disproves the accusation placed on Singaram. So the police must keep it for a few days. After that, we'll return it to you," he explained.

Peethambaram wanted to know how Theechatti Singaram knew details about him, and how the blame had been laid on Singaram. But he did not want to ask those things in front of the officer.

Singaram and Peethambaram both wanted to meet and speak privately. Each needed a different piece of information. Without the other knowing it, both decided that the police officer alone should first be sent away.

But the officer explained why Singaram needed to return to the village with him and why steps had to be taken to establish officially that he had no connection with the suicide. He therefore took Singaram along, said farewell to Peethambaram, and left.

Before going, Singaram eagerly lifted the kitten once more, kissed it, then placed it in Peethambaram's hands.

When Karpuram heard that the blame laid on her brother had been removed, she plunged into a sea of happiness. The villagers too expressed their joy when they saw Singaram.

"I was frightened, anna! I thought Ponmani might believe that false accusation against you and form a bad opinion of you!" Karpuram began softly.

<!-- source boundary: scan 125 → scan 126 -->

"Ponmani? She would never form a bad opinion of me!" Singaram said with a laugh.

"Oh! Has it come that far already? I didn't expect this much so soon..."

"Why didn't you expect it? Wasn't she the one who released me and sent me out?"

"Oh-ho-ho... is that what happened? Anna! Even that doesn't surprise me. What's surprising is that the ghost which had caught you ran away the moment Ponmani came near you!"

"I know everything, Karpuram! I know perfectly well that all that was a drama you staged to tie a knot between Ponmani and me!"

"Ah!"

"Don't be afraid! You did it out of love for your anna; you didn't do it with some other bad intention. Even so, women like you shouldn't get ideas like this!"

"Scold me however you like—I don't care. If that beautiful green parrot Ponmani becomes my anna's, that's enough!"

"Do you know Ponmani and I have already made an agreement?"

"Ada-de! Even agreements have become secret now? Karpuram, you clever girl—clever indeed! Anna, what agreement is this?"

"From now on, I am always her anna—and Ponmani is my beloved younger sister. That's the agreement!"

"Don't joke, anna!"

<!-- source boundary: scan 126 → scan 127 -->

"I'm not joking, Karpuram! There will be no marriage for this Singaram ever again! Only if Maina somehow comes back to life will there be family life for me. Otherwise this is how it will remain forever. Every woman born into this world is a mother, younger sister or elder sister to me! If there was one woman born to be my wife—she is dead! Dead!"

Having said this, Singaram burst into loud sobs.

Karpuram stood unable to answer.

Just then Akilandam from the next street entered the house and called out, "Karpuram! Did you hear the news? Our Karagam-dancer Kannan is going all over the village saying your anna bribed the police and escaped the charge!"

The instant those words reached him, Singaram growled, "Ah! Won't he keep his mouth shut? I'll settle this with him! Either I must disappear from this village—or he must!"

He sprang forward.

"Anna! Anna! Stop!" Karpuram cried in panic, trying to hold him back.

He would not listen. He ran to a corner of the house, picked up a heavy staff, and came toward the street.

Karpuram fell at his feet and tried to stop him. Singaram shook his sister off, let out a tiger-like roar, and ran into the street.
