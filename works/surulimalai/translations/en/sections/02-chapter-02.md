## 2

She must have been someone he knew. If the sight of her corpse made him faint, there must have been some relationship between them. And Singaram's cry itself seemed proof that it was a joyous relationship in which their hearts had joined. One could say that at Samayapuram Theechatti Singaram, who carried a fire-pot in his hand and whirled around with a *kara-kara* like a spinning top, had encountered an entirely new experience. The experience was not merely that the pot had slipped while he danced, fallen, and shattered.

In that village he had met a young maiden who so bewitched him that the pot slipped from his hand... The pambai and thavil thundered in competition with one another. As though to strengthen still further the emotion raised by the instruments, sambirani smoke spread its fragrance throughout the street.

Little priests, bunches of neem leaves in their hands, danced and ran in every direction blowing vibhuti from their mouths, all busily engaged in spreading a terrifying kind of devotion. Cries of "Mother! Mother! Mother of the world! Kali! Mari! Devi!" intoxicated the ordinary people with devotion and set their heads spinning.

<!-- source boundary: scan 22 → scan 23 -->

It was in that atmosphere that Theechatti Singaram displayed the art he had learned and won everyone's praise. Slowly the fire-pot procession passed through many streets and reached the end of the last one. The temple administrators asked him to dance there for a little while.

"I'm very tired. Need a little water," Singaram said, giving a wink as he spoke. Understanding what kind of "water" was being requested with a wink, the administrators brought "it" to him in a cup. Singaram drank it and got ready to dance. By then the people of that street had begun coming in rows to pour oil into the fire-pot. Each would pour the oil brought in a small vessel into the pot, and Singaram would immediately place vibhuti and a kumkum mark on their foreheads, bless them, and send them on their way.

Among those who came in that line was "she," the woman who had captured his heart. When she poured in her oil, Singaram tried to place a kumkum mark on her forehead. For some reason his fingers trembled. He steadied himself and applied the kumkum. But everyone nearby burst out laughing. He looked in surprise. The kumkum dot was not on her forehead at all; it was on the tip of her nose. He was embarrassed. She was embarrassed. The spectators did not fully understand the matter. The temple administrators merely laughed among themselves and said, "The unsteadiness given by the water."

<!-- source boundary: scan 23 → scan 24 -->

Wiping the kumkum from her nose, she went and stood at the doorway of her house and began watching the dance. Singaram too began dancing with fresh enthusiasm. He danced so marvellously that the hands of the musicians, bound to answer the beat of the dancer's feet, were ready to give out from exhaustion.

The smoke spat out by the red tongues of flame blazing in the fire-pot rose toward the sky, and beneath that cloud of smoke he whirled and whirled as he danced. Something in that sight must have created a new feeling within her; otherwise there could have been no reason for the new light that appeared in her eyes. She imagined that the real fire-pot was not the one in Singaram's hand, but the one in her own heart, raising flames and pouring out heat. Singaram tried dance after new dance. He produced astonishing sequences of steps. He let the fire-pot burn with terrible force. In the place where he danced, a great hollow nearly half a foot deep had been worn into the ground.

"Is *he* dancing? Amman is making him dance!" said the devotees.

"It is *that* which is dancing!" said the administrators who had given him the "water."

None of those was the cause. The slender young woman standing before him was the cause—but Singaram said nothing of that. He went on dancing with effortless ease.

A figure as though shaped on a lathe—the fullness of youth over her whole body—lips like a pair of kovai fruits—skin the colour of a lemon. Near her on the veranda was a kitten. She kept<!-- verified source join: scan 24→25 --> lifting it, stroking its soft body, setting it down again, and all the while enjoying Singaram's dance. She was afraid of what the people nearby might think if she stared at him without blinking. Those who look without stain in their hearts do not worry about those standing around them.

But is it not natural for people who cannot hide some feeling rising and overflowing within them to fear the people beside them? That the lotus blooms because it falls in love with the sun is a poet's stock-in-trade! That the water-lily blooms in order to embrace the moon is likewise only a dream of imagination. The word "love" has neither meaning nor work to do there!

And what of her? Did she even know the word "love"? No. Yet when she saw him, some new blossoming had arisen in her heart.

Love is not the basis of the blooming of lilies and lotuses. That is a law of nature. But in her blossoming the feeling called love lay mute within her, struggling to draw her toward him.

A hungry child says "sochi." "Sochi" means rice. Rice is cooked from grain. Grain comes from paddy. How many people must pour out their sweat to grow that paddy—the child knows none of this. Even we, who do know, do not think about how the rice came to us while we are eating it.

In just that way she gave herself over to him. She did not know that its name was "love." She was still at the stage of the child asking for "sochi." She pressed the kitten tightly to her face. Poor thing—it gave a "meow," freed itself from her hands and jumped down. In the speed of its leap, her blue upper cloth caught on its leg and slipped down. Leaving the cat, her hands hurried to straighten her garment.

<!-- verified source join: scan 25→26 -->

Before she could finish, there was a *thadaar!* Singaram's fire-pot fell from his hand and smashed to pieces. She let out a little *kaluk* laugh. She understood how the fire-pot had fallen. Thinking to herself that all of it was the kitten's mischief, she adjusted her clothes again and stood there. The kitten remained seated on the veranda.

Afraid to reveal his own mistake, Singaram set off an alarm: "Some danger is going to come to the village! That's why this bad omen has happened!" The devotees moved away with fallen faces and fear-filled eyes. "Mother! Ambikai! Protect us!" they cried as they began circling the temple.

Singaram could not remain still in the place where he was staying. He wandered here and there.

"Who is she?" From whom could he obtain an answer to that question? If he went and asked someone, what would they think of him? Even if he did ask, what identifying detail could he give in order to inquire about her?

Suddenly an idea came to him. He decided he would ask, "Who was that woman on whose nose I accidentally put the kumkum?" He gently began a conversation with one of the temple administrators. The man explained.

"I've been asking about her myself.

"She came to this village a month ago. It doesn't seem that anyone is with her. She has been living alone in that house. I sent a few people to ask for particulars. She flared up at them<!-- verified source join: scan 26→27 -->, said whatever came to her mouth, and sent them away. They say she hardly speaks to anyone. I have heard that she does not even talk freely with the women in the neighbouring or opposite houses. Beyond that, I know nothing about her. Only her name, they say, is Maina!"

The administrator's explanation surprised Singaram and only stirred his curiosity further. In Sirur, the title "sour face" belonged to him alone. He was secretly pleased that she too seemed to be like him.

He decided that somehow he would meet her face to face. Maina! The woman who had raised a storm in his mind. He set out to see her. Dense darkness—the village lay in the stillness of a cremation ground. Without anyone knowing, Singaram left and walked toward her house. Fear that someone might see him did not stop him. The reason: he had drunk enough liquor to give a man courage even to commit murder.

How many protections a drunkard has! Drinking gives a man new courage—and is that all? Even if he commits some wrong, society will sometimes forgive him with, "Aiyo, poor fellow; he did it in drunken frenzy!"

Armed with those protections, Singaram stepped onto Maina's threshold. The door was shut. He pushed it gently. It did not open. He pushed a little harder. A sound came from inside. The door opened.

There was no other sound; only the loosened bolt had fallen to the floor. Even in his drunkenness Singaram acted cautiously. He did not enter immediately. He listened to see whether the sound had drawn any response.

<!-- verified source join: scan 27→28 -->

Only the kitten gave a single "meow" and then fell silent.

"Ssh! You've already eaten... sleep! Sleep!" he heard her say through the haze of sleep. Then he slowly opened the door wider and entered. Maina was asleep on a mat. The cat slept beside her.

They say cats stay awake at night hunting rats; this cat lay there sleeping comfortably, and Singaram wondered at it. If you feed it until its belly swells, why would there be wakefulness and wandering afterward? All that trouble belongs only to half-hunger and quarter-hunger!

Singaram thought of calling, "Maina!" His tongue would not rise. He simply stared at her face. Then he looked at her neck.

She wore necklaces strung with beads. Sleeping alone in that house gave her a beauty of its own. With his eyes he drank in the full radiance of youth that had caused the fire-pot in his hand to slip and shatter. A tremor began to pass through his body. His drunkenness began to subside. Something at the back of her neck glittered *pala-pala*. He bent down and looked closely. It was a thali, threaded into the necklace she wore and fallen backward onto the pillow behind her neck.

The moment Singaram saw the thali at her neck, every trace of intoxication flew away somewhere. He turned to leave the house. His foot stumbled and a vessel rolled away with a great noise. The cat sprang up! Its sharp claws raked Singaram's neck. Maina woke with a start, ran and shut the door, and shouted:

"Who's that? Stop!"
