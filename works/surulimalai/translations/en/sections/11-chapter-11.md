## 11

As the possession that usually came upon her seized her again, Karpuram began her deity-dance with an agitation greater than ever before. "Hey, sinner! Traitor! Watch what I do to you! I curse you for betraying the woman who trusted you. May you be ruined! Hey! Without understanding the heart of this poor life, you spoke sinful words! Aay! Ooy!" she screamed, hair loose, dancing wildly. Kannan was a little frightened by the sight.

At once he recovered himself, slapped his cheeks in reverence and said, "Mother! Forgive me... Devi! Forgive me! Sacrilege... sacrilege!"

At last the deity that had come upon Karpuram departed. Kannan soothed her. "What a great fool you are! I only played a little trick to tease you, and you jumped as though every word were true! Would any lover go to his beloved and ask her to carry a message to another woman? I threw that line out just to learn what was in your heart. Before I knew it, that hasty deity of yours came running!" he joked.

Karpuram's suspicion did not disappear. Her eyes became question marks and she flung them at him. "Kanne! Don't be angry, Karpuram! Believe me, I swear! I was only playing deliberately. You got frightened! Don't be afraid, thangam!" Kannan piled up soothing words and drew Karpuram back against his shoulder. She believed him.

"Will your anna Singaram accept our relationship? Uh-uh. He never will. He's a terribly rough man. I don't know how we are ever going to become one against his will!" Kannan lamented.

Seeing this, Karpuram assured him, "Don't worry. Turning my anna's mind around is my job."

But Kannan had already thrown the doors of his mind wide open and was waiting for Ponmani to enter. None of the desire-filled words flowing from Karpuram's heart had any sweetness for him.

Isn't it the nature of some people to keep stretching after what they cannot obtain, while treating what comes without difficulty as unimportant? Even the trick of calling an unreachable fruit sour arises only after the last attempt has failed! So Kannan convinced himself that he had not yet exhausted his efforts. Somehow he would bend Ponmani to his desire and tie a marriage cord around her neck. He remained confident that the castle he had built would not collapse.

Karpuram was not the summit of his ideal! She was only the shade of a date palm found during a journey across the desert. Nothing more. That was the measure of the affection he showed her.

Anyone placing Singaram and Kannan side by side would describe Singaram as the more dangerous man and Kannan as the virtuous one. Singaram's appearance and his frightening bulging eyes painted him as a brute. Karagam-dancer Kannan was an example of danger hidden inside beauty. The tainted affection he had shown Karpuram was already echoing that truth. Singaram and Kannan were simply two kinds of character commonly found in human society.

<!-- source boundary: scan 86 → scan 87 -->

In plays, stories and films, villains are often made frightening merely to look at, while the good characters—the heroes—appear handsome. But in Sirur, Singaram and Kannan moved about in a form exactly opposite to such imaginings.

Between those two kinds of men stood Karpuram with a character of her own. She had a heart capable of overflowing with affection. At the same time that heart could change and pour out poison. She did not think wrongdoing for the sake of love was a crime. She was delighted that the result of her wrongdoing had brought Kannan to her. How, then, could she see the shape of the mountain-snake coiling inside his mind?

Promising to meet her again, Kannan left Karpuram and went away. Her mind would not settle. She stood dazed enough to wonder whether what she had seen and experienced had been only a dream.

Unable to bear the pain that had broken through even the turmeric paste, Singaram lay on the cot groaning and slowly turning from side to side. Pooncholai-amma had lain down muttering that the child who had gone somewhere still had not come home; before long she was snoring soundly. Ponnaiya's cough did not trouble him either, and he too slept peacefully.

Ponmani lay in the room adjoining the hall, turning something over and over in her mind. However much she tried, sleep would not come. Singaram's groaning condition caused her great confusion. She repeatedly recalled Karpuram's suggestive<!-- verified source join: scan 87→88 --> words and sighed. At times she suspected that Singaram had been kept in her house by Karpuram's own arrangement. Immediately superstition intervened. Believing in ghosts and spirits, she would withdraw the accusation she had imagined against Karpuram. Yet in the end superstition did not win. Perhaps he *was* possessed by a ghost—fine. But why, for that reason, must Singaram stay in *her* house? Let him stay if he must; helping someone would not bring sin. But were not two different dangers now advancing and laying siege?

Using this excuse, Karpuram was looking for a bride for her brother. Judging by the way she was going, she might even start a story in the village. People would begin asking whether there was something between Singaram and Ponmani. Karpuram would use that and arrange her brother's marriage according to her own wish. And did it end there? That Karagam-dancer Kannan too was using the same excuse to enter the house as though he were an exorcist and cast his eyes everywhere. His coming often was a great danger. Who knew how he might behave, or when? Thoughts like these weighed heavily on Ponmani's heart. There was only one remedy for all of it: somehow Singaram had to leave the house. For a little while the fear returned that this had been a deity's command, spoken during possession. But in her anger toward Karpuram, even that fear did not last.

Breaking into her thoughts, Singaram called out, "Water! Water!"

Ponmani did not know what to do. "Amma! Amma!" she called. The snoring stopped for a moment

and then began again—louder than before. She got up and went toward her mother. She worried that if she called too loudly, her sick father would wake and suffer restlessly until dawn. In a low voice she said, "Amma!"

By then Singaram softly called, "Ponmani!" She started. Was he calling only to ask for water—or with some improper thought? Her heart pounded. She remained silent.

Again he called, "Ponmani!" She did not answer.

"Please come near, thangachi!" he said. There was affection in that call. There was pity. Above all there was pleading. Without her realizing it, something stirred in Ponmani's mind. She did not fail to value the bond and genuine affection contained in the word *thangachi*—younger sister.

"What do you need, anna?" she asked, going near.

"Thangachi! At least *you* think about my condition, amma! No ghost has got me, no spirit, nothing! This is all nonsense. Everything has come from the possession that seizes my sister Karpuram. Don't misunderstand me. This is the chance—untie all these bonds and open the door too. I'll run away. First give me a little water!"

The tenderness in Singaram's plea melted her heart. She brought water and gave it to him. After drinking it, Singaram muttered to himself, "If only I could get a dram of arrack, this body pain would go away."<!-- verified source join: scan 89→90 -->

Ponmani said nothing. Then, looking at her, Singaram pleaded again and again, "Please let me go, thangachi. I have no ghost in me, nothing!"

"You truly think of me only as a younger sister, don't you?" Ponmani asked, trembling.

"Yes. Why do you doubt it?" he replied.

"No... Your sister Karpuram—what she told me about you was different!"

"Different how? Tell me, amma—tell me!"

"That you wanted to marry..."

"Oh-ho... Did my sister tell you I wanted to marry *you*?... Ponmani! There is nothing like that. I have always thought of you only as my younger sister!"

"Appada! My mind is at peace now..." Ponmani said, and untied his bonds.

With difficulty he sat up. "Thank you very much, thangachi! Open the street door too," he asked.

Holding his hand and slowly helping him along, she said, "Anna! You mustn't tell anyone that *I* was the one who let you out!" He nodded in agreement.<!-- verified source join: scan 90→91 --> The street door was opened. Singaram looked at Ponmani with gratitude and left the house. Ponmani bolted the door again and returned to lie down in her place.

Before she had even settled, a terrifying noise came from the street.

A great cry—"Aiyyo!"

Ponmani sprang up, shouting "Amma! Appa!" without realizing it.

Pooncholai and Ponnaiya both woke.

Ponmani ran and opened the street door.

Opposite their house stood a savage bull—and directly before its horns, a man! The man was lying in the street. She could not identify him clearly. He lay curled like a ball and now made no sound. The bull alone kept goring and shoving him, rolling him along the street!
