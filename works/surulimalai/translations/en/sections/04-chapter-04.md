## 4

"The news of that untimely death in the house on the very day after the wedding left all the relatives wailing in grief. They muttered among themselves that something inauspicious had happened to the bridegroom's family. Except for the few relatives of mine who had come for the wedding, everyone else looked at me with nothing but dislike. I fell upon Appa's body and cried aloud. You now know that there was a reason my sorrow became twice as great! I felt one consolation: fortunately my father had gone without having to learn that I was lost in a trackless forest, bewildered and without direction. By evening all the rites for his cremation were over.

"Darkness was spreading quickly. Darkness had already closed over my life before that. I wept and wept over the suffering I had sought out for myself. Everyone, my husband included, thought I was crying only for Appa, and began consoling me.

<!-- verified source join: scan 37→38 -->

"But after ten days of mourning in the house, the terrible hour would certainly come when I would have to meet my husband alone in a room again—Aiyo! My head began to spin at the thought itself. At first I thought: that crisis will come only after ten days; until then let me remain in his house and somehow manage.

"But another fear surrounded me. What if, during those ten days, he showed me great affection and tenderness in the way he dealt with me, and—even if I never developed toward him the attachment a woman feels for a husband—I began to feel toward him the affection one might have for an elderly man, a father, or a grandfather? What would I do then? I was terribly afraid.

"Just as I expected, he tried to console me and tell me not to grieve over my father's death. Perhaps he had memorized it for some play—I don't know—but he even recited, 'Though one rolls and weeps year after year, will the dead return?' and tried to comfort me.

"I had made my decision. I would not live with him for even one more moment. You must understand that this decision came only after a great struggle within my mind.

"'He is your husband—can you betray him?' my mind preached. Then that same mind answered, 'So what if you do? Did he not betray you by enchanting you with an outward disguise?'

"Another question would arise in my heart: 'You utter fool! Did *he* come to you and ask you to marry him? Weren't you the one who went searching for him? Is it proper to blame him now?' And immediately an answer would arise to that too.

<!-- source boundary: scan 38 → scan 39 -->

"'It is true that I went looking for him. But if that old man had shown me his own true form—who he really was—would such deception and disappointment ever have arisen? Therefore he is the guilty one!'

"Yes. That was the case I argued myself, and the judgment I gave myself! According to the judgment, I had to leave him immediately.

"That very night I made ready for my journey. Everyone was asleep. He lay in his room. How could sleep come to me? I rose quietly. In the hall where my father's body had lain that morning, a lamp was flickering. I bowed to it once. Then I opened the back door and left.

"Until then this cat had been sitting near the door, waiting for a chance to get out. It came out behind me. Why should I go alone? I picked up the cat too—a piece of property from my husband's house.

"The next question was: where was I to go? At first I thought I would simply return to Singanallur. But I feared I could never shut the village's mouth, and I was terrified that my husband would find me. So I changed my mind. I wandered from place to place. At last I came to this village. I found this house at a low rent. I have been feeding myself with the little money I had in hand and the money I got by selling the jewellery I wore. Is that enough? Must I tell you still more of my story?" Maina finished, weeping.

"From here on, *I'll* tell your story!" Singaram began, clearing his throat.

<!-- verified source join: scan 39→40 -->

She too waited eagerly to hear what he would say.

"A woman named Maina was deceived by the outward disguise of a stage actor, married him, then learned his true form and ran away from him. One day that woman happened to meet a young man called Theechatti Singaram. Their eyes spoke the language of love. On the very night they met, Singaram boldly came to her house. She told him her whole life story. Hearing it, Singaram's heart melted and softened. 'Don't worry, woman! I'll keep you and protect you!' he promised, giving his word. She agreed. After that, the two of them began living happily together."

Singaram told the story this way and looked at her face. Redness rose in Maina's cheeks. Her head lowered of its own accord. Taking that as a sign of consent, Singaram rested his face upon her shoulder.

Stroking his face with her hand, her eyes filling with tears, she asked, "Will you accept me as your wife and protect me always?"

In a trembling voice Singaram promised, "I swear on God, I will never forget you!" and gathered her into his arms.

She freed herself from his embrace, fixed the bolt on the street door and shut it, then came back to him. The cat slowly went to the kitchen and lay down in its warmth.

<!-- source boundary: scan 40 → scan 41 -->

Maina lay there dazed with the fullness of having given herself to Singaram. She dreamed that a rebirth had appeared in her life. Only the faint smell of liquor from Singaram's mouth did not please her very much. She told him that too.

"Don't be afraid that I'm a drunkard! Don't suspect that when morning comes I'll forget this talk! I love you more than arrack. No drunkard will say something like that. So trust me! But as for this drinking habit—now that I've got used to it, I can't be changed anymore!" Singaram spoke openly and managed to make her accept even that.

"Maina, the first time you saw a man in costume, thought he was King Arjuna, and got deceived. Now again, the moment you saw me in the fire-pot dance, you fell for me. What would you do if you were deceived this time too?"

To that mischievous question from Singaram, Maina answered with her lips. But no words fell from that answer. Only a sound arose.

Singaram, who had been roasting in the flame of the fire-pot itself, had found a cool mango grove to delight him. Maina too had found support. The darkness that had clung to her heart lifted. Her heart leapt with joy.

The next morning Singaram had to leave for Sirur. The plan was that he would go, return in two days, take Maina with him, and begin a household with her in Sirur. They parted unwillingly, and at dawn Singaram left her and went to the house the temple administrator had arranged for him.

<!-- source boundary: scan 41 → scan 42 -->

With thousands upon thousands of happy thoughts surging through her mind, Maina began attending to her chores. She touched the mat on which they had lain and bowed to it, kissed the pillow, and rolled both away. She lifted the kitten from the kitchen, stroked and stroked its soft fur, pressed it to her breast and face, and bounced about in delight.

She sprinkled water at the house entrance and swept it. With a plan to draw a larger and more beautiful kolam than ever before, she laid siege to the street entrance with the bowl of kolam flour in her hand. In every dot she placed at the threshold she saw Singaram's majestic face. In every line bending and curving for the kolam, she thought of the bends in her own life. Wherever the curved lines joined, she rejoiced in the thought that she and Singaram too had joined.

She finished a large, richly beautiful kolam. Singaram would pass this way when he left for his village, would he not? He would see the kolam as he went. Let him see this too, she thought. She drew a little design like a fire-pot, and made a flower appear above the flame. Even to her it looked new and marvellous. Delighted that she had completed the kolam to her satisfaction, she straightened up with the bowl of flour in hand.

She started.

Her eyes spun—she could not believe herself. Opposite her stood Peethambara Bhagavathar, staring at her without taking his eyes away.

Maina said nothing. Her tongue would not rise to speak. Without her knowing it, the bowl slipped from her hand. Flour scattered all over the entrance, ruining the kolam. She went inside. Peethambaram followed her in.

She entered and stood motionless. Her eyes were clouded with tears.

<!-- verified source join: scan 42→43 -->

Peethambaram did not fail to notice. Without waiting for her to say anything, he sat down inside the house. Seeing him, the cat cried out and ran toward him.

"Oh, you're here too?... What, you don't like me either? How many days did you eat the rice from my house—and the moment you found a new friend, you came away too?" Peethambaram said, pulling the cat toward him and stroking it.

"Sit down, Maina—why are you standing?" he asked affectionately. She did not sit.

"I've walked a very long way since early morning. I can't bear the hunger... Is there anything to eat? Will you serve me?" he asked in the most ordinary tone.

Maina could not understand Peethambaram's manner at all.

"All right, why trouble you? If there's something there, I'll serve myself and eat!" he said. He went to the kitchen, took leftover rice from a pot, put it on a plate, and sat opposite Maina to eat. The cat began eating from the same plate with him.

Maina could no longer remain standing. Her entire body trembled. Still, by force she summoned firmness of mind and tried to steady herself.

"Is there salt?" Peethambaram asked.

Maina herself brought the salt bowl and set it near him.

"Put a little in with your own hand—just the right amount!" Peethambaram requested. She began putting salt into the leftover rice, then her hand slipped and she overturned the entire bowl.

Seeing it, Peethambaram laughed, "Kala-kala!"
