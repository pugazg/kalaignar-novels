## 14

"Kannan walked beyond the lower street toward a ruined mandapam in the distance. An arasu tree stood there, its thick roots splitting the mandapam into pieces as though it had torn it apart with powerful hands and now stood laughing in victory. Beside it was a sacrificial pedestal, half ruined and half standing. At night the sight of the two would frighten people passing that way.

"They say a terrible muni called Payasa Muni lives at that pedestal. They say he has a mistress named Katteri Amman. That Katteri, they say, is crazed for blood. If she gets the smell of human blood, she will not let it go.

"One day, when she was hungry for blood and could find no one, they say she finally struck down Muthannan, the priest who worshipped her, and drank his blood. The next morning the villagers found the priest lying dead in front of the pedestal and put the blame on Katteri.

<!-- source boundary: scan 106 → scan 107 -->

"A few days later, some people also told another version. It seems the priest had quarrelled with a wrestler from the neighbouring village. The wrestler swore he would kill the priest, and the priest swore back at him. One night the wrestler decided to use his brains instead of a knife.

"When the priest finished worship at the pedestal and started home, a frightening figure in a black sari, with anklets on its feet, leapt out from the mandapam shouting, 'Ko!' The priest screamed. That was all. Blood flowed from his nose and mouth. His breath ended.

"If the wrestler had beaten or stabbed him, it would have become a murder case. So, for his own safety, he staged the killing in Katteri's name—that was the neighbouring wrestler's drama.

"Kannan was walking toward that ruined mandapam said to be inhabited by Katteri and Payasa Muni. I did not give up either. Hiding again and again, I followed behind him.

"Kannan reached the sacrificial pedestal, stood and looked all around. Then he sat down and removed two stones from beneath it. For a few minutes he busied himself with something. I stayed well hidden and watched.

"Then Kannan left.

"After he had gone, I went over and examined the pedestal closely. I realized he had dug a small pit and buried something there. I dug where I suspected. There was an old metal plate, a piece of turmeric, a small bone and some other bits of rubbish. I picked them up and threw them far away. Then I closed the pit so that it looked exactly as before and hurried after Kannan. He had crossed the southern street and

<!-- source boundary: scan 107 → scan 108 -->

was heading toward the kiln-bank. I followed slowly, never letting him out of sight.

"There was an Ayyanar temple there—a very famous one. The people of that village said their Ayyanar was the son of Sabarimala Ayyappa Swami's paternal uncle! Many people had made vows to him and placed clay horses in front of the temple.

"A gambler named Patanjali Pillai had once made ten lakh rupees at a single stroke by bribing the jockeys riding the other horses in a race and having his own horse declared the winner. He had fulfilled his vow by setting up a model of a *Manja Kalyani* horse before the temple. They say that at night Ayyanar mounts one of those clay horses and crosses all the rivers.

"Under the leg of one of the horses at that temple entrance, Kannan began his work. When he left, I carried out my own investigation at the same place. It was the same sort of old rubbish as near the sacrificial pedestal. I took the things out, threw them into the kiln fire, closed the pit as before and left.

"Next, Kannan carried out his work under a bridge in the western direction. I carried out mine too.

"Finally, in the north, beside the irrigation channel, he went and sat beneath a tall coconut tree that had split into two trunks. I watched from some distance. I expected him to dig a pit at the foot of the tree. Instead, he sat silently and looked around. Then he stood up. I could not understand what he was doing.

"All at once he climbed the coconut tree. From close to the base it divided into two trunks. He climbed one of them

<!-- source boundary: scan 108 → scan 109 -->

right to the top. After a short while he came down. He took something from the fold of his garment and rubbed something onto the tree. Then he left.

"I understood that he had hidden the plate at the top of the tree. But what could I do? I did not know how to climb a coconut tree.

"Thinking hard, I went to the tree and examined it. He had taken something out and smeared it there—what was it? Then I understood. To mark exactly which of the two trunks he had climbed and hidden the plate in, he had smeared a little lime on it.

"While the lime was still damp, I scraped it off with my finger, smeared it on the other trunk, and erased every trace of white from the original spot. Then I hurried after Kannan again.

"He had returned to the school. Somehow I slipped inside without letting him see me. Just then Kannan came looking for me and saw that I was not where I had been lying.

"I returned too. 'Where did you go, vathiyare? I simply can't sleep... I came thinking that if you were awake we could sit and talk,' Kannan said, covering himself.

"I went one step beyond him. 'Is that so! I was sleeping very happily. I only just got up and went toward the street. You must have come to wake me during that moment!' I said, making a lie sound like truth.

"After that we talked for a while. Pretending not to know, Kannan asked, 'They say there is a coconut tree in this village that splits into two trunks?'

"Pretending not to understand him either, I said, 'Yes. It is a remarkable tree! Even its coconuts sell for more than ordinary coconuts!'

<!-- source boundary: scan 109 → scan 110 -->

"Next he asked, 'How long has this village been suffering like this?'

"'Suffering? What suffering? Who is suffering?' I asked.

"'All this disease, death, poverty and misery in the village—how long has it been like this?' Kannan asked.

"'For a long time,' I said casually.

"'From tomorrow the curse gripping this village will disappear,' he said.

"'Why? Are you leaving the village?' I asked.

"He did not understand the joke. 'I'm going to take out the plates—the plates!' he said proudly.

"It had grown very late. We closed our eyes toward dawn, thinking we might sleep for a little while.

"The next day the whole village was in an uproar. News spread like wildfire that Karagam-dancer Kannan was going to remove the plates buried by a Malayali sorcerer. Thousands gathered in that small village—four times the crowd that had come for the karagam dance.

"Everyone spoke in fear and wonder about the plate-removing ceremony. Suppose the Malayali deity fought back with greater power and drove away the deity Kannan invoked? Then Kannan would have to die on the spot—blood pouring from his mouth and nose, falling dead there. Poor fellow!

"They gathered with such sympathetic fears. 'Poor fellow—such a handsome young man! Why does he need this kind of courage? Why is he going to test his strength against a Malayali deity?' many said, scolding him affectionately.

"In the morning Kannan bathed thoroughly, smeared sandal paste over his whole body, placed a broad vermilion mark

<!-- source boundary: scan 110 → scan 111 -->

on his forehead, slung a chrysanthemum garland across his neck, and stood on a bullock cart.

"The cart was beautifully decorated. Banana saplings were tied around it, and sambrani smoke spread everywhere, arranged to captivate whoever saw it. With tarai, tappattai, horn and other instruments sounding, Karagam-dancer Kannan was brought in procession.

"He came with his eyes shut and one hand raised high, shouting, 'Hey Kalika Devi! Mariamma! Mother! Sakthi! Sakthi!' Anyone seeing that performance could have been deceived.

"'There! Take me to the Lower Street Payasa Muni mandapam!' he shouted. The cart went there.

"'Here! It is under this very sacrificial pedestal that the Malayali sorcerer buried the first plate. Hey Kali! Hey Mari! With your power I will take out that plate and crush it to dust—watch!' he cried, digging furiously beneath the pedestal.

"But there was no plate there... I had already taken it away.

Aravazhi said this to Pooncholai with a smile and turned toward Ponmani. She stood like a statue.

Can a statue smile?

That question gnawed at Aravazhi.

"Hm, then what happened?... Did Kannan search in all four directions and end up disappointed? Aiyo, poor fellow! You're a very mischievous boy, thambi! What happened after that? Tell us!" Pooncholai asked.

Aravazhi answered:

"At every place Kannan was disappointed, and the people who trusted him were disappointed too. At last they came to the coconut tree with the two trunks. Kannan looked for the mark he had made.

<!-- source boundary: scan 111 → scan 112 -->

"How was he to know it had been shifted?

"Poor fellow—he climbed rapidly. Aiyo, what pity! He came down disappointed.

"All kinds of talk broke out in the crowd. Kannan's head bent of its own accord. Even then he did not give up his trickery. Grinding his teeth like a man seized by possession, he shouted fiercely, 'Hey Malayali Bhagavathi! You cannot deceive me! Very well—tomorrow morning I will crush your arrogance!' He swore it.

"At once I interrupted. 'I'll crush *your* arrogance right now!' I said.

"Everyone, including Kannan, stared at me in astonishment.

"I explained before them all the entire secret of the previous night. Pointing to the exact trunk where Kannan had placed the plate, I said, 'There it is—on that trunk. The plate! Take it down!'

"One man climbed the tree and brought down the plate exactly as I had said.

"The villagers' anger turned upon Kannan. They would have beaten him to death right there if I alone had not stopped them.

"In the end the people who had come to watch the plate-removing ceremony listened to my speech and went home.

"Kannan gave me one look and left. The meaning of that look was only this: 'Revenge for revenge!'

"The moment I was transferred to Sirur, Kannan came to my mind. Along with him came the memory of that look. Even so, I decided it did not matter and set out.

"I left the village, travelled by train and car and other vehicles, and finally entered this area in a bullock cart. I had been travelling all night and was exhausted. I had planned my journey so that I would reach this village only around four in the morning.

<!-- source boundary: scan 112 → scan 113 -->

"Accordingly I entered the village. At the edge of the village the cart-bulls lay down and refused to get up again. So I paid the cartman his fare and began walking, carrying my own boxes and bedding.

"That was when I suddenly happened to meet Kannan.

"Not only Kannan... through Kannan's 'help'... good people like you as well..."

Aravazhi kept stretching out the words, unable to bring himself to stop, while sending only his eyes toward Ponmani.
