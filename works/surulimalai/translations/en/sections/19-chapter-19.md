## 19

"Listen—that flute you can hear. Do you know who it is? He's the Krishna of this village—a mischievous boy named Surulimalai!" Singaram said.

"I've seen him—I've seen him. I know that boy," Aravazhi replied, turning away for fear that Singaram might notice the shade of emotion that had crossed his face.

The moment he heard the name Surulimalai, Ponmani's image began dancing before his eyes. The chance that had taken him to her house, the affection and hospitality her mother had shown him, and above all the delight he had gained from the scorpion-sting episode—all of it sweetened his heart.

Singaram spoke again, breaking those pleasant memories.

"That boy has an elder sister—a beautiful girl! Very good-natured! Ponmani really is a golden jewel worthy of her name. My thangachi has a tremendous desire to get her married to me!"

Aravazhi's face changed suddenly. He struggled hard to keep the change from showing. He pretended to receive the news with complete indifference.

<!-- source boundary: scan 144 → scan 145 -->

"Why? If she's a good girl, why not marry her according to your sister's wish?" he even asked.

Yet as he said it, fear gripped him. What if Singaram answered, "Yes, that's exactly what I've decided"?

Fortunately Singaram gave no such shocking reply.

"I think of Ponmani as my second younger sister!"

Aravazhi could feel that the words had burst out from the depths of Singaram's heart. Even so, he kept himself steady and did not spill his own feelings there.

"My thangachi Karpuram—she's true camphor! Touch a match to her and she catches fire. Suddenly, suddenly, possession comes and she dances. Anger comes just as quickly—and so does affection for someone!

"Now look at the comedy of it. That Kannan goes roaming about with one eye fixed on Ponmani. My sister doesn't understand this and wanders around longing for *him*! The woman her lover is chasing is supposed to become my wife. And she's going to marry the mortal enemy of my family. Tell me, appa—what kind of madness is this in my sister?" Singaram asked Aravazhi wearily.

Aravazhi felt a new fear: was Kannan going to sprout up as an enemy in *his* love as well?

Still, to settle the doubt, he asked eagerly, "The girl's family is willing to give her to Kannan, aren't they?"

<!-- source boundary: scan 145 → scan 146 -->

"Nothing like that! Even if they agreed, I wouldn't let that marriage happen. Better to tie the girl up and set her afloat in the sea than give her to Kannan!"

Singaram spoke with such intensity.

Aravazhi was delighted at the thought that another ally had come to him, without his even seeking one, in the task of protecting what was dearest to his life. He felt he wanted to become still closer to Singaram.

"In just one day of knowing each other we've spoken about so much. But you still haven't unloaded any of the burden in your mind onto me," Aravazhi said regretfully.

"Let all my worries and sorrows stay with me. Don't trouble your own mind uselessly by hearing them, thambi."

Singaram closed his eyes. He did so to hide that his eyes had become pools. But contrary to what he intended, drops forced their way through his lashes and wet his cheeks.

"I've been waiting for the right time to take revenge. And my sister is desperate to form a marriage alliance with that sinner's family! Aiyyo! Appa! What am I to do? How am I to fulfil the promise I gave you?" Singaram cried aloud.

He covered his face with his hands and began weeping again.

"Son! I die happily because I trust that you will carry it out. That family must be wiped out root and branch. Do not show the slightest mercy because there are children in it! Do not hesitate even for a moment because the villagers may condemn you! Do not fear that the law may stop you!\n\n<!-- source boundary: scan 146 → scan 147 -->\n\nNever forget that a father's word is greater than the village and the law that rules it!

"Do not care who tries to stop what you are going to do! Son! Even if I myself were somehow to return to life and try to stop you, you must not listen!

"Black spots! Red spots! A donkey procession! The villagers must see that sight. Even if the result is murder, so be it. Whatever happens, so be it. Even if you receive life imprisonment—or the gallows—do not be afraid at all! Risk your life and carry out my command!

"Here is your sister. It is enough if you give her a life. Do not worry about your own life! Let the family shoot that springs from her womb carry our family's honour for many generations. Think of that and sacrifice your life for my command!

"No father would give his son such a harsh punishment—but I give it to you! Not without reason, my dear boy. You know completely what created this hunger for revenge. That is a family that committed an unspeakably wicked act no sinner would dare commit! Should that family live? Should it retain honour? Should the village remain ignorant of that family's corruption?

"So take revenge, son—take revenge! Answer for answer! Revenge for revenge! Carry out my command, and only then come to see my grave! Until then, you must not even look at my grave from a distance!

"If, for any reason, you fail to carry out my command... it will mean that you spat upon my grave! It will mean that you placed an *erukkam*-flower garland on my grave!"

The words his father had spoken on his deathbed tortured Singaram, cutting him apart bit by bit.

If he failed to carry out his father's command...?

<!-- source boundary: scan 147 → scan 148 -->

"Aiyyo... my chest burns merely remembering the harsh words Appa spoke," he lamented and cried.

"Do not care who stops you!" That was his father's fierce command.

But now the one standing in the way was his sister. And his father had also commanded him to give that same sister a good life.

To whom could he explain this strange situation and seek a remedy? A wave on the seashore throws something onto the sand. A larger wave drags it back. Another wave brings it ashore again, and the next carries it out to sea. In just that way, his father's command tossed him back and forth.

Aravazhi understood how badly Singaram was suffering in this inner struggle. Not wanting to hurt him further, he sat silently.

"Why have you gone quiet, thambi? Say something," Singaram said, wiping his eyes.

"Let's not say anything now. Tomorrow is a school holiday for me. I'll stay at the school without going anywhere. If you would please come there..." Aravazhi let the request trail off.

"I'll certainly come. I absolutely will!" Singaram said, getting up.

Once on his feet, he looked at the tree and said, "Maina," one time. Then he began walking away.

Aravazhi watched him leave, then turned back in deep thought.

Surulimalai came riding on the temple bull. The moment Aravazhi saw him, an indescribable flood of happiness rose within him.

<!-- source boundary: scan 148 → scan 149 -->

"How are you, thambi? Everyone at home—appa, amma—are they all well?" he asked.

He did not ask, "Is your akka well?" All that asking about appa and amma was mere courtesy. What he really ought to have asked was, "Is your akka well?" Yet he himself felt shy to ask it.

Surulimalai answered quite normally: "Everyone is well—including akka!"

He did not say this deliberately or mockingly. He simply thought Aravazhi had forgotten that an elder sister existed and supplied the omission.

But the answer pricked Aravazhi sharply. Wasn't his a guilty heart?

"Tell me—every time I see you, you're roaming around with the temple bull and the flute. Have you no interest at all in studying?"

Surulimalai gave no answer.

Aravazhi continued.

"Listen to me. Study at least a little while every day!"

No answer. Surulimalai smiled.

"Will you start studying from tomorrow?"

Silence again.

"Don't you like studying?"

Surulimalai stared closely at Aravazhi.

"'Learn while young' is an old saying, thambi! It's good to study at this age. What doesn't bend at five won't bend at fifty. Numbers and letters are said to be our eyes. Education is very important—very important! Do you understand what I'm saying, thambi?"

<!-- source boundary: scan 149 → scan 150 -->

Surulimalai watched Aravazhi even more intently. He still did not answer.

"Why are you staring? Does what I'm saying sound like a madman's babble? Don't think that way. Only through study can you learn about the world. Knowledge will shine. Don't treat it lightly, thambi!"

Now Surulimalai burst into laughter.

Aravazhi could not understand. He was bewildered.

Then Surulimalai took a roll of paper from the fold of his clothing and handed it to Aravazhi.

Aravazhi eagerly took it, opened it and read.

It was a petition signed by the villagers demanding that he be transferred immediately from that village to a school elsewhere.

The petition did not shock Aravazhi greatly. It was what he had expected. Nor did the accusations written in it disturb him.

Five or six sheets had been joined together. The first sheet listed the reasons he should be transferred: he was disrespectful; he behaved arrogantly toward the villagers; he lectured others as though he knew everything; he did not teach lessons properly; he would not make anything worthwhile of the children; all he did in school was tell useless stories.

Such accusations filled the first page. The following sheets were filled with the villagers' signatures.

Panchayat Paramasivam's was the very first signature.

Aravazhi read down the list, seeing who else had signed.

Then he started as though a sledgehammer had struck his heart. Pain seized him.

At the very end of the signature list was Ponmani's signature too.
