## 15

Although Aravazhi's story astonished Pooncholai, it did not make her disbelieve him. His face and gentle speech had already created such attachment and affection for him in her.

Pooncholai had learned only to bow devoutly before old beliefs; she had never turned toward the direction of new ideas. Aravazhi's words did not transform such an old woman all at once, but they did bring her as far as deciding that Karagam-dancer Kannan was a cheat. The powers of magic and sorcery were all real, of course! But she could come down to the position that Kannan had pretended to possess those powers and deceived people.

She was very eager to learn why the temple bull had chased Aravazhi. The daughter was even more eager than the mother to hear that story.

<!-- source boundary: scan 113 → scan 114 -->

Why would she not be? The heart of a cultured mother bound Aravazhi with a cord of affection. Ponmani's heart, leaving room for no one else, shut Aravazhi alone inside and folded itself around him like a lotus bud. What great comfort that act gave her! A deep sigh! A feeling that she had already won! A dream of delight! Tears of happiness gave still more lustre to eyes that were already shining. With those eyes she devoured him bit by bit and packed him into her heart.

He too seemed to have opened his heart before her, hung welcoming festoons with his eyes, and called, "Colourful parrot! Come! Come!"

"Thambi! Leave all that aside—that cheat Kannan can lie where he is! Tell us properly how the temple bull chased you!" Pooncholai asked, instantly breaking Aravazhi's train of thought.

"Kannan arranged the whole thing to take revenge on me!" Aravazhi said firmly.

"I was coming along the street alone, carrying my box and bedding... Dawn was near... Even one or two roosters had begun crowing early. Suddenly Kannan appeared in front of me. The instant I saw him, he saw me too. 'Come, ayya, Rationalism! So you've arrived! Sabash! Sabash!' he shouted loudly. I stood where I was without getting agitated and smiled. I don't know whether he saw my smile or not—but before I expected it, he drew back his arm and threw a

<!-- source boundary: scan 114 → scan 115 -->

punch at my head. Because I was carrying the box and bedding in my hands, I could not block it immediately. Before I could put the things down and ready myself to fight back, another punch landed on my chest..."

The moment she heard this, Pooncholai melted. "Ammaadi! My precious boy!"

Ponmani felt as though spears had pierced her own chest. She trembled, thinking how much pain there must have been in Aravazhi's chest. Tears flooded her cheeks without her realizing it.

Aravazhi began describing the fight.

"The punch landed on my chest and I lost my balance. Even so, I caught my breath and prepared to resist Kannan. He did not come at me for an honest fight. The moment he saw that I was going to resist, he lifted a large black stone and took aim at my head. I looked around. There was nothing I could use as a weapon. He did not throw the stone at me either. Perhaps he was afraid that if he missed and it fell to the ground, I would pick it up. He chased me with the stone in his hand.

"I had no choice but to run. I ran toward a tree. The temple bull was lying there. I could see someone lying on a roadside load-rest. Kannan saw him too. Suddenly he hurled the stone at me with great force. I moved out of its path. But the stone struck the bull.

"The bull sprang up with a great roar. Kannan flew away from there. That was all I knew. The bull charged at me. I ran. I ran. I rolled in the street. It seemed the man lying on the load-rest shouted something,

<!-- source boundary: scan 115 → scan 116 -->

but I could not hear clearly what it was. The bull thought *I* was the one who had thrown the stone at it and chased me. What happened after that—you all know already!" Aravazhi stopped.

"Do you know who was lying on that load-rest? Me!" Surulimalai said with a laugh.

"What—you? Then you shouted something. What was it?" Aravazhi asked.

"'Don't let him go! Kill him! Kill him!' That's what I shouted," Surulimalai said casually.

"You sinner! Is *that* what you shouted?" Pooncholai scolded.

"What else was I supposed to do? I thought somebody had come to steal my bull, so I shouted like that!" he said. Then he added proudly, "In the end I saved you, didn't I?"

Aravazhi looked at Surulimalai with affection. "Is that bull a close friend of yours?" he asked.

"Why? Is that beneath me? Being friends with cattle is far better than being friends with human beings! My opinion is that it's better to keep company with dogs, donkeys, horses, cows, goats and chickens than with ungrateful traitors!"

Surulimalai's answer astonished Aravazhi and made him laugh.

"Well, well—you talk like a great philosopher..." Aravazhi said, laughing.

"Why drag Vedanta and Siddhanta into this? Experience is enough... experience!" said Surumalai.

<!-- source boundary: scan 116 → scan 117 -->

"Experience? What experience have *you* had at this age, thambi?"

"Age matters for experience—I agree. But there's nothing surprising in old people having the experience of children and little children having the experience of old people! The temple bull Siva and I became friends. Do you know when we became friends?"

"How would I know? You are the experienced man—tell me yourself!"

"You're going to be living in this village, aren't you? I'll tell you after a few more days!"

"Why postpone it, thambi? Just tell me now!"

"Uh-uh. I can't tell you now! I'll tell you later!"

Saying this, Surulimalai rose and quickly left the house.

Aravazhi watched him go with attention, then finally turned toward Ponmani. She was no longer there. Before he could wonder where she had gone, a voice came from the backyard veranda.

"Amma! Amma! Come running! Come running!"

"Aiyyo! What happened, Ponmani?" Pooncholai ran. She trembled, thinking something must have happened to Ponnaiya, who had gone toward the backyard. Fortunately it was nothing like that. A scorpion had stung Ponmani on the finger. She had gone to get a dung-cake for the stove, and a scorpion in it had given her finger a thorough sting. Ponmani writhed and sagged with pain.

<!-- source boundary: scan 117 → scan 118 -->

Aravazhi too slowly rose and hurried toward the backyard veranda.

"Aiyyo! A scorpion has stung the girl! What am I going to do?" Pooncholai began lamenting. She would go around treating the whole village, but the moment danger came inside her own house her mind would not work at all.

Ponnaiya came stumbling to the spot. "Run and bring Kannan! He'll chant a mantra and draw the poison out!" he cried urgently.

"Yes, Kannan indeed—Kannan and mud! We don't need him. What does he know? His mantra couldn't even make a mango fall!" Pooncholai snapped back, then went on crying.

Ponmani could not bear the pain and burning. Sweat poured all over her body. She did not even care that Aravazhi stood before her; she cried out loudly.

Aravazhi looked at Pooncholai. "Shall I treat this?" he asked.

"You know how? Somehow make her better! You'll earn a crore of blessings!" Pooncholai pleaded.

How a smile came to Ponmani amid so much anguish, pain, poison twisting her nerves and making her head spin—who could say? Such is the greatness of love!

Aravazhi hurried toward the backyard and searched along the fence. Then he plucked ten or fifteen green leaves from a plant and came back inside. He put the leaves in his mouth and chewed them. Coming near Ponmani, he said, "Turn your ear this

<!-- source boundary: scan 118 → scan 119 -->

way."

Her whole attention had already turned toward him. Blessing the scorpion inwardly, she turned her ear. With his mouth full of chewed leaves, he blew forcefully into her ear. She had the pain of the sting on one side—and the ticklish sensation of breath being blown into her ear on the other.

"Is it enough just to blow the air from those leaves without saying any mantra?" Ponnaiya asked in surprise, interrupted by his troublesome cough.

"The leaves draw out the poison; the mantra draws out the money!" Aravazhi replied with a laugh.

As he kept blowing the leaf-scented breath into Ponmani's ear, she felt the force of the poison slowly begin to subside. He placed some more of the leaves on the finger and tied them there. The new sensation of his touch on the finger struck by the scorpion's sting spread through her body like a breeze.

The treatment continued for more than half an hour. The flood of sweat on Ponmani's body disappeared. The poison's torment had completely passed. The burning was gone. The pain had vanished entirely. Aravazhi understood this very well.

Even so, he looked at her and asked, "How is it now?"

"Aiyyo! I can't bear it... Ah... aiyyo!..." she writhed.

At the speed of her writhing, he began blowing into her ear again. In his hurry and agitation, his lips once brushed the rim of her ear. Another time they touched the edge of her cheek.

"How is it now, amma?" her mother asked after a while.

<!-- source boundary: scan 119 → scan 120 -->

How could she possibly explain to her mother how it was? "It still burns up to my wrist, amma!" she said.

The blowing treatment continued. Once Aravazhi repeated to himself: the "blowing treatment." Blowing—*oodal*, lovers' sulking—what if one turned into the other? A thrill ran through Aravazhi's whole body. Boundless delight!

"How far does it burn now?" the mother asked.

"Only up to the finger!" the daughter replied.

"He blew—and blew—and kept on blowing!"

"Is it all right now?" Pooncholai asked.

"Yes, amma," Ponmani finally said, only half-heartedly.

Pooncholai began searching for the scorpion, regretting that they had let it escape without killing it.

"Good thing it got away—won't it come sting me again tomorrow?" Ponmani murmured eagerly inside herself.
