# 51

They were Mayandi's own words. The letter began, “To dear Durai.”

“Heartfelt greetings to dear Durai. Because of the work in which I am involved, I have begun to fear that the two of us may never be able to meet again. That is why I am writing this letter in the hope that it may somehow reach your hands, or the hands of some other good person.

“If the time comes when you are reading this letter, it will mean that I am already dead. Perhaps you may even have to read it while standing beside my corpse. I began my journey like an ascetic who had come to despise everything. Then you and Parimala crossed my path. Along with the responsibility of protecting the treasure secret came the responsibility of protecting the two of you as well. Even calling it a responsibility does not quite fit. It must be called affection, my son—affection is what it must be called. I cannot see your astonishment or your sharp eyes widening, my boy! I am your father, Kumaravadivu.

“I am that Kumaravadivu who, unable to make a living in his own country, crossed the sea in order to eke out a livelihood. I had told you that Kumaravadivu entered a temple, was bitten by a snake while prying loose the idols, and died. No, my son—no! Kumaravadivu did not die then. After coming out of prison, Kumaravadivu made a living by continuing the work of prying loose idols.

“Not under the name Kumaravadivu, but under the name Mayarandi! It was while doing that work that one day, in the temple at Marungappallam, the treasure secret fell into my hands. I told you I had been bitten by a snake; though it was not truly a snake in form, it came into my life with the nature of a snake. After the treasure secret came to me, my life took a turn. Carrying that secret around as though I had tied a snake inside my garment, I began wandering from place to place. I moved among the people like a doctor, like a holy man. Somehow, I managed to live.

“I am the owner of gold coins worth countless crores. Yet I did not even wish to see them with my own eyes. A kind of weariness had taken hold of me. I have pried loose idols, stolen the coins and stones beneath them, and used that to keep my life going. But I have never even raised my eyes to look at the heaps of gold lying there in crores for me. I have already told you why that is, Durai! Why should I make you remember the old story again?

“If I do make you remember it, is it the gold bars and diamond stones buried beneath the earth that come to mind? Is it not Thangam, precious as gold, my mother, and my beloved Valliammal who lie dimly beneath the soil like treasures that can never be recovered? There is no time to weep over all that, my boy. I have seen you—that is enough. It is as though I found the treasure itself!

“Before meeting you and Parimala, the calculation I had made about that three-letter Tamil word, love, was altogether different. You have read my entire diary and learned about me yourself. My son! If any of those diaries still remain, write this on the last page:

“‘My father thought love was a cremation ground. But he came to the conclusion that, at times, flowering plants can sprout there too.’

“Write it like that, my son! Neither you nor Parimala should think that my opinion of love has changed completely. Just as a rose may grow on a plant full of thorns, there may be a few Durais and a few Parimalas. That is the final point in my calculation. Perhaps this total may someday have to be increased. Would people like me refuse to be happy about that?

“In a land where wild elephants like Velliyambalam roam, it is indeed difficult for fawns like you and Parimala to live in peace. But Durai, you and your beloved endured so many obstacles and stood proudly to defend your kingdom of love. I praise you for that. At the same time, it would be good to reserve half the place you have set aside in your heart for love for service to the people. I lost the strength to enter that work while I was still young. Ceylon squeezed me dry. I wore away my body in the tea country. The later part of my life withered in prison. In the final part I made a small effort. Before I could do much, self-interest intervened—the self-interest of having to save my son and daughter-in-law! That is why I now entrust that work to you.

“So many noble souls of sacrifice leave behind house, threshold, cattle and property and set out singing of the nation, the nation. You too must shine as one among their ranks, Durai! Do not leave Parimala behind. Take her with you! Is there any shortage of work to be done in this country? There are hundreds of Velliyambalams. Every one of them must be taught a lesson.

“The work of good men must be to put an end to Ambalams who strut about as great men of the village and live wastefully. Good heavens; this Ambalam, with only ordinary means, can run such a jungle-king durbar—then in how many ways might those so-called wealthy lords with great fortunes make the people dance to their tune!

“Has Ambalam committed only one murder or two? Is this Ambalam not a cruel man who throws dust into the eyes of the police and then lives triumphantly, Durai? In my selfish desire to save you, I believe that without realizing it I have also performed a public service. As I write this letter, I do not know what Ambalam's end will be. But I have created circumstances in which he must inevitably fall into the hands of the police. You already know that I was the one who cut off and threw away the hand of Ayyappan, who came licking his lips to seize the treasure.

“But because of Ambalam's scheme, you and I gained our freedom. Ambalam handed Dukkaram over in my place; at that time he needed both you and me. Dukkaram became the sacrifice to that need. Alas, what a pity—find some way somehow to free Dukkaram, who is wasting away in prison! This very letter is good evidence. When I myself say that I was the one who cut off Ayyappan's hand, there is no justice in punishing Dukkaram, is there? I could have confessed to that crime earlier. But if I had confessed and gone to prison, who knows what might have become of you and Parimala?

“It was only because I remained outside that I was able to deceive Velliyambalam himself and get you and Parimala married at his own expense. My boy, do you know how much I suffered for that? I prepared false plates and false boxes. Through them I deceived Velliyambalam.

“Finally I said blood was needed. Through that I arranged your marriage. Events happened so rapidly that my arrangements had to keep changing suddenly, but I turned all those events to our advantage. I won over Raman, the servant in Ambalam's house. He was the one who told me that Ambalam himself had killed Menaka! It was also through him that I learned that a kudukuduppai fortune-teller had been arrested in connection with Menaka's murder. The moment I heard ‘fortune-teller,’ I was afraid that Parimala herself might have been caught! Later I learned that it was a woman named Shokasundari!

“Durai! If you can, free that Shokasundari and make Raman happy! Likewise, with Dukkaram's release, the blame resting on Parimala will also disappear. Take careful responsibility for securing the freedom of both of them.

“What more is there to write, my son!

“You need not fear that Thandavan will interfere in your life. Thandavan is a very close relative of ours. When I told him the truth that I am Kumaravadivu and asked him for his daughter for my son, he was plunged into immeasurable happiness. Only after that did he too dare to oppose Ambalam. You would not have seen the performance he put on with nothing but goat's blood smeared on his knife. Give him my thanks!

“Do not forget Raman—that little fellow helped me at the right time.

“If a case is conducted regarding Menaka's murder, he is qualified to be examined as an eyewitness. I nearly forgot to tell you one more thing. Those friends fortune has given you—every one of them is a gem, my boy, a gem! Mountains of friendship! Ah, was the suffering Anandan went through a small thing? I set fire to Ambalam's house. You were inside, in the nuptial room! The house was set on fire with the intention of getting you out. At that time the doors had been bolted from inside—by Ambalam's men! If there had been even a little delay, you and Parimala would have been reduced to ashes. At that moment your friend Anandan leapt into the house—ah! Shower that good man with my blessings.

“I could keep extending this letter—there is still so much I have to tell you! Another hope keeps raising its head: if I do not die and meet you in person, we can talk about many things. If we met like that, I would have been terribly ashamed and found it difficult to tell you that I am your father.

“If I happen to die before your eyes, I can hand you the treasure secret in my lap. Otherwise it will mean that both it and I have disappeared.

“My boy, do not forget everything I have written. I am coming. What use is there in saying farewell again after already taking leave and going away? Only now I find myself thinking: fools cry out about moksha and hell—why should those things not exist? If they do, would not my ‘antaratma’ remain there watching you and rejoicing? Never mind—I was of some use before dying: I saw you and managed to do a few good things for you. That is enough!

“My beloved son! Give my blessings to my dear daughter-in-law! If my corpse comes into your sight, bury it beside Thangam!”

Your loving father,  
Kumaravadivu.

Durai and Parimala fell upon the old man's corpse, rolling over it and weeping.

Durai cried out, “Appa! Appa!”

The old man looked like someone sleeping in exhaustion after completing all his work.

The police who had gone in search of Ambalam returned empty-handed.

All the people of Marungappallam welcomed Durai and Parimala with joyful cheers. The old man's grave and the memorial to Thangam were placed side by side. Following Mayandi's letter, the police checked whether the kudukuduppai fortune-teller in prison really was Shokasundari and discovered that he was the escaped Dukkaram. Dukkaram would not be caught in Menaka's murder case. Nor had he any involvement in the Ayyappan case. People who knew the law said that he would receive only five or six months' punishment for the offence of escaping from prison. Hearing this, Parimala breathed a great sigh of happiness.

With that joyful news, she and Durai flew towards Manora to sing a song of love.

They wandered along the Manora seashore like skylarks. As they went on singing their song of happiness, a figure was bobbing before them, battered by the sea waves. They went closer and looked. It was Velliyambalam's lifeless body!

Durai stared at Ambalam's corpse. One hand of the corpse was tightly clenched. With difficulty he opened it and looked. Inside the hand was the treasure secret. Durai looked at it in astonishment.

Parimala jumped and cried, “Agar! The treasure secret?” Durai said, “Yes,” and threw it into the sea.

“Ayyo! You threw away the treasure!” Parimala exclaimed in shock.

“Let it go! When I have you, a speaking treasure, why do I need that treasure which breeds great enmity?” Embracing her, he set out for the police station to report the news that Velliyambalam had returned!
