# 37

The old man untied everyone. They rose with deep breaths. One or two were suffering from serious injuries.

“Where is Durai?” Mayandi asked again anxiously.

They told him that Durai had fallen into Velliyambalam's hands. A fresh worry came upon Parimalam. The old man too was shocked.

Parimalam clearly understood why Velliyambalam must have taken Durai away. It was obvious to her that he intended to extract the treasure secret from him.

She began to tell old Mayandi all of Velliyambalam's plots in detail. From the moment she had been carried to the Manora, through meeting Ambalam again at the riverbank and being hidden in the bungalow beside the canal, she described every cruel event—including the pitiful fate into which Dukkaram had been drawn.

The old man had heard of Velliyambalam's rule like a wild king's court, but he had never heard that he was capable of conspiracies this vast. So he listened in astonishment.

While recounting the events, Parimalam repeatedly insisted that somehow they must free Dukkaram too.

“First we must free Durai. Only afterward can Dukkaram's matter be attended to,” the old man replied. Then he began pacing thoughtfully over the beach sand. Waves roared competitively in the sea and in his mind. A deep sigh escaped him, perhaps as a sign of his thought about the strange circumstances he had been forced to face along the path he was travelling.

Parimalam sat on the sand like a vessel that had lost its direction. Only one of Durai's friends, a man named Anandan, wished to break the silence.

“What will happen if we simply sit here? Let us see what must be done next,” he began.

“Yes, that is right. Ananda! Go and bring a boat. Let us all go to Puduppattinam,” Mayandi said.

“Puduppattinam? Then what about my beloved?” Parimalam asked wearily. No one could see the spring of tears rising in her eyes. Such was the darkness of the hour.

“Listen, Parimalam. Can we rescue Durai by remaining here? We will all go to Puduppattinam. You stay at our Anandan's house. You must not come to Durai's house. The police may come there making inquiries and you may be seen. Then Thandavan may arrive too. So Anandan's house is the right place. I have reached a decision about how to free Durai. I will explain it fully in the morning. For now, it is better that all of us leave this place,” Mayandi said.

Anandan left to arrange a boat.

* * * *

At the very time when Mayandi, Parimalam and the friends were thinking here about how to free Durai, Velliyambalam learned that Parimalam had escaped and was thrown into agitation. The servant Raman received a proper “ritual beating.”

Ambalam understood that it would be dangerous to keep Durai, bound in the cart, confined in the bungalow beside the canal. He feared that Parimalam, now escaped, might reveal the bungalow's secret. So he decided to imprison Durai in his own house and had the cart driven there.

Velliyambalam's house was the largest house in Marungappallam village. Durai was led into that house, which had two or three sections, like a prisoner. A large room in the second section was opened and Durai was pushed inside.

All the men bowed to Ambalam and withdrew. With a foolish grin and a clearing of his throat, Ambalam went and stood before Durai.

Unable to contain his anger, Durai looked at him and asked, “Why all this injustice? Why are you torturing me like this?”

Velliyambalam laughed. “Everything has a purpose! Sleep now. We will talk in the morning,” he said, and started to leave.

“No. Tell me now! Why this cruelty? Where is my Parimalam?” Durai poured out anxiously.

“Parimalam is safe. Boy! If you want to see Parimalam, there is one small condition.”

“What condition?”

“You must give me that secret!”

“What secret?”

“The treasure secret!”

“……!”

“The treasure secret that you and Mayandi seized from the holy man. Give it to me and you and Parimalam can both be freed.”

“Lie! Did we seize a treasure secret from the holy man? Never!”

“Do not hide it! I will smash your skull!” Ambalam said, striking Durai hard on the head.

Durai, standing bound, could not bear it. He kicked Ambalam with his feet. Ambalam became a roaring tiger.

‘Palaaar! Palaaar!’ He slapped Durai's cheeks again and again with all his strength. In his rough hand appeared a thirukkai-vaal whip. Raising it, he demanded, “Are you going to give me the treasure secret or not?”

Durai stood without speaking.

“Answer me, fool!” The terrifying voice and the blows of the whip began alternating. Durai cried, “Amma! Appa!”

“Ayyo! I do not have the treasure secret!” he wailed.

His shirt tore and hung from him. Lines of blood covered his body. The skin on his forehead split and blood ran all over his face.

“Then who has it? That old fellow Mayandi? Tell me! Tell me the truth!” Velliyambalam shouted.

Durai could only groan “Amma; Appa” under the beating. He was not prepared to answer the question.

“Will you tell me—or not?” Ambalam seized his hair and shook him. He threatened to crush his throat and kill him. Durai did not yield to anything.

Durai understood what kind of thing the treasure secret was. It was the property of his father Kumaravadivu. Through Mayandi he had heard how his father had wished it to be used. So he had resolved to fulfil the wish that had arisen with his father's final breath. He did not even care if his own life was lost. He had decided that the treasure must not fall into the hands of wrongdoers like Ambalam.

“I will push needles beneath your fingernails, finger by finger, and learn the truth. Let dawn come!” Ambalam growled.

“Will cutting open the belly of a barren woman make a child appear?” Durai mocked him, enduring the pain.

Ambalam thought the blows and kicks had made Durai understand what a terrifying man he was. So he softened his tone a little.

“Listen, brother. If only you tell me that secret, I will give you every comfort you want—I will give you whatever you ask,” he said, watching Durai's face.

“‘I have made a cradle of rubies, so at least for its sake bear me a child’—no fool would ask a barren woman that,” Durai mocked him again. He could not even raise a hand to wipe the blood flowing from his wounds.

The reply to that mockery was the sound of the thirukkai-vaal whip. Ambalam struck him and struck him until he curled up and fell. He leapt about as though possessed.

“If you do not tell the truth, you will die! A grave for your corpse will be dug right here behind this house! Be warned!” he shouted.

He slammed the door, locked it from outside and went away. Durai lay on the floor, writhing without even enough strength to groan.

* * * *

Dawn began to break. Unable to bear the pain, Durai had twisted on the floor until exhaustion pulled him into a doze. With his hands bound and unable even to turn over, could he truly sleep? He woke, but did not open his eyes. With his eyelids closed he sank into thought.

“Who am I? Where was I born? Where did I grow up? How has the wheel of life turned through all these places? Why has such suffering come upon me at the end?”

He could find no answer.

Blood was seeping from his body. The same was true of his heart. Forgetting the entire world, he thought only of himself.

A faint sound near the window disturbed his thoughts. He opened his eyes and looked. Dawn had not fully broken. Someone was standing by the window.

Yes—it was the figure of a woman. In that dim hour he could not make out who she was.

“Who is it? Parimalam?” he asked eagerly.

She did not speak. She simply stood there.

Durai slowly rose and moved toward the window.
