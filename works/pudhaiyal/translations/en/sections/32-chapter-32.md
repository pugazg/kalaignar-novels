# 32

Without being seen by anyone, Ambalam, Parimalam and Dukkaram reached Marungappallam at night. Under the plan that they were to remain there secretly, Dukkaram and Parimalam were placed in Ambalam's bungalow beside the canal.

Left under the supervision of the servant Raman, they were tormented by his mischievous hospitality. Acting on Ambalam's instructions, Raman took charge of the cooking and began rolling pots and vessels around the kitchen. At exactly eleven o'clock he served everyone, including Ambalam, rice that had not cooked properly. Declaring that “Raman's cooking is always nalabagam,” he poured out a curry without tamarind and made them struggle through the meal.

In the middle of Parimalam's anguish, did she have time to study the flavours of half-cooked rice and tamarindless curry? She sat down and rose again only because Ambalam insisted. Dukkaram too noticed none of the tastes of the dishes. A man smacking his tongue in anticipation of tasting an unreachable fruit—would Raman's feast seem important to him? He too had no need for food.

Ambalam himself had no mind to sit and eat at leisure. A storm surrounded his thoughts.

Was he not a man tormented by an uncontrollable desire to make the treasure his own? He resolved to carry out his schemes one after another. The first plan was to discover whether Parimalam and Dukkaram really were lovers. For that, the two had to be left alone in the bungalow—while at the same time being guarded so that they could not deceive him and escape.

Ayyo! A great man of the village, enslaved by the greed to become lord of a mountain of gold—

—because of that greed, how many troublesome affairs he had to involve himself in!

“What an arrogant, idle fellow he is—I don't even know who he is. Young dandies like him normally walk seventy feet away when they see me. And I, of all people, have to bare my teeth and smile at this mere calf!”

Velliyambalam did not fail to think this to himself and feel aggrieved.

So many marriages in the village had either been stopped or made to happen through his intervention. Yet a man like him now had to bend and yield again and again, going out of his way to arrange Parimalam's marriage to some orphan. That too sent smoke rising in his pride-filled heart. But the dream of a heap of gold did not allow any of that resentment to last.

Looking at Durai and Parimalam, he said, “Be careful. I will come in the morning. Raman will be lying on the veranda. If you need anything, send word through him,” and left the bungalow.

Raman too prepared for sleep with a torn mat and a pillow from which the cotton was coming out.

Dukkaram could hardly contain his joy. What would it be like if a blind man's eyes were opened and a heap of gold were poured before him?

What would it be like if a deaf man were treated, given the power to hear, and then made to listen to a song in classical Tamil?

That was Dukkaram's state.

Parimalam, however, stood at a stage of trial in which she had to save herself. Unable to understand her mind, Dukkaram summoned a foolish grin. He spent a few seconds wondering whether Parimalam should speak first or whether he should. Then he gathered courage and opened his mouth.

“My darling!”

Parimalam remained silent.

“Parimalam! God has understood our sacred love—look how he has brought the two of us together!” Dukkaram began pouring out lines in the manner of a royal hero on a drama stage.

“My beloved! Why do you refuse to speak? Are you shy? Your shyness pierces and gouges my ribs like a spear!”

As he said this, he moved closer to her.

Parimalam, who had stood patiently until then, looked at Dukkaram with pity.

“Dukkaram!” she said.

“What? Dukkaram? Is it right to call your husband by his name? Sin! Sin!!” cried that mad fellow.

<!-- source scan 281 / printed 277 -->

“Listen to what I say. Like me, you too have fallen into trouble. I know your suffering. At a time like this the two of us must behave with a little intelligence,” Parimalam said.

“What? You speak to me without respect, saying ‘I’ and ‘you’ like this?”

“Listen to everything I have to say, Dukkaram. You already know that I have no love for you—no affection for you—don't you?”

“Ah! I feel faint!”

“Don't babble like this. To escape from Velliyambalam I told some lie, thinking you might also receive a small lesson. That joke has now grown into this much suffering. Believe me absolutely: I do not have even a drop of liking for you.”

“Don't say that, Parimalam! I am ready even to give my life for you. My heart wanders after you like a bicycle without a handlebar!”

Dukkaram tried to touch her. Parimalam angrily ordered him, “Get away!” He would not listen. He did not want to lose the precious opportunity he believed he had been given. He caught Parimalam and pulled her toward him.

Using all her strength, Parimalam struck him hard across the cheek.

Dukkaram stood motionless for a little while.

<!-- source scans 282-283 / printed 278-279: `நின்றான்` + `சிறிது நேரம்.` is one sentence across the physical page boundary. -->

The blow brought his mind into one stable state.

“Parimalam! Enough. Don't show me any more of what your hand can do. I cannot bear it,” he said, moving away and standing at a distance.

Parimalam began to feel pity for him.

“Now at least listen to me, Dukkaram,” she said.

“What are you saying? Say as much as you like with your mouth. Just don't say it with your hand!” he replied pitifully.

“Don't we both have to get free of Ambalam?”

“Certainly!”

“Then you must call me your beloved in name only. You must not behave wrongly toward me like this.”

“Why? For what? Why should I do you a favour by calling you my beloved only in name? I will tell the truth—that there is no relationship of any kind between you and me—and when dawn comes I will run away. Just watch!”

Parimalam was jolted. Fear rose that he might expose her to Ambalam. If she agreed with Dukkaram before Ambalam, Velliyambalam would use her as a tool to trap Durai. That terror arose with it.

But to save Durai, was she to submit to Dukkaram's wishes? Parimalam could not even bear to think about that question.

<!-- source scans 283-284 / printed 279-280 -->

Dukkaram kept waiting for Parimalam to give some favourable answer. He waited and waited. Finally, without even realising it, he fell asleep where he sat.

Parimalam did not close her eyes all night. She tossed in a sea of worry. Raman's snores from the street veranda and the croaking of frogs from the edges of the fields seemed to compete with each other.

All night, without anyone knowing, Ambalam circled around the bungalow and kept watch. From the conversation between Parimalam and Dukkaram, the truth became clear to him: they were not lovers, and Parimalam had deceived him. Anger surged in him. Whatever little respect he had held for Dukkaram shattered too. He had embraced the fellow only because he had business with him; in the morning, he decided, he would spit in his face and chase him away shouting, “Get out, you good-for-nothing!”

Dawn came.

Dukkaram woke and saw Parimalam before him. He lamented that the night of pleasure he had expected had passed like a dream.

Parimalam looked at him and spoke gently. “Do not be angry with me, Dukkaram. In anger and haste I hit you. Give me two days. I will think about whether I can fulfil your wish...”

<!-- source scan 284 / printed 280: dialogue remains physically open and continues on scan 285. -->

“...and decide,” she requested softly.

The foolish young man who had already been enchanted by the verbal magic of rough-voiced Velliyambalam—how could he fail to become the slave of the deceptive words of this beauty, a pure-gold maiden whose words made one wonder whether they were speech or honey, whether they were a flute or a cuckoo?

At first he hesitated. Then he was enchanted. She did not let him come near her, however. With the tiny joy that she had won, she waited for the sun to rise.

In another corner of the same village, a mother was beating her chest and stomach and lamenting that her daughter was missing. The daughter herself was being kept imprisoned here. Velliyambalam too, who had not closed his eyes all night, came and sat at the bungalow entrance and let his thoughts run.

There was no use trusting Dukkaram. From now on he had to catch Durai. But how could that be done? Where had Durai run and hidden himself? Velliyambalam remained tangled in such thoughts.

In the morning he showed neither Parimalam nor Dukkaram any sign of his anger. He behaved as though he knew nothing of what had happened during the night. Dukkaram too believed Parimalam's words and began speaking and behaving with her as before.

Evening came.

Raman ran to Ambalam, who sat at the bungalow entrance, and said excitedly, “Thandavan is coming.”

At once Ambalam told Parimalam and Dukkaram to remain carefully inside. Eager to see whether he could obtain some new information from Thandavan, he hurried out to receive him.

<!-- source scans 285-286 / printed 281-282: `பரி` + `மளாவையும்` is continuous inside `பரிமளாவையும்`. -->

“Has Parimalam been caught?” Ambalam asked hurriedly.

Thandavan pursed his lips and answered weakly, “No.”

“I heard the police searched everywhere,” Ambalam said.

“They did search. She fell into our hands—but then she deceived us too and disappeared somewhere,” Thandavan replied.

“What kind of joke is this? How can she fall into your hands and then disappear?”

“Is that the only joke? While we were searching for her, the police got a murder case!”

“A murder case?” Ambalam asked in astonishment.

“Yes. We caught Parimalam at Puduppattinam. She said some man had tried to ruin her, and that an old man and a young man had come to save her, and that a fight was taking place. We ran to the place she described. There was no one there. Only a severed arm lay there. Then Parimalam threw chilli powder into everyone's eyes and escaped. Later we learned that the house belonged to a man named Durai.”

<!-- source scan 286 / printed 282 -->

“Hmm. What happened after that?”

“What else? The matter I went for didn't happen. Instead the police got a new case!”

“How far has that case gone now?”

“How far? At twelve noon today the police arrested that man Durai and old Mayandi and took them away!”

Ambalam's face changed when he heard this. If Durai and Mayandi were caught in a murder case and punished, how would he ever learn the treasure secret? The question tore at his heart.

Thandavan, unaware of the turmoil inside Ambalam, continued talking at length about the murder case. He explained that if the case came to trial, he and even his daughter might have to appear as witnesses, and that somehow the police would solve it. None of this entered Ambalam's ears.

After sending Thandavan home, he sank again into thought.

“Durai and the old man must be released. Only then can the treasure be found. It would also be a mistake to chase Dukkaram away as I had planned. Even if he is not Parimalam's lover, he knows the treasure secret. Besides, if Durai is to be freed, the accusation must be placed on someone else in his place. This Dukkaram is the perfect sacrificial goat. If I can somehow involve him in the murder case, he will not even come asking for a share of the treasure.”

<!-- source scans 287-288 / printed 283-284: `எப்படியா` + `வது` is continuous inside `எப்படியாவது`; the quoted internal thought closes on scan 288. -->

Thoughts spun in this way.

Ambalam called Parimalam aside and told her that Durai and the old man had been arrested. Parimalam could not control herself.

“Ayyo! My beloved!” she cried.

Ambalam told her that he knew everything that had happened during the night, and that if Durai was to be freed she would have to do exactly as he said.

“If Durai is to be released, Dukkaram must go to the gallows,” Ambalam said.

“Ayyo, poor man—Dukkaram is innocent. No. Think of some other way,” Parimalam pleaded.

“There is no other way. There is only one way for your dear lover to survive—you must obey my words!” Ambalam said emphatically.

“All right. Tell me,” Parimalam said, preparing to hear him.
