# 3

Realising that the cry for help from the lower floor was Parimalam's, Durai began trembling badly. Parimalam had come to meet him that night, caring for nothing, driven by her unquenchable love for him, and had now been subjected to all these troubles—and he too had become a cause of them. The thought flashed for an instant in Durai's heart and vanished. He pulled himself together and began descending.

Before he had crossed the steps of even one gallery, another cry—“Darling!”—came from directly ahead. She was running toward him.

<!-- source: scan 30 → 31 -->

“What is it, Parimalam?” Durai asked in a trembling voice.

“They look like those same rough men—they're following us,” she had barely finished saying when a heavy blow fell on Durai's head from the upper part of the gallery. Yes, it was the old man: with all the strength he possessed, he had struck Durai with a stick. In the sudden shock Durai fainted. Parimalam sprang upon him and gathered him into her arms.

“Darling!” she cried now—and ah, how much life there was in that form of address! The sorrow-filled face of a beautiful woman acquires a new radiance; even the words of sorrow shed by such a radiant maiden seem to possess a flavour of their own. Parimalam embraced him crying “Darling,” and stroked his face with her flower-soft fingers; it was as though an empress of art were drawing out the sorrowful register of the veena of love. How soft are the petals of the flower of affection! How cool that flower becomes when soaked by hot tears. Parimalam appeared just such a flower of love. The scene was not allowed to remain there for long. The old man seized Parimalam's hand and dragged her roughly. Though she thrashed and tried to run, he would not let go; with the cloth he wore over himself he bound her eyes tightly. He tore off the part of the cloth left after blindfolding her.

<!-- source: scan 31 → 32 -->

“Ayyo! Let us go! Sinner! Sinner!” Parimalam screamed. With the remaining cloth, the old man tied Parimalam's hands behind her. She screamed, struggled, sagged. “Make a sound and you'll die!” the old man thundered frighteningly. Was this the same old man who only a short while ago had looked like the very embodiment of calm? Was this the old man who had prayed toward the sky like one who had studied the world? Parimalam understood nothing. Let whatever comes, come, she thought. She wept at the thought that her eyes had been covered and she could not see Durai. Her weeping seemed useful only for wetting the cloth over her eyes—not for wetting the old man's heart.

“Darling! Darling!” was all she murmured softly.

The lover lay unconscious, unable to open his eyes; the beloved suffered bound, unable to see him; between the two stood the old man. Moonlight lay lightly across his face. Tears rolled down his wrinkled cheeks and gathered there.

The man who had sent words of calm, peace and restraint toward the herds of clouds grazing in the sky had suddenly turned into a terrifying man—and that transformation too had vanished at once, and now he stood between the two people he had harmed and wept. Why? The drops rolling like mercury along the furrows of his shrunken cheeks fell upon his thumbai-flower beard and recalled drops of dew sleeping upon a morning<!-- source join: scan 32 → 33 --> shoot. With a long nose, deep-set eyes and quiet movements, why had this old man suddenly taken on a savage nature—why, the very next moment, did he stand in the same place and cry? Perhaps even he did not know the answers. That was what his face seemed to say. Without wiping the flowing tears, the old man looked at them.

<!-- source: scan 33 -->

Because he had used his upper cloth to bind Parimalam's hands and eyes, the cold wind now struck his bare upper body directly. His ribs showed so clearly that they could be counted. There was some knot at the upper end of the dhoti tied around his waist; he had tucked it securely into his waist. A stout paper envelope also protruded slightly outside the dhoti. The old man carefully adjusted these things. He bent down and looked at the bound Parimalam and the unconscious Durai. As he bent, a tear fell from his eye onto Parimalam's foot. Startled, she moved her leg.

“Who?”

“What is this drop of water on my foot?”

“Where did it fall from?”

She uttered a single word that contained all these questions—yes, the word “Darling.” There was no answer. The old voice rose, choking with emotion.

<!-- source: scan 33 → 34 -->

“Dear ones who have done me no wrong! Children! Listen, an old thing is speaking!

A pitiable old thing is crying out; turn your ears toward it!

Jewel who cannot open your eyes! When your beloved regains consciousness, tell him too what I tell you.... How many times have I yearned for my death-tears to help the tree of goodness grow lush! But now I realise that the tears of my life have tormented and hurt you. Forgive me. Forgive me as you would forgive your father!

For so many days I have suffered with the desire to do one good deed—a good that no one else can do. In the garden of human life, where selfishness and deceit, gambling and jealousy have become fences, are flowers of goodness forbidden to bloom? Is that the harsh law decreed by the one called God? So I am gathering up the good deed I have meant to do and crossing the sea. Do not ask me what that good deed is. Who are you, who am I—how can I dare tell you? You must not even see me—I think that would be terribly dangerous!

So I will not tell you the truth about that good deed. Even if that<!-- source join: scan 34 → 35 --> truth dies, I do not care. I will not allow myself to be destroyed in vain and that truth to fall into the hands of scoundrels. That is why I am fleeing this country for another. If I find a man whose mind is like mine, I shall entrust that truth to him and then embrace death. I shall anoint goodness with my death-tears! Somehow you came across the path of the great effort I have undertaken. You did not come deliberately—I know that! Yet for my own protection I became your enemy. I am going now; forgive me! Please forget what happened here. Think that some toothless old fellow opened his mouth and babbled lies and nonsense, and go on with your affair of love! There—that young man is handsome—he seems a good man too. But he has fallen into the net of love—and I cannot stand lovers! How can I entrust this great task to them? No, no! I am going—lovers born to live! Imagine that Maharaja Sarabhoji built this pleasure pavilion for you and enjoy yourselves! Why do you need to know why he built it?”

<!-- source: scan 35 → 36 -->

While he rambled on in this fashion, Durai regained consciousness halfway through the speech. Yet, eager to hear it all, he continued to lie as though still unconscious. But when the old man began, “Do you know why Sarabhoji built this?” Durai's lips moved as he almost asked, “Why?” He immediately suppressed the word and gave a small groan to cover himself. That was enough. The old man decided he should not remain there any longer and hurried down. Once Durai knew he had descended, he immediately rose, set his head right, and began untying Parimalam. Parimalam, who had been listening to the old man's words in wonder, too received new life when her beloved's hand touched her. It was as though the fantasy of dying and receiving a new birth had become true for them. Durai freed Parimalam's bound hands, lifted one and kissed it. Parimalam the beauty received that sweet kiss as though it were a jackfruit segment washed in honey. Her eyes, which had lain bound, received kisses too. Parimalam blessed the old man who had blindfolded her.

It did not end with kisses. Durai sprang up and looked down through one of Manora's windows. Parimalam looked too. The old man went and stood by the shore. The men who had earlier been coming toward Manora now surrounded him.

“These are not the rough men who chased us!” Durai said with a great breath of relief.

<!-- source: scan 36 → 37 -->

“Did you hear everything the old man said, darling?”

“I heard more than half. I understood it—and did not understand it.”

“I heard all of it, and I feel the same!”

“Wait, wait! Let's watch this.”

Standing by the shore, the old man was speaking to the men who had come. About two miles out from land a small light appeared and disappeared. It looked as though someone had struck a match and thrown it upward. Yes—that was exactly what it was.

One of the men on shore also struck a match and threw it upward in the same way.

“What is this?” asked Parimalam.

“This is what they call a clandestine boat! Watch—the boat will come to shore by the signal of the match-light,” said Durai.

They stood watching the boat. The old man kept moving about here and there.

“It looks as though dawn is getting near, darling. How am I going to get home?”

“Wait a little. Let's see this. If we go down now, that old man will throw us straight into the sea!”

Parimalam pressed her face into Durai's shoulder and watched the shore.

<!-- source: scan 37 → 38 -->

The boat came near the shore and stopped. All the men ran to it. The old man struck a match, took the paper at his waist out once and read it. On the front of the envelope, worn letters could be seen: **Treasure**. He threw away the match, tucked the envelope securely back in, checked the small knot at the end of his dhoti, and went to the boat. The boatman gently helped him aboard. The other men hurriedly loaded many bundles into the boat; several bundles from the boat were also rapidly unloaded onto shore. Then the boat moved away—its voyage toward the sea began again.

Parimalam and Durai stood watching the sight.

He had looked like a saint. Then he had turned terrifying. After that he said this was the guise he had assumed to do good. At last he had even boarded a clandestine boat. Who was he?

These thoughts left the lovers silent for a while. When the boat moved some distance and began to disappear into the darkness, Parimalam returned to the problem of getting home.

“What a night this has been. We went to the temple. There a man like a kombaeri-mookkan snake came. We came to the tower—and met a strange man. Don't unexpected obstacles always come when no one<!-- source join: scan 38 → 39 --> expects them!” Durai teased, threading Parimalam's fingers through his own.

<!-- source: scan 39 -->

“Come, darling, let's go!” she urged.

“I mustn't come now, Parimala! If both of us go, that man from the temple will identify us. So you go alone. Go without fear—dawn is coming!”

“That's exactly what frightens me—if day breaks, how will I enter the village? What will happen at home?”

“Nothing will happen at home, Parimalam! If your parents flare up, tell them the story I taught you with all your cleverness. If even that is not enough, if Appa and Amma pour out their anger and everything comes into the open—then begin at once: ‘What is wrong, Appa, in my going to seek my lover? Look, you have hung up a picture of Valli's bridegroom—isn't that Murugan the lover who sought Valli in the millet field?’ Ask them that. But don't start giving them examples of love incidents from Sangam literature! If you say Sangam literature, your parents will think Sangam is the name of some village.”

“All right! All right! Let whatever comes, come. I'm leaving—when will we meet?”

<!-- source: scan 39 → 40 -->

“We will meet, my dear—whether at the temple or after climbing the tower, from now on we must meet somewhere those two men cannot see us!”

“Tell me. It's getting late!”

“At the same temple—at the same time—next Friday. Come, let's go!”
