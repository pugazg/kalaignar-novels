## 2

He embraced the frightened Parimalam. Stroking her beautiful hair, which curled like the rim of an anna coin, he hid himself behind the field ridge and concealed her with him so that no one would see her. The two crawled on their bodies toward a small sluice there. Her heart beat pada-pada. Even when his hand touched her, she started violently, trembling in fear that perhaps they had already caught her.

<!-- source: scan 22 → 23 -->

Slowly they entered the sluice. The men approaching from both directions came together above them.

“What, didn't you find them?”

“No—they've flown off somewhere!”

“You think we're going to let them go without catching them tonight?”

“He's a hot-tempered man—he'll kill us!”

They stood right on top of the sluice in which the lovers were hiding and talked. The two inside were afraid even to breathe. Trembling with her body pressed against his, Parimalam turned her eyes aside. Ayyo! A snake stood there with its hood spread. Fortunately he too turned that way at the same instant and saw it. Fearing that she might scream, he immediately covered her mouth. Though in the urgency of the moment he did it boldly, his eyes stayed fixed on the snake. The rough men standing above the sluice and talking about them now barely registered in their ears. All their attention was on that snake. They had become so frightened that even the slightest movement seemed dangerous—it might strike!

“Let's go look somewhere else. But one thing: both of them must be Marungappallam people. So keep watch all along the road to Marungappallam! Sakkili and I

<!-- source: scan 23 → 24 -->

will go this way—you go that way!”

Knowing that the men above the sluice had spoken like this and gone away gave them a little relief. But what of the snake in front of them? To escape it, they moved backward. A wind began to blow. To the two who had lain as though trapped inside the sluice, even that wind brought some relief. The cool, jil-jil breeze grew a little stronger. Crawling backward, they kept their eyes on the snake. What a wonder—the snake's head alone suddenly flew toward them. It struck Parimalam's face and fell. She screamed aloud.

He stared at the head. Only then did he discover that what had flown at them with its hood spread was not a snake's head at all, but a leaf of the poovarasu tree carried by the wind. The truth—that a poovarasu leaf caught on a pirandai vine had looked like a hooded snake—became, at that painful moment, a tiny comic incident for them.

They looked around. The rough men could be seen moving along the road to Marungappallam.

“How are we ever going to get back to the village, darling? They're all over the roads!”

“We mustn't go into the village now, Parimalam—come! If we walk a little farther, we'll reach the shore.

<!-- source: scan 24 → 25 -->

If we hide near Manora at Saluva Nayakkan Pattinam, no one will see us. Somehow get through tonight, and in the morning you can go home.”

“In the morning? Ayyo—what am I supposed to tell Appa and Amma?”

“Are your father and mother educated?”

“No—what about it?”

“We must make use of their superstition.”

“Explain it so I can understand, Durai!”

“I'll tell you. Say that you were asleep at home during the night; that in your dream a goddess came and held some medicine to your nose; that however hard you tried to wake from the dream you could not; that you could not even make a sound; that after that you lost consciousness and knew nothing of what happened; that at dawn you woke and found yourself lying beside a sluice; and that you immediately got up and ran home. Your father and mother will believe you!”

“Long live superstition!” laughed the beauty, rubbing her cheek against his.

“All right, Parimalam—come, come! We must leave before their eyes turn this way—”

<!-- source: scan 25 → 26 -->

“Durai! It is true that the path of love is rough and stony!”

“Every path is like that! Doesn't the very word path come from affliction?”

Durai and Parimalam began walking from cover to cover. The silence of the night was broken now and then by the sounds of frogs. The moonlight too grew dim and bright by turns beneath the embrace of clouds. They reached the shore near Saluva Nayakkan Pattinam. Because a cold wind was blowing, Parimalam walked leaning against Durai's shoulders. She did not even have her upper garment to keep off a little of the cold. Durai took his own cloth and wrapped it around her. She gave him a shy smile as a gift and followed.

As though comparing Manora's majesty with the majesty of her lover, her beautiful eyes moved back and forth between the tower and him. But he was watching something with intense concentration. His pace slowed. He stopped.

“What is it, darling?”

“Nothing—look there!”

They both looked. A figure stood on a large mound beside Manora. As though to help them see the figure completely,

<!-- source: scan 26 → 27 -->

the moon freed itself from the clouds. The aged man, bald-headed and with a grown beard, stood on the mound and raised a hand toward the sky. The sight of that frail elder stretching his trembling arm heavenward—especially on that night—seemed to possess a kind of sanctity.

Durai and Parimalam stood without taking their eyes off him.

“Who is that, darling?”

“An old man!”

“Is it really a man? Or is it like that poovarasu-leaf snake?”

“Wait. Let's watch—”

Without turning his gaze from the sky, the elder spoke. It was not speech—it sounded like a prayer.

“O herds of clouds grazing in the sky! Do you think your life will last a hundred years? Do not forget that the cold wind is your Yama! Clouds! Your death-tears help the land to live. Sometimes they help destroy it too! Yet long live your death-tears! I too yearn for my death-tears to help someone live. Creatures of the clouds, give the power of your death-tears to the death-tears of this poor human being—hmm—quickly! Before that cold wind blows! At once! At once!”

<!-- source: scan 27 → 28 -->

Hearing the elder's words, Parimalam spoke to Durai.

“What is he lamenting about, darling? What are ‘death-tears’?”

“Death-tears! The clouds' death-tears are rain! Parimalam, wait, wait—let's watch a little longer!”

The old man, whose beard was like the fibrous tuft left on a mango sucked clean by a child, finished his prayer and came down from the mound. Durai told Parimalam to remain there and followed the old man. The elder entered Manora and reached the first storey by its ruined steps. Durai followed without making a sound. A ladder made from a palmyra trunk led from the first storey to the next. It was not easy to see. Moonlight poked its head into the interior. Ah, how clearly the elder's face could be seen in that light. The eagle of age had scratched all across it with sharp claws. A face full of wrinkles, but with blazing eyes. Durai watched him with fascination. The elder climbed upward by the ladder. Durai hid in a corner and watched. Someone placed a hand on his back and pressed. He started. It was no one else—it was Parimalam.

“What, darling?” she asked in a faint voice.

<!-- source: scan 28 → 29 -->

“Parimalam—you stay here. I'll go up, see what he's doing, and come back.”

Saying this, Durai climbed. Parimalam, understanding nothing, stood looking only at the sea. Her heart too had become like the surging ocean. Durai kept following the elder upward. One, two—three! The old man reached the seventh gallery. Durai stopped on the sixth and watched above. Complete silence. Nothing seemed to be happening. Through the gallery window he could see the shore. He looked—and, yes, he truly began to tremble. The rough men who had followed them were coming toward Manora. Yes—it was them—he knew for certain. But Parimalam stood in another direction on Manora's first level, looking at the sea. Durai did not know what to do.

The old man at the summit! The maiden on the lower level! Yes—the maiden of love. He had to save her. The old man who had gone to the top could go no farther. He could deal with him later somehow. Thinking that he must first save Parimalam, Durai turned downward. Just then he heard a sound above.

“Death-tears!
Death-tears!”

<!-- source: scan 29 → 30 -->

That was the cry. Durai looked upward in surprise.

The elder's voice rang without a tremor: “O herds of clouds grazing in the sky! Do you think your life will last a hundred years? Do not forget that the cold wind is your Yama!”

Durai heard half the sentences of the earlier prayer—

Before he could hear more, a cry of alarm—“Darling!”—tore through the silence of the night from the ground floor. Durai started and turned.
