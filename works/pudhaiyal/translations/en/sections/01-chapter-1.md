# Treasure

## 1

Velliyambalam was walking about inside his garden. His eyes kept looking all around. The strong belches that escaped him again and again announced that he had only just finished his evening meal. It was quite clear that his eyes were roaming toward the open garden gate, expecting someone. Dark-skinned, imposing in appearance and possessed of fearsome moustaches, Velliyambalam also carried a stout staff in his hand—what more need be said? The fine gold chain around his neck and the amulet hanging from it gleamed in the dim moonlight. Clearing his throat repeatedly, lifting his head like a tiger and rolling his eyes as he prowled about, Velliyambalam was a big man in the Marungappallam region. Not, one might say, because of wealth; because of manpower! If word came that Ambalam was approaching, that was enough: the people of the region embraced their fear and slipped away, each to a different corner. Nothing good or bad could happen in the village without Ambalam being involved. If there was some special occasion in anyone's house, he would turn himself into the man of the occasion and take charge of the arrangements. Whether he did all this out of affection for them,

<!-- source: scan 13 → 14 -->

or because he wanted his figure and his name to remain stamped in people's minds, or because he had the confidence that he could pocket as much money as he pleased from such occasions and there would be no one to question him—these were matters the people of the area had discussed and then abandoned after deciding for themselves that even speaking about them was work that could cost a life. If anyone so much as raised his head a little and looked at him while he went down the street, Ambalam would immediately spring at him in fury, strike him once, and only then move on. In a region inhabited by lives too frightened even to think of the phrase “jungle king's court,” whatever Ambalam laid down was the law. Yet a fear had begun to take shape in Ambalam's own mind; that much was somehow true. How long could he keep frightening everyone and live this way? What would he do if, one day, they all united and began to resist? Doubts like these began to grow. This fear and uncertainty gave Velliyambalam a little time for thought, but they did not obstruct any of his actions. And precisely because they did not obstruct him, he had resolved to dig around the Siva temple of Marungappallam and somehow take possession of those heaps of gold. The day fixed for carrying out that task was the very day on which he was now pacing. And by “the day fixed,” it was not a day he himself had fixed;

<!-- source: scan 14 → 15 -->

it was the day fixed by an astrologer. Ambalam had boundless faith in omens, astrology and such things. That was why, even for this work that had to be done in secrecy, he had asked that the almanac be consulted. The Ayyar who examined the almanac knew Ambalam's nature well. He understood that if he predicted success in the search and success then failed to come, his own life might be in danger. So—Venus's period is present; Saturn's aspect is also faintly there; therefore victory must be stated like a cat on a wall. Perhaps victory may indeed come—thus, with great clarity, he delivered his astrology and selected a day.

In order to dig at many places at once and somehow discover where the hoard of gold lay, Ambalam had arranged for more than fifty men. He had been waiting at the garden gate for them to arrive. They arrived.

“Why the hell did you take this long?”

They stood silent. Even though there was a proper reason for the delay, fear that explaining it would amount to talking back silenced them.

“Hey, Samban—Sakkili—Muniya—all you fellows had better watch the work properly tonight. Otherwise I'll skin you. Yes.”

Saying this, Velliyambalam walked in front. The others followed. Each of them

<!-- source: scan 15 → 16 -->

carried tools such as spades and crowbars. Ambalam crossed the canal behind the garden and went ahead along the single narrow field-ridge there, the others following behind. No one in the village saw the procession. Once it was eight at night, did that not mean villages had gone quiet? The dry music of night birds and the kor-kor chorus sung by frogs sitting over pools of standing water sounded like music composed especially for Velliyambalam's procession. They crossed many fields and finally came to the Siva temple. The moon was struggling to emerge after hiding behind clouds, and the old “torch light” in Ambalam's hand helped them see the path clearly. Leaving the men outside the temple, Ambalam alone entered. Though he had pored over the letters carved on the stone there any number of times, he wanted to read them once again now. He shone the torch upon the letters.

“Between the one who rises and the one who bows,
In the shadow of the crow's beak, even if found, in the eyes of the Kammalar......”

Ambalam read further—the last line too, though it was worn and on the verge of disappearing.

“Seventy crores of pure gold.” Do you know what a pounding those final words created in Ambalam's heart?<!-- source join: scan 16 → 17 --> The letters glittering in the torchlight seemed to his eyes like a shower of diamonds. Once more he explained their meaning to himself.

“‘The one who rises’ means the sun—the one who bows means—the deity in this temple—between the sun and this deity—then there cannot be pure gold in the sea stretching from sunrise all the way to Saluva Nayakkan Pattinam—so the treasure must lie somewhere between Saluva Nayakkan Pattinam and this temple—we already investigated in the morning what ‘in the shadow of the crow's beak’ means too—I marked the shadow of the beak of the crow that sat this morning on that odhiya tree over there; that mark must surely be correct—somehow—everything is Siva's doing—God! Maheswara of Marungappallam, if I find the pure gold I seek, if I get hold of the gold bars, I shall have a silver bull-vehicle made for you”—

Dreaming such dreams of desire, he turned to come out of the temple. As he turned, some sound caught his attention. He stood motionless and listened.

An ich-ich sound! He understood what sort of sound it was. He listened again.

Words heavy with intoxication struck his ears.

<!-- source: scan 17 → 18 -->

“Darling!”

“My dear!”

“My king!”

“What is it, my queen!”

Velliyambalam understood nothing. He burned with the desire to see who the owners of those voices were. At the same time he feared they might somehow interfere with his own task. Who were they? Without his knowing it, his thick lips moved in this way:

“Could it perhaps be Parvati and Parameswara themselves—nonsense! Was there no room in Kailasa that they had to come here......but why shouldn't they come......wouldn't they come......even if it were them, they wouldn't call each other king and queen......”

Velliyambalam stood as the sole representative of suspicion, struggling even to hold back his heavy breathing.

I can hide and see who they are—nonsense! I mustn't go in this state! Mustn't! he advised himself. A man who had come seeking gold surrendered his mind to the breeze-like language of fledglings seeking pleasure.

Again the lute trembled.

‘You won't forget, will you, my dear!’ No answer came—only an ‘ich’ sound floated through the air and entered his ear.

<!-- source: scan 18 → 19 -->

Velliyambalam could not remain still. He decided that only if he brought this problem quickly to an end could his gold-digging proceed without delay. He thought of calling all his men, making them shout and driving the pair away. He took one step to call the men. A small pada-pada sound—Ambalam started and looked. Nothing. Another bat had approached one hanging upside down; that was all. Pressing a hand to his pounding heart, he began walking again.

The sound of conversation came a little louder. Ambalam stopped instead of continuing.

“Darling—forget everything that happened—at least from now on......”

That was all that reached his ear; he could not hear properly what followed. Thinking that the only thing left was to bring the men and create a commotion, Ambalam hurried out. But if he brought the men and caused an uproar—if something untoward happened and the village gathered—then his plan to dig out the gold might fail. If he missed the day the astrologer had fixed, he might never get another day like this. Thinking in this way, he decided instead to go himself and frighten them away, and returned once more into the temple.

<!-- source: scan 19 → 20 -->

The ‘ich’ sound came again and again. Ambalam hesitated for a moment. A man's voice was heard.

“Parimalam! Look, there it is, that mango tree—that spot under the tree is where the gold was buried.”

The instant those words fell on Velliyambalam's ears, he rushed in without the slightest hesitation. The two seemed to have been behind a small wall at the rear of the temple. The moment Ambalam burst in, they both trembled violently. Crying “Darling,” the beautiful woman clung to him and entwined herself around him. The embrace lasted only the blink of an eye. By then Ambalam had shouted, “Under which mango tree is the gold buried, you! Tell me!” and charged toward them. Seeing him, the two doves began to run. Velliyambalam tried to catch the man in a single leap. As he sprang, he tripped over a stone, fell, and badly struck his knee. At once he began calling his men. When they came running, he shouted, “Hey Sakkili, Samban! Two people are running that way—a man and a woman. Don't let them go. Catch them—run!” The men began running. “Hey Muniya! You and a few others stand on our village road and on the field ridges too—that fellow and the girl have to enter the village somehow—we can catch them for sure!” he ordered Muniya. All the others ran eastward.

<!-- source: scan 20 → 21 -->

The love-doves too flew eastward. There were so many paths in the village, so many fields, so many ridges—how could anyone discover which way they had run? With his knee badly hurt, Velliyambalam rose limping. The glass of his torch had broken in the force of the fall, but it still gave a little light. With its help he looked all around. The woman's upper garment lay on the ground. A good sandal lay there too. In his haste, one of them must have run off wearing only one of a pair of sandals, Ambalam told himself. He shone the light some distance around and searched. The other sandal was nowhere to be seen. Taking the upper garment and sandal in his hands, he walked on, limping.

“He said the gold was buried under a mango tree—which mango tree? Of so many mango trees, which one am I to find? Hmm! The crow's-beak shadow fell under the odhiya tree! He says mango tree—I don't understand anything—God!” Ambalam rambled as he moved on. His men, meanwhile, roamed like hunting dogs on a scent, searching for those two creatures of love who had flown eastward.

<!-- source: scan 21 → 22, chapter-1 portion -->

Hiding along the inner side of the canal, crouching and peering from bushes, coping with thorns that stabbed their legs and bodies and drew blood, the two kept running in a direction with no fixed destination. They looked back. Ten men could be seen coming along their path. Keeping hidden behind a field ridge, they looked ahead. Four or five men were approaching from that direction too.

“That's it, we're caught, darling,” she said, clinging tightly to him with a trembling hand.

“Don't be afraid, Parimalam!” he said, stroking her curling hair.
