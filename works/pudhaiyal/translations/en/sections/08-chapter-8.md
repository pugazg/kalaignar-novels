# 8

At the very time Parimalam was being carried away, a new danger arose for old Mayandi's boat travelling in mid-sea. A little after darkness fell, the men hurried, on Ayyappan's orders, to drive the boat toward shore. But the wind was not favourable that day either. Bending their bodies and pouring sweat, the men laboured to propel the boat. Ayyappan alone shouted confidently, “Only ten miles more! Once we can see the light<!-- source join: scan 75 → 76 -->house at Ooraththurai, then we'll really run—reach Valvettithurai and jump straight ashore—hmm—move it faster!”

<!-- source: scan 76 -->

“The wind isn't favourable,” Mayandi said.

“If it isn't favourable, we'll have to make it unfavourable,” Ayyappan mocked.

“Why are you talking without meaning, appa?”

“Ayyappan is a man without meaning!”

“Yes, yes—that is true!”

“What is true! Hey, old man! You know my story, don't you?”

Suspicion and dread arose in old Mayandi. He understood why Ayyappan, who had behaved toward him with respect and concern, had suddenly changed. He also realised that Ayyappan had set his sights on the paper envelope and the small knot kept safely in his lap.

Why had he unnecessarily told Ayyappan that old story of the blacksmith's family? Why had he not stopped with mentioning it, but given every detail to this brute? He reproached himself. At first he had thought that if he somehow protected his secret from Ayyappan until they reached Ceylon, there would be nothing to worry about afterward. Now, looking at Ayyappan's behaviour and tone, he feared the man might not leave him alone even after they reached Ceylon.

<!-- source: scan 77 -->

With great difficulty the men kept turning the wind-tossed boat in the direction it had to travel. But old Mayandi struggled to control the waves in his own mind. Able to see, little by little in the moonlight, the changes in Mayandi's face, Ayyappan bit his lip and prepared himself to do something serious. The men were all busily engaged in their tasks. Ayyappan alone leaned against the mast in the middle of the boat and kept watching Mayandi. When the thought comes that a man standing before us intends to seize something we possess, our hand naturally touches that object without our awareness and tries to guard it. In the same way Mayandi kept touching his lap again and again.

“Oi, Mayandi!” Ayyappan shouted terribly.

The old man spoke calmly. “Ayyappa! Why have you suddenly changed like this? Think how we have all dealt with one another—what has happened to you now?”

“You cheat the very man who treated you as a friend, ayya? I thought, let it go, and brought you aboard my boat, and you show your cheating tricks to us ourselves!”

“How did I cheat you, appa?”

<!-- source: scan 77 → 78 -->

“If you tell me what's in your lap, will your head burst? What happens if you say it?”

“That is none of your business; that is why I didn't tell you!”

“Chi! Lying old rogue! Grab him!” Ayyappan sprang at the elder. Another man behind Mayandi obeyed Ayyappan, leapt on him and pulled his arms behind his back. The old man struggled. How could that frail elder in mid-sea fight all of them? Yet rage and rough courage rose in him and he kicked Ayyappan with all the strength in his leg. Ayyappan managed not to fall. Mayandi broke free from the man holding his hands and sprang to another side of the boat. “Hey, don't let him go!” Ayyappan screamed. Men working at the opposite corner too closed around him. With men running here and there and Mayandi leaping in fury, the boat began rocking violently. It floundered so badly that one might think it would capsize. Mayandi concluded that he could do nothing more. Only by falling into the sea could his purpose be fulfilled; otherwise the objects he had protected for many days would fall into the hands of these villains. So when the men blocked him ahead, he made one great leap toward the sea. But Ayyappan would not let him fall in; he caught and held him.

<!-- source: scan 79 -->

With everyone surrounding him, the old man was trapped helplessly. Ayyappan opened his lap-cloth and took the paper envelope, then untied the knot in the dhoti. Inside was a small stone; he took that too. Wanting to see what was in the envelope, he told the man beside him to light a match. In the matchlight one could clearly see Mayandi weeping. Ayyappan spent the whole burning match simply enjoying the sight of the old man's face.

“One paper envelope—one stone! Why does this old man cry like this at losing them?” The question confused Ayyappan deeply. He had another match struck and in that light opened the envelope. Inside was a small copper plate marked with lines running crosswise and lengthwise—a circle like a well—a sign like a tree—and several other shapes. Ayyappan remembered the missing copper plate and little stone from the story the elder had told. Mayandi's concluding claim—that if the small stone were taken to the place shown on the copper plate and set down, it would reveal where the treasure lay—now filled Ayyappan with delight. What a magnificent dream he saw in that blink of happiness! He takes the stone to the place indicated on the copper plate and sets it on the ground. The stone is drawn like a magnet and runs.

<!-- source: scan 80 -->

He digs where it stops. He finds the kulisakkal that turns iron into gold, and pot upon pot of gold particles—then? Ayyappan lies on a diamond-studded bed surrounded by his kept women. Many ships bearing the name “Ayyappa” carry passengers and import-export goods across the oceans spread over the whole world.

Such a blissful dream appeared for an instant and vanished. Ayyappan looked back and forth between the copper plate and the small stone. Then one of the men aboard cried, “Ayyo! Ayyo! Danger!” Everyone turned in shock. A flash of light struck the boat. “God!” Ayyappan screamed. Because the beam struck his eyes, for several seconds he could not see properly.

“Police! It's the police!!” one man shouted.

“Finished! Finished!!” another cried.

“Turn the boat and let it run fast with the wind, you thick-headed fellows!” Ayyappan shouted in his grotesque voice.

“They've come close,” said one man, his thighs trembling.

“Chi! They'll come close enough to ram us—complete fool! Push quickly—hmm, change and tie the sail, you wandering bastard! Rice-fed dogs have no quickness at all!” Ayyappan barked.

<!-- source: scan 81 -->

The boat turned with the wind and ran fast. The onion sacks were thrown one by one into the sea, though they could not get all of them overboard. Was it easy to lift every sack carefully and throw it into the sea? Ayyappan's body trembled. To old Mayandi it seemed the whole world was spinning. He said nothing. He wept, thinking that just as the onion sacks were going uselessly into the sea, his secret objects too were about to become useless to everyone. The boat was nearly emptied. Ayyappan drew a relieved breath, thinking they could now escape by claiming, “We came to fish.” However fast his boat went, it could not escape the police boat pursuing behind. “Pat, pat”—gunshots began to sound. Ayyappan's men trembled and stood motionless. The boat alone continued with the wind—that is, back toward the direction of Manora. Without the men's effort, how long could it travel on wind alone? The police boat came very near. All the men trembled. Without showing his own fear, Ayyappan told them, “Don't be afraid. Don't blurt anything to the police. All of you say the same thing—that we came to fish!”

<!-- source: scan 81 → 82 -->

Then old Mayandi roared, “Ayyappa! Be careful. I'm going to tell them we came to conduct clandestine-boat trade.”

“What, old man?”

“Yes—give me that copper plate and stone properly, or I myself will expose every one of you,” Mayandi growled.

“What did you say—you'll expose us? Old dog!—I'll make you float alive in the sea, watch!—” Ayyappan shouted and began lifting him to throw him overboard. At that moment a police searchlight struck Ayyappan's eyes like lightning and made it impossible to open them. At the same time several shots burst in rapid succession.

Shocked, Ayyappan dropped the elder back inside the boat and returned the copper plate and stone to him. “Old man! Old man! Don't expose us!” he begged.

Mayandi secured the small stone and copper plate inside his garment and suddenly jumped from the boat into the sea. Rather than be searched by the police and have the stone and plate confiscated, he had fallen upon the decision that he and those objects should disappear and perish in the sea. His falling into the sea and the police taking possession of Ayyappan's boat happened at almost exactly the same moment.

<!-- source: scan 83 -->

Among the police officers was an imposing young officer with a handsome young beard. Seeing old Mayandi jump into the sea, he jumped after him. The bearded officer struggled to swim through the raging, roaring waves. For a while there was only stillness at the place where both had jumped. It seemed necessary to conclude that neither would return. The police put handcuffs on everyone, including Ayyappan. Until then neither of the men in the sea could be seen. A large torch from the police boat was shone toward the water. In its circular beam only a tuft of beard floated. None of Ayyappan's group saw it; only the police did. They immediately turned the light elsewhere. Carrying the old man's body, the police officer came toward the boat. All the policemen pursed their lips; their eyes conveyed that the elder was finished. The officer placed Mayandi's body in the boat. He realised that his own young beard had gone into the sea. The old man did not open his eyes. Had he opened them and looked, he would have recognised that this police officer was Durai, the man he had met the previous night.
