# 47

“I had heard of Ambalam only as a big man in the village—a hot-tempered man—a brave fellow who would not hesitate even to commit murder. But only now do I understand that Ambalam is a great master strategist!” old Mayandi said with a faint smile. That praise made Ambalam's head swell a little more.

“If the whole secret of the treasure had been told to that Durai fellow, it would have been like pouring water onto barren ground; like throwing a diamond onto a rubbish heap. Luckily, the secret reached the very place it was meant to reach. If the two of us had not been brought together by God's grace, how could we possibly have obtained the treasure with such cleverness?”

Mayandi went on praising Ambalam beyond measure. Though Ambalam was intoxicated by the flattery, he had already begun thinking about how he might cheat Mayandi without giving him even the share due to him. There was only one way available to him—to destroy the man himself! Very well; let the work be completed first. Afterwards this curry leaf could be crushed and thrown away, he decided, and began attending to the arrangements for the marriage of Parimala and Durai.

The men needed to put up a large wedding pavilion had gathered. In keeping with the plan to serve everyone an extravagant meal, cooks capable of preparing a full six-flavoured feast had been summoned. The whole village was talking about the wedding at Velliyambalam's house. Though Thandavan was furious, he was forced to remain like a tiger inside a cage. He had no courage to do anything openly against Velliyambalam. The lesson everyone in the village knew was that anyone who valued his life must not oppose Ambalam. So the number of drums beating praise even for Ambalam's wrongs kept increasing. What could Thandavan do in such a situation? Besides, what Ambalam was doing appeared to be a good deed. He was arranging a love marriage. People merely mocked the father for foolishly opposing such a thing.

Parimala's mother alone was overwhelmed by a longing somehow to see her daughter and son-in-law seated in the wedding chamber and rejoice.

After all, was she not a mother? Her only daughter! She had guarded her like her very life. At a time when she had been in tears and grief believing that the girl had disappeared, the mere news that she was alive had poured into her heart like sweet honey. A festival day had now arrived in the life of that beloved daughter—would a mother not long to see such a day to her heart's content? She had argued with Thandavan about it and lost. Even so, she had been making plans somehow to run there at least at the time of the wedding, see it, and come back.

The wedding day arrived. Hearing that Durai was to be married, his friends too came and gathered, led by Anandan. Ambalam received them all and arranged for them to stay.

Old Mayandi neither spoke to Anandan nor wished even to look at him.

Seeing the old man, Anandan asked, “What is it, Grandpa? Are you well?”

Mayandi replied, “Who are you, son? I don't seem to know you!” and walked away.

At once Anandan turned to Ambalam and growled, “Did you see that? The old man says he doesn't know me! Looks as though once people get into high places their eyes stop seeing. Let it be, let it be. I'll question this old man later.”

Mayandi took Ambalam aside and warned him, “All these boys are Durai's friends! We must keep a careful watch on them. Some danger may come from them!” Ambalam appointed one of his thugs to keep watch over Anandan's group and went back to attending to his own affairs.

People came in crowds even from other places to attend the wedding at Velliyambalam's house. In Marungappallam, apart from Thandavan's house, there was no cooking going on in anyone else's home. Everyone had a feast at the wedding house, after all! Wedding in the morning, procession in the evening, nuptial hour at night—and, to mark the marriage, a street play! While the whole village sat enjoying the street play, the couple would be murdered here! Their blood would be sprinkled into the well! The treasure would burst forth! In this manner Ambalam laid out the wedding programme with precision.

Accordingly, at daybreak the wedding rites began according to shastric custom. The priest's mantras rang out. Drums, cymbals and band instruments thundered. Parimala was brought in dressed as a bride. Thinking that by that night she would instead be dressed as a corpse, she too came and sat on the wedding dais. Gold chains glittered around her neck. A stone-studded ottiyanam was fastened around her waist. Head ornaments such as the nagar and thiru-uruvappu had been placed on her. She sat there looking as though beauty itself had been made more beautiful. But before her eyes she could see only the vision of herself and her beloved Durai being hacked to pieces.

After a little while Raman brought Durai too. Dressed in costly silk garments and arrayed as a bridegroom, he sat beside Parimala. From a distance old Mayandi watched the bride and groom come and sit in the wedding chamber, then slipped away without letting them see him.

Durai and Parimala looked at one another with terror-filled eyes. She was distressed, wondering whether her beloved knew anything about the danger that was coming. Durai had become firmly convinced that they were trapped in some mysterious whirlpool and would have to endure some terrible outcome. Even from the wedding pavilion the two of them might have escaped, in full sight of the villagers—but who were standing all around them? Men with fierce moustaches and threatening eyes, holding gleaming knives! Who was going to ask why such a force was standing there? Even if anyone did ask, Ambalam simply answered that it was to prevent Thandavan from causing any harm to the couple. Thugs like mad dogs all around them—a cluster of priests before them croaking mantras like frogs—the villagers watching for entertainment—many people talking about Ambalam's concern for the lovers' marriage—and in the midst of them all, Parimala and Durai!

“Loud music! Loud music!” the ayyers shouted. The villagers showered turmeric-stained rice over the heads of the bride and groom, and Durai tied the thaali around Parimala's neck. Thaali—daanam—the frog-like croak of dharpaasooran—Durai liked none of it, but unable to do anything, he went through everything according to Ambalam's wishes like a mechanical doll that had been wound up. Parimala's heart lamented: what difference did one kind of marriage or another make to people who were going to die that night?

Two eyes watched the wedding ceremony through a gap in the woven leaves of the pavilion. They belonged to Parimala's mother. A new light was born in her eyes. Without even realizing it, she murmured, “They really are a well-matched pair.” Pouring out all the blessings that surged from her heart, the mother slipped away and ran home, hiding herself as she went.

The feast and the other celebrations continued. Early in the night came the wedding procession. The bride and groom were seated in an old car, which the thugs pushed along in procession. Even then Parimala and Durai were able only to look at one another anxiously; they had no opportunity to speak. Thinking that she was seeing for the last time the village in which she had been born and raised, she let her eyes travel all around it.

From inside the car she saw the place where she had first met and spoken to the young Durai when he was in the condition of an orphan. Tears welled up. The lotus pond where she had leapt and played—the side of the temple where she had lain embracing her lover—everything increased the weight on her heart. Durai kept looking often at Parimala and also at the thugs surrounding them. His only thought was whether there might be some way to escape.

Durai's friends, Anandan and the others, had joined the procession. One thug came along keeping watch on them. Raman had flown off to attend to the arrangements for the street play.

The procession ended. Night—a fish feast! After that, the street play! The whole village gathered there.

Durai and Parimala were sent separately to the bridal chamber. The moment Parimala saw Durai, she sprang towards him, embraced him and began sobbing, “Aththan! Aththan!” Tears filled Durai's eyes too.
