# 28

Here Parimalam stood alone on the riverbank, suffering. Back in Marungappallam, Dukkaram was suffering because he wanted to see this very Parimalam. He kept his eyes fixed on the road, waiting for Raman, who had gone as his messenger and still had not returned. He was delighted with himself that his love seemed to have won an unexpected victory.

Velliyambalam was a great man in the village—the people trembled merely at the sound of his name—such an invincible hero, such a rural notable. And if a man like that had become the chief messenger of his beloved, how could Dukkaram even describe his own luck and greatness? Velliyambalam had come all the way to Pattukkottai, searched him out, seated him in his house, fed him, treated him like royalty, gone as a messenger to Parimalam and returned after conveying the matter. Thinking again and again that no one else could show such concern for his love, Dukkaram heaved happy sighs.

Raman, who had gone to Parimalam with the message, still had not returned.

“What would Raman have told her? What would she have answered? I told him to say that, separated from her, I lie here without food or sustenance. What torment will she suffer when she hears that? Will she realise that I sent him with a lie? Hmm—who knows? Perhaps she has been starving for days because she cannot see me. Have her bangles slipped from her wrists? Did this Raman tell my Parimalam everything I asked him to say properly? I told him to ask where we could meet and when we could meet. What answer will she give? Oh God! You alone have taken whatever forms you wished and gone wherever you pleased! You gods have changed form—as stag and doe, as male elephant and female elephant—and deceived the eyes of the world while enjoying yourselves. In the same way, even if Parimalam and I cannot turn into elephants, can you not at least give us the power to become cats so that we can meet each other and be happy? If we changed like that, we could throw chilli powder into the eyes of the villagers and enjoy ourselves together. Can you not grant us such power for at least one day, even one hour, you wretched god!

“And why can I not see Raman returning? He can come only if Parimalam lets him leave quickly! Poor fellow—what all must she be asking him about me? There is a special joy when a lover hears others speak about his beloved, and when a beloved hears others speak about her lover! I have not even earned the merit Raman has earned. At least he will have seen my Parimalam's beautiful face and come back. I cannot even do that. Let Raman come; I will touch his feet and worship them. Are the feet that stepped into Parimalam's house ordinary feet?”

Muttering all this to himself, Dukkaram wandered back and forth. Before his madness could ripen any further, Raman fortunately arrived.

“Raman! Raman!” Dukkaram cried eagerly and ran to him.

“Did you see Parimalam? Did you see my dark-eyed beauty?”

“I saw Parimalam!”

“What did she say?”

“I told her, ‘Sir is lying there without food or water. Nothing will go down into his stomach.’”

“And what did she say to that?”

“She said you should take medicine for your digestion!”

“Ah, how much concern she has for me! Raman! You did tell her, didn't you, that the medicine isn't with the doctor, but with you?”

“Would I fail to tell her? I said I had the medicine. She told me, ‘Then give that itself to your master.’”

“With you? You fool! You should have said that the medicine capable of curing my illness is with Parimalam!”

“Oh, is that so? I didn't know that woman had studied to be a doctor. I'll tell her the next time I go.”

“Raman—how is Parimalam?”

“Very beautiful!”

“Leave that aside. What did she say about me? Where and when did she say we could meet?”

“That woman is aching to meet you!”

“Is that so? Ah, my very life! Inbavalli! Are you pining for me? What am I to do? How am I to meet you?”

“She gave a way to meet!”

“She did? She gave a way? Raman, tell me quickly what the way is!”

“She says the two of you can meet after the village goes quiet tonight!”

“Splendid! Splendid! Where, Raman?”

“You know there is a Siva temple in this village?”

“Yes—I saw it when I came.”

“She told you to come near that temple tonight!”

“Ah! Ah! Raman, what am I going to give you for bringing me this news? Don't worry. Once the love books I have written are printed, I will send you fifty copies free!”

“Yes, send them. I am suffering badly without a pillow!”

“So, Raman, tonight, after the village grows quiet, I go near the Siva temple—and my beloved will come there!”

“Yes!”

“Long live love! Long live lovers!!”

* * * *

Dukkaram waited longingly for night to come. Even the palm-jaggery coffee Raman made for him that evening brought him no sweetness. For dinner Raman cooked a curry with fine viral fish and field crabs, but Dukkaram was in no state to taste any of it. His heart beat “pad-pad.” Though he had seen Parimalam directly many times, the thought that this would be the first day they were going to speak freely left him unable to remain in one place. He watched with intense attention to see whether the village had gone quiet.

The sound of children from the settlement beating the tamukku drum and playing pierced his ears. From somewhere else came the voices of people gathered together singing Rathi-Manmathan lavani songs and making merry; even that sounded harsh to him.

Little by little the noises subsided and quiet settled over the village. Dukkaram set out toward the Siva temple to see his beloved.

“Will she come only after I get there? Or will she already be waiting before me? Oh! If she has been waiting, her legs must be aching. The first thing I must do when I arrive is massage her legs and relieve the pain. Ah-ah-ah! What shall I say when I see her? What will she say?”

<!-- source scan 252 / printed 248 -->

Thinking in this way, he reached the temple. As he had expected, Parimalam was nowhere to be seen. It was very dark. He opened his eyes wide and looked all around. No one. He cleared his throat softly. He called “Parimalam” in a low voice. There was no answer.

The rustling of the trees, the croaking of frogs and the noise of insects reminded him of a terrifying atmosphere. Once again he called, “Parimalam.” No answer. But it sounded as though someone had cleared their throat. He eagerly imagined it was her. No one came.

Again, softly, he called, “Parimalam!”

Someone laughed with a rattling laugh. There was no warmth in it. It was full of cruelty—a terrifying laugh. Dukkaram was jolted. Pressing his heart with his hands, he stood motionless like a tree.

“Caught at last?” a rough voice said.

“Who? Who is that?” the pitiable Dukkaram screamed, almost weeping.

“Who? It is I—the ghost!”

“A ghost? Ayyo, a ghost?” His arms and legs trembled like the strings of a veena that had been struck loose.

“A ghost indeed! I am the ghost that guards the treasure!”

“Ghost! Ghost!” Dukkaram screamed and began to run.

<!-- source scan 253 / printed 249 -->

“Don't run—stop! If you disobey the ghost's command, I will grind you to powder and eat you,” the voice thundered.

Dukkaram froze where he stood. Sweat poured from his entire body. Without knowing what he was doing, he babbled incoherently.
