# 16

“Until grandmother explained the details, I too believed that Appa was the one who had killed Amma. Only later did I learn what circumstances had led to Amma being murdered—and why she was murdered!

“The tea plantation on which Amma, Appa, grandmother and the others worked for wages and survived belonged to a white proprietor. His name was Kaakdurai—but the workers of that region privately called him ‘Dog Durai.’ They said the name suited him because of his character. But my grandmother's explanation was different. He was highly skilled at sniffing out women. Grandmother argued that this talent for sniffing was the most fitting reason for the title ‘Dog Durai.’ Whatever one may say, when grandmother described the acts of cruelty that took place on the tea plantation, I became convinced that it was entirely true.

<!-- source: scan 146 → 147 -->

“Countless women would rise before dawn, shivering in the cold, carry tea baskets on their backs and work plucking tea shoots. Protecting themselves from the roaming eyes of the white proprietors and from the treacherous designs of certain kanganis waiting to carry out those men's orders had become an everyday terrifying occurrence—a problem of life that had to be faced and overcome each day, grandmother would say with sorrow. Pitiful women became objects used to satisfy the appetites of proprietors, supervisors and kanganis, and then had to search for a way to satisfy the hunger of their own stomachs. When I think that the tea shoots sprouting from the blood-tears shed by those women become the tea that we savour, smacking our tongues and drinking before its heat has cooled, the heart cannot help but soften!—nowadays, perhaps the savage dance of animal lust cannot continue on tea plantations to the same extent!—proprietors, supervisors and kanganis are becoming people with human feeling—they have to change, because the workers' struggle for a sense of dignity has begun to raise its head. Better a one-span rope than a one-span belly, appa—we already grind this eight-span body down in labour until the bones show; we will not give room to the arrogance that demands we donate our honour as well—because this cry of feeling has risen from the camp of the poor, those degraded men who once strutted in the belief that they could do anything at any time and that there was no one to ask why have now begun, at least a little, to quiet down—
