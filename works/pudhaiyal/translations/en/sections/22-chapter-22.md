# 22

“What, Mahavishnu appeared? Really?” Parimalam asked eagerly.

“Really. I ran in the direction where he was standing. The more I ran, the farther away he seemed to move. I did not give up. Without caring what lay ahead or what it was, I ran straight toward him.” Parimalam could not suppress her laughter.

“This sounds like a new purana! Only now have I heard that Bhagavan came running when he saw a young man suffering from hunger.”

“This is nothing new, Parimalam! Have you not read that he appeared with conch and discus before Dhruva, whose life had been ruined by the cruelty of his stepmother? Have you not heard the story that the young Prahlada escaped many mortal dangers through Vishnu's grace? Does not the purana say that sixteen-year-old Markandeya defeated Yama at Thirukkadavur through divine grace?”

“I have heard the stories the puranas tell, and I have read them too. But I have never seen with my own eyes, nor heard from anyone who has seen, that my lord appeared before real-life ‘Dhruvas’ trapped in suffering like you and showered them with mercy.”

“I saw it with my own eyes. I saw Vishnumurthi grant refuge to Dhruva. If Vishnumurthi had not been there, could this Durai have remained alive all these years?”

“What are you saying?”

“Listen with a little patience, Parimalam! See how quietly and calmly the elder is listening to the story. You listen like that too!”

“Mm. Go on.”

“Did I run toward Vishnu? Even the faintness and exhaustion caused by hunger vanished. I ran that fast. I leapt toward Vishnu. Mahavishnu stood there, bearing conch and discus, dark as a blue rain-cloud. Another fellow like me stood facing him.”

“Who was that?”

“He was Dhruva!”

“Dhruva?”

“Parimalam! Do you think I am going to fool you by saying that I fell asleep in a hunger-faint and saw some mythological dream? No, Parimalam, no. This was certainly no dream. It happened before my eyes!”

“All right—go on.”

“I caught hold of Mahavishnu's feet. At once there was a tremendous noise—”

“A noise? What noise?”

“The people began shouting!”

“People? Where did they come from? Residents of Vaikuntha?”

“Listen, Parimalam! I heard the noise. I rubbed my eyes and tried to steady my mind a little.”

“In other words, you woke up!”

“No. I stared about without understanding anything. Dhruva cried ‘Ayyo!’ and ran away. Vishnu too stood dumbfounded. The crowd rushed toward me. Total commotion—confusion!”

“And then?”

“What else? A curtain fell between the crowd and me. Mahavishnu glared at me and asked, ‘Who are you, da?’ I opened my mouth to say something like, ‘Without understanding what was happening...’ Before I could, he seized me and dragged me away at great speed.”

“Where?”

“To the make-up room!”

“Oh! A play?” Parimalam laughed.

“No, Parimalam! A street performance!” said Durai.

“Mm—what happened after that?”

“What would happen? I fell at the feet of the real Vishnumurthi.”

“Are you creating another complication?”

“No. The real Vishnumurthi was the proprietor of that drama company. His play Dhruva had been arranged for the village festival. Earlier that night, weak from hunger, I had collapsed in exhaustion at the end of a street and fallen asleep. When I woke, the play was in progress—the scene where Mahavishnu appears before Dhruva. That was where our commotion happened! The ignorance of boyhood and the dizziness brought by hunger joined together and confused me. I mistook the actor dressed as Vishnu for the deity himself. I trampled through the assembled crowd, sprang onto the stage, drove Dhruva away, nearly pierced the heart of the actor playing Vishnu, and created all that uproar. Would they simply let me go? They took me to the proprietor, Vishnumurthi.

“His name was Vishnumurthi, but with his twisted moustache he looked more like Hiranya! He was an elderly man. They said that in his youth, if he played Chandramathi in the cremation-ground scene of the Harishchandra play, the whole village would weep. In the story of Nandanar he would play the Vedic priest and sing magnificently the song, ‘You who eat cattle, outcaste—do you deserve the holy day of Margazhi?’ One day he had taken a little ‘water’ before going on as the priest—that is, he had drunk a measure of milk. That was all. His singing that night was extraordinary! Instead of singing the proper line, he sang a drunken jumble that turned it into something like, ‘O cow that eats grass—do you have a Mar-thiru-kazhi day?’ Fortunately more than half the rustic audience were themselves floating in ‘water,’ so Vishnumurthi escaped. There were many such stories about the proprietor.

“They stood me before him. Though frightening to look at, he had a tender heart. I explained my condition, caught his feet and cried. The man who had played Vishnu alone was not prepared to forgive me. Why? He had only one scene in the whole play, and I had ruined even that scene with my commotion! Proprietor Vishnumurthi not only forgave me but gave me work in the company. At first my job was to ring the drama bell. Then I was given work pulling the scenery. From that job I even received a chance to become an actor.

“Our company was staging ‘Avvai Piratti.’ By then we had grown beyond simple street performances. We could go to a town, stay for a month and stage plays continuously. We put up a shed in the centre of town, charged admission, collected money and staged dramas. It was during one such run that I became an actor.

“It was the Avvaiyar play. The first four or five scenes were over. You know the scene in which Murugan, trying to trick Avvai, sits in a naval-fruit tree disguised as a cowherd boy? The fellow playing the cowherd had done something wrong and had been beaten by the proprietor in the make-up room. He stood there crying. Then came the naval-fruit scene. Avvaiyar entered the stage singing. She looked up at the tree. The boy who was supposed to be there was missing.

“What could Avvai do? She was stunned. Seeing this, I made a decision immediately. From inside the stage I climbed up to the loft and, from above, dropped down into the tree on the set and sat there like the cowherd boy. The ragged clothes I ordinarily wore helped greatly. The proprietor learned what had happened. From the next day onward I myself was cast as the cowherd boy. From cowherd to Markandeya, larger roles came to me one after another. People in those days said no one played Dhruva as I did. In the Prahlada play, when Narasimha burst from the pillar, killed Hiranya, tore open his belly and wore the entrails as a garland, I would sing a virutham; the applause would shake the shed.

“It was while I was playing Markandeya that, on one occasion, danger came both to my life and to Shiva's life.

“The scene went like this: Yama chases me, Markandeya. I run and embrace the Shivalinga. Yama throws his noose. At once the image must split open, Shiva must emerge and leap at Yama with a spear as though stabbing him. That is the scene.

“Yama chased me as usual. I ran and clung to the Shivalinga. Before Yama could throw the noose, the linga split and Shiva came out. The noose, thrown late, fell not on me but around Shiva's neck and tightened. With his neck being strangled, Shiva hurled at my chest the spear he was supposed to thrust at Yama. ‘Markandeya’ fainted and fell. Shiva too rolled over. ‘Yama’ had to help both of us! The actor playing Yama was also a good country doctor. I recovered after his treatment.

“In the Nallathangal play I was the eldest child too. When I stood by the well and sang, there was not a day without shouts of ‘Once more!’ I have even played Nallathangal herself. Many funny things happened in our company's Nallathangal. One day I was playing Nallathangal in the scene where she lights the stove with a banana stem. Ordinarily they soaked the banana stem in kerosene beforehand. As soon as fire touched it, it would burn, and the audience would clap. That day they seem to have forgotten to pour the oil. Nallathangal screamed like Kali, ‘If I am a chaste wife, let this banana stem burn!’ She screamed and lamented, but nothing happened!

“Nallathangal understood the problem. I did not know what to do. I ran backstage, poured kerosene into a glass bottle and came back onto the stage. Then I spoke like this:

“‘Guardians of the eight directions! Until now this Nallathangal has burned only a bare banana stem. Now, to prove that she is a truly chaste wife, I shall pour a little water into the stove as well and make the banana stem catch fire!’ I improvised that new line, poured the oil into the stove and lit it. That day's scene was better than ever—tremendous applause!

“In the Harishchandra play I used to play Lohitadasan. The scene in which Lohitadasan is bitten by a snake and dies looked very realistic on stage. Chandramathi's lament too greatly moved the audience. As Lohitadasan, I lay dead from the snakebite. Chandramathi had brought me to the cremation ground and laid me down. After the argument between Harishchandra and Chandramathi, Harishchandra was about to cut Chandramathi down. I lay on the funeral pyre like a corpse, breathing so that the audience could not notice. Apparently there was a scorpion among the dried cow-dung cakes piled for me. Nobody had noticed it. It stung me badly.

“‘Ayyo! A scorpion! A scorpion!’ I screamed and sprang up. Harishchandra, who had raised his sword to cut Chandramathi, froze. The sword was supposed to turn into a garland around Chandramathi's neck, and then Paramashiva was supposed to arrive and restore Lohitadasan to life. The scorpion in the funeral fuel changed the whole story! A man who had died from a snakebite was brought back to life by a scorpion. Fortunately the actor playing Paramashiva had some presence of mind. He ran onto the stage.

“‘Behold, come, child Harishchandra! We have admired your devotion to truth. Hearing your wife's voice, we hurried from Kailasa to save your son. On the way the leg of our bull broke. We thought it would take time to “repair” it. Before Lohitadasan's life reached Yamaloka, from where it could not be taken “back,” we sent Vayu Bhagavan whom we met on the road. He took the form of a scorpion and has raised the child who died from a snakebite. May there be auspiciousness—subham!’

“He delivered that new sermon, and the audience applauded even that. I, however, could scarcely bear the pain of the scorpion sting.

“Life went on like this, full of performance and merriment. Four or five years rolled by. I kept longing to meet Parimalam, who remained an indelible picture in my mind. Then the company proprietor Vishnumurthi suddenly died. The entire responsibility for the company fell on my shoulders. With the cooperation of my fellow actors, we prepared new plays.

“We bid farewell to Valli Thirumanam and staged a play called ‘The Withering Poor.’ Nallathangal, Savithri, Harishchandra and Prahlada were left without takers. My thoughts became plays. Through drama we spoke new and newer ideas. Opposition was strong; support grew just as strongly. I found consolation in the joy that I could tell people what I thought. Even so, I longed to meet the enchantress of my heart. I met her. We became one. I recognised Parimalam through the clues of the Pattukkottai school, Marungappallam village and Thandavan.

“But she could not recognise this orphan. Still, we became lovers. Elder! Then we met you as well. It was only to understand your riddle that we came in police uniform. The police uniform belongs to our drama company—that is all. There, elder! My story is finished. Now you may begin yours,” said Durai.

The moment he finished speaking, old Mayandi fainted and collapsed to the floor.
