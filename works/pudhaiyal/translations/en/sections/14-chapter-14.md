# 14

After they reached Puduppattinam, old Mayandi too recovered enough strength to get up and move about. He began talking cheerfully with Parimalam and Durai. The old man had understood that Durai was not a police officer and that he had put on the police disguise for some reason. But he could not understand what that reason was. He wanted to ask and find out; he waited for a time when they would be free.

Durai, precisely because he expected the old man to ask about his police disguise, kept his friends beside him and went on talking. There was something in the house that gave them plenty of occupation for passing the time. Four of them sat together in great delight while the game went on, with Parimalam and the old man sitting nearby and enjoying it. Many hours passed. Whenever a carrom piece that seemed about to fall into a pocket bounced out and came to rest beside it, the old man would laugh to himself. Perhaps he remembered how he too had fallen into the sea and then returned to shore.

<!-- source join: scan 128 → 129 -->

Durai remaining absorbed in the carrom game and his friends never leaving him caused the old man great trouble. At last he reached a decision and spoke openly and boldly.

“All of you must allow your friend Durai to speak with me alone for a little while.” The moment he said this, the friends began to rise. Durai could hardly stop them. Though he had already thought in advance about what the old man might ask and how he should answer, he now felt a little afraid. His friends took leave of him and moved away. Durai waited eagerly for the old man's lips to move. Parimalam too stared, not knowing what the old man's conversation was going to concern. The old man kept walking slowly back and forth. Durai did not want to ask on his own, “What secret do you want to discuss?” The old man too did not begin quickly. He simply continued pacing, like an aged tiger. Finally Durai decided to make him open his mouth himself. “Should Parimalam not be here either?” he asked as though sincerely seeking clarification.

<!-- source: scan 129 → 130 -->

“It's all right—let her stay, Durai! I'm not going to discuss any secret that should frighten anyone. Is it really true that you are a police officer? I want clarity! Answer me at once without hesitation!” the old man demanded in a stern voice. Since this was not a question asked doubtfully, timidly, or in a pleading tone, Durai decided to give him the truthful answer.

“I am not a police officer!”

“Then why the need for the disguise?”

“Would I put it on if there were no need?”

“So if someone loads goods onto a clandestine boat, you threaten him and then make use of the goods without any trouble—is that it? A fine livelihood! It's like the story of a fellow who dressed as a fox or tiger to hunt donkeys.”

Though these words made Durai somewhat angry, his respect for the old man kept him silent.

“I won't take your silence as agreement. Did you come to hunt clandestine boats—or did you have some other purpose?” the old man continued.

“I undertook it in order to find a mysterious man.”

“Who is that mysterious man?”

<!-- source: scan 130 → 131 -->

“You yourself. Your speech and actions created a great confusion in me. I wanted somehow to find you and learn the reason for those actions.”

The old man's astonishment grew.

“Am I a mysterious man? No—wrong! Wrong!!” he said loudly.

“If you aren't mysterious, why pray to the sky in that fashion? Why suddenly attack the two of us and knock us down? Why board a clandestine boat? Why fall into the sea and try to give up your life?”

“Are those the signs of a mysterious man? They're the signs of a man whose head is confused! I am a madman! Know that for certain!”

“I know for certain—you are not mad!”

“How do you know?”

“A madman has never admitted that he is mad!”

“This is advanced madness—it admits even that!”

“But for me to admit that, wouldn't I have to be half-mad?”

The old man began to laugh at Durai's reply and continued speaking through his laughter.

<!-- source: scan 131 → 132 -->

“Do not try to learn anything about me. As time goes on you will discover who I am by yourself—but one thing: I am a moon shining in a forest! Don't think I am boasting.

“And don't laugh because I compare myself with the moon. I am telling you the truth: I am a moon shining in a forest.” The old man spoke with emphasis, then began pacing a little faster.

Durai stood looking at Parimalam. Parimalam stood enjoying the old man's walk. After pacing for a little while, he returned and stood before Durai.

“All right—everything is fine. You torment an old man asking, ‘Who are you? Who are you?’ and take the life out of me. But who are the two of you? Tell me that!” he asked mischievously. Durai remained silent.

“Do you think I don't know? One thing I know for certain—you two are illicit lovers. I heard what you were saying in that room last night. You spoke thinking the old fellow was asleep. You even locked the doors from the outside—afraid I would leave! Hmm—you locked the doors. Could you lock my ears? You and Parimalam were discussing where you could go and hide and live together, weren't you?”

Durai and Parimalam laughed.

“Ayyo—children who know nothing of the world! In this blind world, love or snake-gourd, what does it matter! I cannot stand the sight of lovers. I do feel sympathy for them—but I never feel any desire to praise them!”

<!-- source join: scan 132 → 133 -->

“That's how old people are,” Parimalam replied.

“What? What?” the old man asked quickly.

“Doesn't the evening sun naturally hate the alli flower?” Parimalam said.

“Why?” asked the old man.

“The evening sun is taking leave of the world. It knows that after it goes, the moon and the alli will meet. So the sun hates the alli flower, thinking: the alli that I could not have is going to belong to the moon!”

“And that means.........?”

“Old men like the evening sun hate a life of love like the alli flower.”

“Yes! That's true,” Durai said and added an explanation.

“Young men dislike the ways of old age.”

The old man became a little angry.

“The old dislike what they have already experienced. Young people like you dislike old age, which you have never experienced!”

<!-- source: scan 133 → 134 -->

Parimalam immediately asked:

“Then grandfather has experienced love, I suppose?”

“Certainly not that terrible experience.”

“Then may you hate something you have never experienced?”

The old man was caught by Parimalam's sudden question and could not answer. He paced back and forth, then turned to Durai.

“Thambi! A perfect match—a very clever girl! She suits you. Your whole life will become one long game—one long amusement—one long pleasurable pastime. She is the queen for that!”

“Not only a pleasurable pastime—it will also be an ideal dream,” Durai said firmly.

“An ideal? Yes, thambi—it is going to remain only a dream for you! Don't think I'm speaking badly of your beloved. I have never read of anyone caught in the illusion of love who fulfilled his duty.”

Parimalam became angry.

“All right, elder! For your sake I'll agree that those caught in love's illusion failed to fulfil duty. But will you at least accept that many heroes of duty have themselves fallen into love's illusion?”

Durai immediately interrupted:

<!-- source: scan 134 → 135 -->

“Why go that far, Parimalam? We may be caught in love, but we have not been caught in the illusion of love,” he said. The old man replied at once:

“Love—or illusion—whatever it is, you're caught in something. At least agree to that much.”

“There are knots that can be untied too!” said Durai.

“Why keep growing word upon word, Durai? What have the two of you decided to do now?” The old man changed the subject.

Parimalam, absorbed in the lively conversation, heard some sound from the backyard and went there with a lamp to look. All she saw was a cat running in the darkness. She returned with the lamp to where the old man and Durai were. But she failed to notice a human figure standing hidden in the backyard.

The figure pressed itself against a tree so that the lamplight would not fall on it. In its hand was a gleaming knife. Once Parimalam went back inside, the figure came and stood near the backyard entrance.

Durai and Parimalam, paying no attention in that direction, gave all their attention to the old man's speech. Durai stood not knowing what answer to give when the old man asked, “What is your next arrangement?”

<!-- source: scan 135 → 136 -->

“Hmm—you know only how to love! Shouldn't you know how to protect yourselves too? Wherever you remain in this region, they will catch you. From what you have said, Parimalam's father seems a great brute—and not only that, like me he appears to be an enemy of love. So run away to foreign lands. Isn't running away the third chapter of the book of love? The first chapter ends in quarrel—the second ends in a demonstration by the forces of opposition—the third begins with flight!”

“It looks as though you could write an entire book about love,” Durai mocked.

“What great task is that! If I stay with the two of you and write a diary every day, it itself will become a book of love!”

Parimalam laughed. Durai privately marvelled at the old man's clever speech.

“Listen to me without talking! If you stay here, you're in danger. Run away to Ceylon,” the old man said.

“To Ceylon? No, no!” Durai cried loudly.

“Why? Because you won't find a livelihood there? Or because you can't get permission? Don't worry! The clandestine boat is always there. If you go there, I'll give you a place where you can live peacefully.”

<!-- source: scan 136 → 137 -->

“I'm not saying this because there is no way to go there—or no means of living there after I arrive. I will never set foot in Ceylon again.”

“Why do you hate Ceylon like this? Because it is foreign soil?”

“No—no! Ceylon is the land where I was born—”

“What—you were born in Ceylon?”

Parimalam too was astonished. Looking at her, Durai said:

“Yes—I was born in Ceylon. Even my Parimalam doesn't know this. Until now there has been no need to tell my entire story. She can know only some portions of my life.”

“I don't understand why being born in Ceylon means you must never go there again, thambi!”

“My story is very strange—and very painful! Elder! The youthful part of the book of my life is all tears! Tears!! Tears!!!”

“Tell it, then. We'll listen—”

“I am going to tell it—

“You said love is unnecessary—that you dislike it. If that love alone had not come to me, I would have become dust and earth. This Parimalam created a beautiful turning point in my sorrowful life. Yes—my life is a flood-land filled with so much pain! No young man could ever have walked carrying a burden of sorrow as great as mine!”

<!-- source join: scan 137 → 138 -->

“Don't write such a long preface to the story! Come on, tell your story!”

“Here, I'm telling it,” Durai said, clearing his throat. Parimalam looked only at the beauty-filled face of her beloved. The old man too prepared to listen to Durai's story. The frightening figure at the backyard entrance moved closer to the wall, stretched its ear toward the inside, and waited for the story.
