# 23

The old man had listened closely to Durai's entire story. When he suddenly fainted and collapsed, Durai and Parimalam were bewildered, unable to understand the reason. They lifted him and began trying to bring him out of the faint. His whole body was soaked in sweat. Pearls of perspiration rolled across his wrinkled forehead. Durai kept soaking a cloth in the water Parimalam brought and pressing it against the old man's face. After a little while the old man moved. Slowly he opened his eyes.

“Elder! Elder!” Durai called anxiously. The old man gestured that he wanted to sit up. Durai and Parimalam lifted him and seated him in a chair. Leaning back in it, the old man let out a long breath.

“Why did you suddenly faint? Does this happen to you ordinarily?” Parimalam asked. He shook his head to indicate that it did not.

“Did my story perhaps move you too deeply?” Durai asked eagerly.

The old man slowly moved his lips to say yes and murmured, “Mm.”

“If I had known this would happen, I would never have told you the story,” Durai said to himself in distress.

“There was nothing wrong in telling it, Durai,” said the mysterious Mayandi, and released another long sigh.

“You are hiding some pain. Why shouldn't you tell us what it is?” Durai asked in agitation.

“I will tell you. I am going to tell you.”

“Not now. If you are tired, tell us later,” said Parimalam.

“No, amma. I must finish telling it now. Only then will the burden in my heart become lighter.”

Durai and Parimalam stared at his lips. The old man cleared his throat. The movement seemed a prelude to the disclosure of some great secret.

“Durai! Kumaravadivu left behind his aged mother, ten-year-old you and your five-year-old sister, that tender little shoot, and entered the prison fortress. I shall turn a few pages of his book of life for you. Listen carefully.”

“What? My father's book of life?”

“A book? It was a mound of sorrow—a forest into which happiness could not enter! Kumaravadivu went to Ceylon as a man who had once lived well and then fallen; he entered prison branded a murderer—and came out like a sage.”

“What are you saying? Then... you...?” Durai asked, drawing out the words in a voice full of excitement.

“Kumaravadivu was accused of murdering his wife. The diary note saying that the valli tubers had to be cut made him a prisoner. You have already told me everything you know of his story. But while he suffered like a bird with broken wings shut inside a cage, he knew nothing of the terrible things that happened outside to the young birds preyed upon by criminals.

“You may or may not know this about a prisoner sentenced for life, Durai: under prison rules, a life sentence does not necessarily mean remaining in prison until death. Yes, Durai, he need not stay there until he dies. Before he dies entirely, such a prisoner can certainly be released. According to conduct there are remissions of days; at the end a ‘board’ meets and may remit another year or two; there are yearly remission calculations. In these ways seven or eight years can be deducted, and a life prisoner may be released after ten or twelve years. Kumaravadivu too was released in that manner.

“When he came out of prison, you may have been around twenty or twenty-two. Once outside, Kumaravadivu wandered everywhere searching for you and your sister. Who was going to give a proper answer to a man known as a murderer? In prison he had wandered like a madman, doing the work assigned by the officers and spending every free moment thinking about you. Imagine what it was like when, after release, he could not find where you had gone. He must have dreamed so many happy dreams: his children would have grown; Thangam would have become a beautiful young woman; his son would be a vigorous young bull; he could embrace both and rejoice. When all those dreams were shattered by the very release he had awaited, how much must he have suffered?

“There are many kinds of prisoners. In the prison where Kumaravadivu was held, the number of men who had killed their wives was particularly high. When prisoners gathered to talk, they would certainly unfold the stories of how they came to prison.

“‘She was lying with another man. Could a man with honour tolerate that? I finished her off—with a sharpened sickle!’ one prisoner would boast.

“Another would say angrily, ‘She announced that she would stay only at her mother's house and that, if I felt thirsty for her, I could come there. Could the man who tied the tali tolerate such talk? I smashed her head against the ground until she died.’

“Another would tell his revenge tale with enthusiasm: ‘Ayyo, darling! How can I bear to part from you? she cried as she sent me away. I pretended to leave for my village and returned home in the middle of the night. There was my goddess of chastity, rolling about with her lover. I saw it with my own eyes. What next? My hand itself became the knife. That was all—the two lovers went together straight to Kailasa!’

“Another prisoner would shout with sparks in his eyes: ‘She and her beloved plotted to kill me, her own husband. Could that succeed against me? One night my wife and that man were asleep. I decided the time had come and cut off both their heads. I tied the two heads together like coconuts, hung them from a pole, put the pole on my shoulder and walked straight into the court. The judge fainted. The police inspectors were shocked. I had to comfort them all. This sentence is the reward for the good deed I did!’

“One prisoner, however, would weep: ‘I am a fool! A traitor! A sinner! I suspected my wife without cause and killed her. She was a good woman—a chaste wife.’

“Among prisoners who openly admitted their killings, Kumaravadivu alone earned the nickname ‘liar.’ ‘When I wrote that Valli must be cut, ayya, I meant the tuber, not my wife!’ he would shout again and again. The prisoners would mock him—‘Don't lie!’—and pierce his heart with their ridicule. This lasted only a few months. Kumaravadivu was then promoted from an ordinary prisoner to a prisoner-overseer.

“After that he became a convict warder. Inside prison, convict warder was a somewhat respectable position. The salary for the job was remission of sentence days. But one could not sleep long, or lie down whenever one pleased. There was work at every hour, day and night, and there could be no lapse. The hardship had a reward—the remission of days I mentioned.

“Countless human beings live like walking corpses in some corner of the world, ordered that they may walk only within a certain boundary and must suffer if they try to cross it. In such terrible days, would they not welcome even one day cut from their sentence? Kumaravadivu too counted his days like that. Only after release did the thought come to him that perhaps he should have spent his whole life inside. What could he do then? A prison is not a place where, when they release you, you may say, ‘I don't want to go,’ or, ‘Let me stay another day and leave tomorrow.’

“Leaving the small prison for the larger prison called the world, Kumaravadivu wandered for some days in Ceylon and then came to India. He searched for his son and daughter until he himself finally accepted that he would never find them. Whenever Kumaravadivu wet the soil of Tamil Nadu with his tears, he did not know that you were acting on that very soil while your sister slept her long sleep beneath it. How could he know? What chance did he have to learn it?

“He would tell himself that bookworms had eaten the family book and that Kumaravadivu alone remained as a single loose page, flying with the dust in the winds of time.

“That wandering page travelled from village to village and became a sage preaching new philosophies. Yes, he regarded himself as a sage; villagers mocked him as some mad Vedantin. He did not care. He longed to tell somebody the ideas he believed he had discovered. There was another reason people listened to his preaching: he had acquired a reputation as an expert healer. Kumaravadivu, who had once plucked tea leaves, now became a Siddha physician who plucked medicinal herbs.

“He learned that skill in prison. A fellow prisoner who was a great expert in medicine taught him, and that lesson helped Kumaravadivu earn his food. Medicine gave a new turn to Kumaravadivu's path—and it was Marungappallam village that caused that turn. Yes, Durai! The very Marungappallam where your sister lies mingled with the earth changed your father's path too. Marungappallam is the place where seventy crores in pure gold are said to lie hidden. Even if it is not seventy crores, something must surely be there. It is true that many people roam about searching for that treasure. When greed for gold could grow so great that a swami would sacrifice a child, do we even need to ask about others? Even Ayyappan, the rough boatman in the boat you intercepted at sea, is one of those licking his lips for that treasure.

“Like Ayyappan, many people are brushing their teeth in readiness to eat that treasure. The secret of such a treasure—a copper plate containing the entire method of reaching it—and a small stone came into your father's hands.”

“It came into his hands? A clue to the treasure? How?” Durai asked with a start.

“Wait. I will tell you,” the old man said, clearing his throat and continuing.

“He had obtained the method and means of taking the treasure. Then questions began to gnaw at Kumaravadivu: What was he to do with it? For whom should he take it?”

The old man had not finished speaking. Durai, who had listened eagerly until then, suddenly became impatient. He had concluded that the mysterious old Mayandi was his father and had wanted to learn everything in the man's heart without revealing that conclusion. Yet suddenly he could restrain himself no longer.

“For whom should the treasure be taken? For me! For your son!” he shouted.

Parimalam too was stunned. The old man stood like a tree.

“Appa! Appa!” Durai cried, embracing him.

“I am not your father Kumaravadivu. He is dead. It is true. Believe what I say—he is dead,” the old man cried.

“Truly? You are not my father?” Durai trembled.

“No.”

“Then who are you?”

“I am your father Kumaravadivu's friend—his prison companion. After our release, he and I lived as friends. Until Kumaravadivu died, I never left him,” said Mayandi, shedding tears.
