# 43

Wondering how much water might have seeped into the well and whether it would obstruct his attempt, Ambalam came with Mayandi to the school entrance, absorbed in examining the well. He leaned over it eagerly.

He could make out nothing in the muddy water that had seeped into the well. At last it was decided that Mayandi would climb down and inspect it. A rope was tied around his waist and fastened to a wooden piece above. With Ambalam's help, Mayandi descended into the well. From above, Ambalam watched every one of Mayandi's movements with agitation.

After a little while Mayandi shouted, “Victory! Victory!”

“Aha! Is that so?” Ambalam danced above the well.

Mayandi shouted that there was a box in the well fastened with an iron chain. Ambalam immediately began climbing down himself to help lift it.

At once Mayandi cried, “No—no! It's only a small box—I'll bring it up myself.”

“I'll send down another rope—tie it to that! I'll pull the box up,” Ambalam said.

Old Mayandi laughed and mocked him: “Why? Do you intend to take the box up by rope and seal me inside the well? No, no. I'll bring it myself!”

“Come now, old man! Your share will be given to you, ayya! Don't be afraid!” Ambalam said, leaping about with uncontrollable joy. The old man too came up out of the well with the box.

It was a heavy little iron chest, attached to a thin chain. With great difficulty Ambalam opened it. Inside, once again, all they found was a gleaming metal plate. Old Mayandi pursed his lips wearily. “Fine, then! Whenever we search for treasure, all we get is another plate,” he muttered.

Seeing this, Ambalam said, “Why lose heart already? Those who persist do not fall into disgrace! Leave that aside—let us read this,” and handed the plate to Mayandi.

The old man struggled greatly to read the writing and at last somehow began to decipher it. This was the message on it:

“The treasure is here. If a married couple are killed on the very day of their wedding and their blood is sprinkled on this place, the treasure will burst forth.”

When he finished reading, old Mayandi cried, “Ayyo—what an atrocity! Must a married couple be killed? We do not want such a treasure at all!”

“Where, ayya, are we to find a couple suitable to be killed immediately after marriage?” Ambalam lamented.

Hearing him, the old man said firmly, “Oh! Have you already begun looking for people to sacrifice? Velliyambalam! Listen to me. Let us not take this treasure by committing such a cruel act. Should two flowers in the bloom of youth lose their lives for our greed? Cruelty! Cruelty! I don't want the treasure—I don't want anything.”

“You're a cowardly old man! Why are you so afraid of killing? We are going to become millionaires—will the business succeed if we grow frightened thinking about all this?” Ambalam began trying to give him courage.

The old man stood firm and kept refusing until the end. It was no use. A situation arose in which he had no choice but to submit to Ambalam's decision.

“Where are we to go and find a newly married couple right on the wedding platform?” Ambalam remained confused by the thought as the two of them turned back towards the house.

* * * *

When Menaka saw Ambalam enter the room, she pretended to be sleeping from exhaustion. Ambalam too lay down without intending to wake her, letting his whole mind wander only around the treasure. Menaka carefully watched only where he placed the bunch of keys.

“Married couple—married couple”—Ambalam's mouth kept murmuring these words softly. Suddenly an idea came to him.

“Durai is here with us, isn't he? Why not get him married and finish the business?”—thus spun his thoughts!

“If there is to be a marriage, there must also be a woman—where are we to find one? And even if there were one, wouldn't the bridegroom and the woman both have to consent before the marriage could be smoothly completed and the business easily accomplished?”—thus came strings of questions!

“Ayyo—if only that Parimala had not run away, we could now have married her to Durai and fulfilled our purpose!”—thus raged storms of thought!

Velliyambalam reached the very limits of confusion. Whenever he closed his eyes to sleep, scenes began moving one after another before him: a wedding taking place; the couple being killed even before their first-night meeting; their blood being sprinkled into the well; the well bursting open and gold bars scattering out in vessel after vessel, slab after slab. Menaka, who had been waiting for the bunch of keys, watched until Ambalam had dozed a little, stole it and slipped from the room like a cat.

* * * *

Durai and Malayalam Bhagavathi, realizing that the door of their room was being opened, moved apart from each other and stood ready. The door opened. Menaka entered with a beautiful little box in her hand and urged Durai, “Mm—quickly, quickly!” Durai began walking slowly behind her. Malayalam Bhagavathi too set off with them. Opening the back door, the three reached the backyard. With deep sighs, all three revealed the desires of their hearts.

Menaka, shut away like a life-sentenced prisoner in a cruel jail, perhaps thinking of the freedom she was about to gain, released such a long sigh from the depths of her lungs. They had to cross the backyard wall. Menaka said that she alone knew the way well and that she herself would climb the wall first and show them the route. Durai, however, planned that he should cross first, Malayalam Bhagavathi after him, and Menaka last.

According to that plan, Durai and Malayalam Bhagavathi, climbing and jumping over the wall first and second, could prevent Menaka from following them and run away. In that way they could save Menaka too from committing a wrong. As planned, Durai climbed the wall first. It sounded as though there was some noise inside the house. Malayalam Bhagavathi hid well in a bush near the backyard. Menaka was helping Durai climb the wall. Durai had climbed halfway.

From near the back entrance came Ambalam's shout: “Hey!” Menaka turned in shock.

Ambalam furiously threw a gleaming knife at Durai. Menaka sprang forward and blocked the knife thrown towards Durai's back. The knife plunged into Menaka's chest and lodged there. Menaka screamed and fell. The guards who had been sleeping woke up.

Ambalam ran to where Menaka had fallen. The guards seized Durai as well. Understanding the crisis, Malayalam Bhagavathi slowly slipped away from the bush, ran into the house and lay down in the room where Durai had been confined, pretending to sleep. She could not control the pounding of her heart. Fear of what might happen next enveloped her.

They dragged Durai back and shoved him into the room. “Is the fortune-teller there?” Ambalam asked, looking inside. There he was, apparently exhausted and snoring away!

“All right! Don't keep the fortune-teller with this fellow! Put him in another room! Put Durai in this room!” Ambalam ordered his men, like a jailer giving instructions to warders.

Durai was locked in that room. They woke the fortune-teller from his sleep and made him walk to another room. He went with them, staggering as though still deep in sleep, falling and getting up again. He too was locked in a room.

Menaka lay as though swimming in a pool of blood. They lifted her and placed her on a bed. Her life hung in the balance. That beautiful flower, which had wanted to discover something new in life, now appeared on the verge of withering. The tears pouring from her eyes defeated the blood pouring from her chest. Ambalam and his men stood around her. Menaka had longed for change in life—for freedom from darkness into a world of light. But freedom was waiting to come to her in another form.
