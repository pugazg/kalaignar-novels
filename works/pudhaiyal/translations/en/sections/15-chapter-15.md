# 15

Durai began telling his story.

“My father was a plantation labourer—!” No sooner had he begun like this than old Mayandi interrupted. “Then say he was a Tamil who migrated from here!”

“Yes,” Durai nodded, and continued.

“My father was born in Tamil Nadu. He too was one among the lakhs upon lakhs of Tamil people who, finding no way to survive in their homeland, left with tears filling their eyes and were battered and ruined along foreign shores. My father's father—my grandfather—had lived as a great wealthy man in Tamil Nadu. Today grass has grown over the place where he lived.

<!-- source: scan 138 → 139 -->

“My grandfather's life in Tamil Nadu had been extremely luxurious. They say his house was a great mansion. Today you couldn't find even its ruined little wall there—a huge temple was built, and now trees and plants grow over that too. Grandfather conducted one great festival every year! At it he would give gifts of gold to a thousand Brahmins. In between, once every month there would be a samaaraadhanai for Brahmins! Gifts and charities! There was hardly a day without some festival in his house. Spending! Spending!! The mound of money my grandfather had accumulated was being dissolved away in the names of gifts, charity and Brahmin feeding. They say he even had to borrow a large sum for the marriage he conducted for his son—that is, my father. The debt matured; the property disappeared!

“The gold gifts and food gifts appeared before grandfather's mind as events of the dead past and only deepened his wound. The people who had come for meals, the people who sang praises to receive gifts—none of them came now. His relatives and all the kin who had surrounded him became like birds abandoning a dried-up tank. Grandfather was broken-hearted. Once the house, entrance, cattle and land had all become property pledged against debt—once everything was about to pass into the hands of the creditors—the pain of wondering how he could ever again walk the streets with honour began eating into him. We once lived with everyone saying ‘Oh-ho!’—today we stand in the street—no one even turns toward us! At the highest point of that misery, grandfather, in order to protect his honour, left my father and mother alone and killed himself.

<!-- source join: scan 139 → 140 -->

“My father and mother were young. Grandfather's end left them floundering. My mother's family was very, very poor—and without anyone to support it. They say grandfather had insisted on marrying his son to a poor girl and conducted the marriage despite the opposition of many important people. Grandmother, Appa and Amma—the three of them had no way to live in Tamil Nadu.

“Because Appa had grown up as the pampered son of a rich household, he did not know how to do any trade and had no practice at one. He decided he might take up agriculture and went to an Ayyar in that village. That Ayyar had received many acres of land from my grandfather as gifts for his priestly work. Appa went to him and asked to be given responsibility for cultivating the land. The Ayyar refused. ‘This is land my father donated to you—at least give me the work of a tenant who ploughs it, raises the crop and measures out the grain,’ Appa begged. That deceitful man asked loudly, ‘Where do you have the money even to buy plough cattle?’ and shut the door. Appa went and told grandmother what had happened and cried. What could grandmother know? She too must have lamented. They reached the conclusion that they could not live in Tamil Nadu. Before taking a begging bowl in hand and going into the street crying, ‘Give me a coin, ayya!’ they tried to find some other path. ‘Tirupati Venkatajalapathi! Will you not show a way to these people with no direction?’ grandmother wept. Venkatesa showed no way—the diamond garland grandfather had commissioned was the only thing that shone!

<!-- source join: scan 140 → 141 -->

“‘Nataraja of Thillai! Will you not end our trouble?’ grandmother cried. The golden house laughed! The merciful lord showed no mercy to quench the burning heat. It was at that cruel time that the island of Ceylon called aloud for labourers to bring itself under cultivation. Thousands upon thousands of Tamils boarded ships to destroy Ceylon's wilderness and rough country and turn it into beautiful groves. Our poor family joined them. Appa, Amma and grandmother all began working on plantations in Ceylon.

“‘When the cold falls, our family leaves for work. My wife and I at least are young. My mother is old—her body weakened, all her flesh trembling. She walks though she can scarcely walk. The body my father had protected without allowing its radiance to diminish has worn itself away plucking tea leaves!’ That is what my father wrote about grandmother in his diary,” Durai said and sighed deeply. Distress spread across his eyes. Old Mayandi listened eagerly without moving. The reflections that naturally pass across a woman's face while hearing a story flashed and vanished over Parimalam's face too.

“Appa wrote one more thing in the diary. If I read that and then remember my grandmother's sacred face even once, even now I feel as though my heart will burst! Grandmother died only after growing ripe with age—but before she mixed with the earth, she had met every suffering the world had for her, in heap after heap.

<!-- source join: scan 141 → 142; Tamil authority retains source-odd `பழுமாகப்` -->

“‘Even after my beloved mother came to Ceylon, she went hungry four days in a row. A son was born to me. There was no money for the childbirth expenses. So my mother secretly sold the grain allotted for her own food, without anyone knowing, and paid one or two days of the childbirth expenses. A son was born to me. My mother is starving. Ayyo, was I born as a son to her only for this—sinner!’ That is what my father wrote in the diary. Even now my heart trembles when I think of it. Who knows what my father's mind must have suffered! Ayya, elder! In your long experience you too must have met any number of human beings—but no one could have been pierced in youth by as many arrows of pain as I was. While still young I lost my father; I lost my mother. My grandmother, a ripe leaf, gave me her shade and I grew beneath it. Not only me, elder! My younger sister too had to grow beside me beneath that shade.

“My grandmother endured the news that her beloved husband had killed himself—for the sake of keeping her son and daughter-in-law alive under her shade. She, who had lived smiling amid beds and mansions, somehow endured even when famine and hunger seized her—for the sake of allowing her son and daughter-in-law to keep the family lamp burning. Then she was pitifully separated from both son and daughter-in-law. Even then she staggered onward carrying grief, driven by the desire to make the two tender grandchildren—my sister and me—into the fruits of the family line. Great sir! No one could have suffered as I did. My father's separation was an unexpected terror! My mother's end is beyond words!

<!-- source join: scan 142 → 143 -->

“My mother was not of an age to die! Grandmother would say exactly that again and again as she cried: ‘You wicked Yama! You left me behind and carried her away!’ Then my sister and I would cling to grandmother and cry. I would have been ten years old. My sister would have been five. At that age, to be separated at the same time from both the mother and father who had guarded us like their eyes—was living after that an ordinary thing?

“Imagine chicks in the forest sitting with their tiny beaks open to the sky, waiting for the mother bird that went to seek food—and then the mother's corpse falls upon them. What would that be like?

“That was our condition. We were mullai buds. Boiling water had been poured at the root of the plant!—

<!-- source: scan 143 → 144 -->

“The streets of Trincomalee in which we had played and wandered began to look like terrifying caves to us. I had gone to school. At midday I came running and jumping home for food. My beloved little sister, who usually stood at the entrance clapping her hands to welcome me, was nowhere to be seen. The neighbours called me over and embraced me. I understood nothing. Why were they stopping me from entering the house—and stopping me with such affection and concern? Why had the faces of those stopping me gone pale? I was bewildered. ‘Appa, kannu! Don't cry—Amma has died,’ they tried to console me.

“My mother had not simply died—no, no! My mother had been murdered. Yes—on the rope cot my mother floated in a flood of blood. There were knife wounds in her side and back! My mother had been murdered. Do you know who killed her? Elder! I tremble even to speak of it. You are listening to my story patiently and calmly—but my heart has become a boiling cauldron, old sir! The person who stabbed my mother to death, they said, was my father! Mother had become a corpse! Father had become a prisoner. Because they sent me to school in the morning, when I returned they no longer called me ‘Son!’ They had become silent—and my father stood at the heart of that silence. ‘Valli! Valli!’ Appa would call Amma with honey flowing from his mouth. And I, at that tender green age, became prey to the terrible news that the same Appa had destroyed in half a second one life he cherished.

<!-- source join: scan 144 → 145; Tamil authority retains source-odd `அவர்களே ஊமையாகியது` -->

“I thrashed—I collapsed—I cried—I rolled on the ground. At the hospital they cut my mother's body open for examination, then stitched the pieces together and handed her back to us. Need I tell you grandmother's condition? At that advanced age she gathered up her daughter-in-law's cut-open corpse and went to the cremation ground. Her son lay locked in prison. My sister and I writhed crying ‘Amma! Appa!’ Grandmother saw all these scenes. Tears swelled over the wrinkles of her cheeks and ran like canals.

“‘Ada paavi! Was that Kumaravadivu a murderer?’ the whole of Trincomalee cursed. My father's name was Kumaravadivu. Kumaravadivu had killed Valliyammal—yes, my father had murdered my mother.

“You may be thinking: you were only a ten-year-old boy; how could you have known all these details then? Though I was only ten, the diary Appa had written and the truths grandmother—who remained alive for some years after this terrible event—told me are the sources for the story I am telling. Parimalam's eyes are filling with tears merely hearing the story. Think for yourself how much the child who is a character in this story must have seethed and suffered.

<!-- source: scan 145 → 146; Tamil authority retains source `சிறுவனுயிற்றே நீ` -->

“Mother murdered—father arrested—little sister suffering—grandmother lamenting—and between them, I, a tender young shoot! See how my life began. Many more coils of pain lie stacked one after another. The news the world gives is that my father murdered my mother—the judgment justice delivered! But the truth is exactly the opposite!”
