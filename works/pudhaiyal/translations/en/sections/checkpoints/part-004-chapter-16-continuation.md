“—they have quieted down. Even then there were proprietors, supervisors, kanganis and storekeepers who possessed this good sense naturally—but they were very, very few in number! Whether or not you have read Pudhumaippithan's story *Thunbakkēni*, Parimalam may have read it. One wonders whether the coils of sorrow in that story could truly have happened—whether a tea plantation could be such an immense forest of suffering!

“Vellaiyammal, an Adi-Dravidar woman, goes to Ceylon through a kangani when she is left without a way to live after her husband is imprisoned in Tamil Nadu. The token of love given by her husband is growing in her womb as a girl-child. At that time, while she walks the Ceylon soil plucking tea at the ‘Water Palam’ estate, she becomes prey to the eyes of the white durais and is reduced to a state in which her chastity is rented out. The treasure given by her husband is born and grows. She resolves at least to protect that child properly. But her daughter too is violated. By whom? The very man who plundered the mother plunders the daughter as well. That, in brief, is the story of *Thunbakkēni*. If one reads even this and has also experienced the cruelties of the tea estates, anyone would conclude that it is not merely a well of sorrow but a sea of sorrow.

“You may think, Periyavare, that instead of briefly telling the story I came to tell, I am circling around it. Forgive me. Amma's murder is a mysterious whirlpool. I believe these incidents from the tea estate will help me prove who committed it and compel you to agree that my father was not guilty. From the fact that I can see no reaction at all on your face as you listen, I cannot conclude that you dislike the story! I conclude instead that you have become absorbed in it—though perhaps even that conclusion may be wrong.” Durai stopped speaking, hesitated a little, and tried to push one of the carrom men into a pocket.

Mayandi then said, “What you say is entirely true. I have indeed lost myself in the story you are telling. Tell me the rest! It is true that I do not know how to show expressions on my face and enjoy a story as your beloved does. But I am following your story, fixing every single letter you speak in my mind without letting one escape. A very sad story! A very, very sad story! Tell it, thambi, tell it! I am in no hurry to learn how you are going to prove that your father was not a murderer. I want to know to what extent young men like you understand the cruelties of Ceylon's tea estates. I too was one of those who imagined that Tamils went to Ceylon and exploited it. Only now do I understand the truth: Ceylon exploited them; they did not exploit Ceylon!

“Appane! Do not cut any incident from your story because you think I may become tired. I am relishing it atom by atom. If you like, add a few love incidents here and there for Parimalam's sake,” he said with a smile.

The old man's words encouraged Durai, and he continued.

“Do not forget that everything I tell you consists of events I learnt from my grandmother, from people in our street, and from the notes in the diaries Appa left behind. As you said, there are also a few love incidents Parimalam ought to hear.

“Every one of those chapters of love is a chapter stained and ruined. One bears a stain of disgrace, another a stain of blood—every love incident I know is a tragic picture ending with one stain or another. Let me give you an example of how greatly the cruelty of the white proprietors, the petty-mindedness of the kanganis, and the frenzy of betraying people for money had grown. It is a love incident. Appa's note in his diary about this incident, and his own views on it, are very long. I will tell it only in brief.” When Durai said this, Mayandi looked at Parimalam. “Listen carefully, Parimalam! This story is for you!” Parimalam smiled. “You can block your ears if you like. A love story will taste bitter to you,” she teased.

“Parimalam! You mustn't keep interrupting in the middle. You are giving my story as many intervals as one gets at a tent cinema!” Durai said, laughing.

“All right! Soda, colour drink, beeda! Hmm! Let the picture begin!” said Parimalam.

Durai began. “Kuppan and Seetha were a celebrated pair of lovers in our street. They lived as one life in two bodies. Opposition from the street, condemnation from relatives, the irritation and control of their parents—none of it mattered to them; their love kept growing. Seetha declared firmly that she would marry no one but Kuppan. Kuppan said the same thing, only in a somewhat louder voice. Because of that their marriage remained obstructed. Seetha's family decided somehow to kill Kuppan and be rid of him. Kuppan decided to live in another place until that fury cooled, and accordingly he went away and lived elsewhere for some days. The Kuppan-Seetha epic of love spread through the street, spread through the tea estate, and spread all the way to the proprietors' houses. Dog Durai made a plan to catch that little bird. He used the net called Kangani Kalimuthu. That traitor Kalimuthu somehow deceived Seetha and satisfied Dog Durai's appetite. News that Seetha's chastity had been shattered reached her parents. They writhed in pain. They feared making the matter public. They trembled that their daughter's future would become dark. Before the news could turn into a forest fire, they hurried to conduct Seetha's marriage.

“Kuppan returned from the place he had gone to. He moved about in hiding, taking care not to be seen by Seetha's parents. One day Seetha's father called to Kuppan as he went down the street. At the sound of the voice Kuppan was startled. He was bewildered. There was no way to run. He stood thinking, ‘My life is over.’ Then the words ‘Seetha is yours!’ fell on his ears. He could not believe himself. Only those tormented by love can know what such happy news would have felt like to a man who had been trembling in fear that he was about to be beaten to death.” As he said this, Durai looked sidelong at Parimalam. She looked at him. The old man looked at them both and laughed. To himself he said, “Hmm, no interval needed!”—the old man's teasing. Durai's story rose again.

“The wedding of Kuppan and Seetha—the ceremonies are taking place. The bride has come to the dais. Who knows what her heart must have been like? But her face alone had gone pale. Those are the words in Appa's diary. She had not gained the firm courage of believing that she had escaped without telling her lover. When the moment came to tie the thaali, they called the bridegroom. But Kuppan said, ‘I don't want this marriage,’ and began walking out of the wedding pavilion.

“‘This is a drama to tie an unchaste woman around my neck!’ he shouted. He raged like a savage. Everyone ran to him and tried to calm him, but he danced like a man who had stepped on fire. Some crooked family-wreckers must have told him what had happened to Seetha. That alone caused the sudden change. Seetha clutched the feet of the departing Kuppan and screamed. ‘Darling! Darling—forgive me! It happened unexpectedly—against my wishes! Who else but you could my heart ever seek? Do not abandon me,’ Seetha cried. The pitiable young woman, who had stood with a garland-bearing breast swelling at the thought that she would receive a new marriage and begin a new life, cried in terror.

“‘I am a sinner—I am one who gave this body to a sinful deed—I am one who fell prey to a hungry beast. Even so, you must forgive me, darling! You may say that food touched by another belongs in the rubbish. But there is a great difference between such food and this poor woman, darling. When a stolen gold necklace is recovered, does its owner not wear it again around the neck? In that way I am a stolen gold necklace!’ she wailed. She held tightly to his feet. My father—Kumaravadivu—was the mediator there. He too put forward many reasons and argued for Seetha. All those reasons are recorded beautifully in his diary, almost like essays. Kuppan ignored Appa's words as well.

“‘Kuppa! It is because of her desire for you, with the single aim of attaining you, that Seetha hid the disgrace that befell her instead of telling you. It was not something she wished and deliberately did. She is a stolen gold necklace—wear her!’ Appa tried to reconcile them, offering thousands of arguments. His words did not enter Kuppan's ears. Seetha's lament did not change his mind. The marriage did not take place. Kuppan married another woman. Seetha, as though competing with Kuppan, went and became Kangani Kalimuthu's kept woman. Whenever the white proprietor desired, she adorned his inner quarters too.

“In his diary Appa wrote a conclusion about these two lovers. His indelible opinion in that entry was that both were people without hearts. Are you asking what this story has to do with Amma's murder? It has a great deal to do with it. Amma's murder—Appa's changes of mind—everything is intertwined because of a few incidents like this.”
