Durai's friends warned Ayyappan's group, “If you get involved in clandestine-boat business like this again, that will be the end of you! Your heads escaped this time—that itself is a great thing. Run away.” Ayyappan fell at their feet and pleaded humbly, “Saami! You will earn a crore of merit! We're leaving—we won't do this again—let us take that old man with us too; please hand him over.” To Ayyappan, taking the old man away would have been like carrying away the whole treasure of Marungappallam. But he received no such permission. Durai's friends said they could not give him the old man. Afraid that one should not keep talking too much to the police, Ayyappan set off with his men. Durai's friends gently lifted the old man, who had still not recovered from his faint, and carried him into Manora.

* * * *

Durai and Parimalam walked toward Manora across the sandy shore, shoulder against shoulder.

“So you told that Ambalam my name?”

“You think I would tell him!—but he believes I did—do you know whose name I gave him?”

“Whose?”

<!-- source: scan 99 → 100 -->

“That fellow who used to gather a gang behind me and make a nuisance whenever I went to school.”

“Who was that?”

“Have you forgotten already?—one day he came along the street beside me and deliberately fell off his bicycle—I struck him once on the cheek with the scale in my hand; you bought a bangle for the hand that gave him that blow—I refused to wear the bangle, and you sulked because of it—then I quoted a Tirukkural about lovers' sulking and made you laugh—”

“Yes—yes,” Durai nodded.

“Do you remember his name now?”

“Dukkaram! The bicycle-shop fellow—”

“Yes—I gave Velliyambalam his name. He is going to hunt him down and ask where the gold was buried—the fellow is going to stare in bewilderment—and get a fine beating. This is exactly the sort of lesson young men who bare their teeth at women ought to learn—”

“You did well, Parimalam!—”

“Leave that aside—tell me your story, darling!”

“After I was separated from you, I had only one thought: I had to discover who that old man was. So I gathered my friends, put on fake police disguises, and pretended to catch a clandestine boat. Ayyappan and the other smugglers were caught. The old man jumped into the sea. I jumped in too and saved him. He still hasn't recovered from his faint. We questioned Ayyappan about who the old man was. We got no proper information. People only say, ‘an old man who roams the village talking Vedanta’; no one can tell us properly. Parimalam! Somehow a great desire has risen in me to investigate him thoroughly. Until I learn the truth about him, I am not going to do anything else!” said Durai.

<!-- source join: scan 100 → 101 -->

“Is that so?” Parimalam turned her cheek toward him.

“This is the exception to the rule, isn't it!” the teasing fellow said, making an ‘ich’ sound. Just then one of Durai's friends ran up and said, “The old man has opened his eyes.”

Durai and Parimalam ran toward Manora. The old man, utterly exhausted, had opened his eyes and was looking around. Durai and Parimalam went and stood before him.

The old man's eyes fell upon them—even in the dim moonlight he could see them.
