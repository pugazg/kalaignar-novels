and her brother too were left floating in the deep sea—the people who had come to witness a wedding scene received the form of corpses instead. The Vellalar grandees who had successfully completed their conspiracy returned to Marungappallam and searched the blacksmith's house, eager to seize the heaps of gold. But they could not find even a tiny piece. The blacksmith, taking precautions in advance, had buried everything in a place known only to himself. Not only the gold! He had buried the stone that made gold as well. They call it the **kulisakkal**; that was the name given to the stone. Having destroyed the blacksmith's family and then been disappointed at finding no gold, the Vellalars involved in the attempt went to Ceylon and settled on the island called Karai near Jaffna.”

Finishing the story, the old man said, “That is how the tale is told.”

“Yes—but is there no information at all about that stone?” Ayyappan asked eagerly.

“Why? If you found it, would you take it and start making gold?”

“If only the story were true, and all that gold came into my hands—ah!”

“What would you do? As they did for Nataraja of Chidambaram, would you put a golden roof on Thyagaraja of Thiruvarur?”

<!-- source: scan 50 → 51 -->

“A golden roof? Nonsense! I wouldn't do that! I'd make a whole golden chariot!”

“Good! O—vast heaps of gold! As long as intelligent men like this exist, may you never fall into anyone's sight!”

The old man prayed like this toward the sky. Then, looking at Ayyappan, he said, “But one thing, Ayyappa!—when that blacksmith was overturned into the sea—he is said to have had a small stone in his lap and a note telling where the buried gold lay.

“The note was on a small copper plate. They say that if that little stone was taken and placed at the spot marked on the copper plate, it would race toward the place where the kulisakkal was buried as though drawn by a magnet. With it, they say, one could recover both the kulisakkal and the accumulated gold. The blacksmith's younger son apparently used to tell this secret in the village. It seems he had not gone to the wedding. He would often weep that his father always kept the copper plate and the little stone in his lap; they had gone into the sea with him—what could be done now? Who knows how much of this is true?” the old man concluded.

“Ayyo! Couldn't at least that copper plate and little stone fall into my hands?” old Ayyappan shouted in his rasping voice.
