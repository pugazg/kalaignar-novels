# 48

The street play began amid a vast crowd of people. As the leading man of the village, Velliyambalam lay by himself in a reclining chair and enjoyed the performance. Against the background of the Mahabharata battlefield, they staged the episode called **The Slaying of Abhimanyu**. Before the Bharata war could begin, a sacrifice called *kalappali* had to be offered. The people watched with intense interest as the performers debated whom to choose and finally reached the scene in which the great warrior Aravan, Bhima's son, was offered as the *kalappali*. But would Ambalam's mind stay on any of that? His thoughts revolved only around when the clock would strike twelve—when the couple's heads could be cut off—and how their blood could be sprinkled into the well.

He called Raman, who was sitting nearby with fearful devotion and respect, and whispered some secret into his ear. Raman answered rather loudly, “There are guards all around the house—there's no way they can run anywhere!” Ambalam caught hold of his ear and twisted it, warning him, “Don't make a noise.” Then he whispered something more into Raman's ear and sent him away. Raman too left the street play reluctantly, though he had no wish to leave it.

On the stage, preparations began for Aravan's *kalappali*. The actor dressed as Krishna sang privately to Draupadi, mixing spoken dialogue and song: only if the Bharata war took place would the burden of the earth be reduced and Mother Earth's headache ease a little; to lighten the weight on Mother Earth's head, he would arrange the Bharata war and cause the warriors of households to die, leaving behind tender children, young women in their prime and aged parents. When the song ended, the next scene brought Aravan forward, and the Pandavas cut off his head and offered him as the *kalappali*.

At that very moment a great commotion broke out among the people watching the play. Ambalam sprang up in alarm. “Here—I too have offered a *kalappali*! I have offered a *kalappali*!” Thandavan came dancing wildly, laughing a terrifying laugh, his face ghastly and a blood-stained sickle in his hand. The people, trembling with fear at his furious dance, moved aside rather than stand before him. Thandavan went straight towards Ambalam. Ambalam too was afraid, but he mastered himself and stood without moving. Thandavan shouted again:

“Ha, haha! Hey, Ambalam!...... Nobody can do anything to me!...... Look here, a patta sword! Look, man, look at the blood smeared on it!...... Do you know whose blood this is? You ignored Thandavan and high-handedly conducted that marriage; this is the blood of that couple!....... Against the father's wishes, as though you yourself had fathered my girl, you tied her to some orphan fellow; this blade carries that fellow's fresh blood and the loving blood of my green parrot! Look, sinner, look!..... Supreme sinner—you Chandalan who ruins the village community! You thought no one had the courage to oppose you, treated her parents like bits of straw and went looking for a bridegroom for the girl; now look, I opposed you and won—I have taken revenge on that shameless woman who disregarded both mother and father—wedding in the morning!.... Corpse at night! Haha... Velliyambalam! Have you seen Thandavan's courage? Go and see! Go and see the miserable woman who received your blessing and protection and that worthless fellow lying there as corpses! Go, man, go.....”

No one could stop Thandavan. No one could restrain him. He danced like a man possessed.

Velliyambalam had been watching the clock, waiting to sacrifice the lovers. Now that he heard the work was already done, would he not be delighted? Yet without showing the joy rising in his mind, he lamented, “You sinner! Have you killed those loving sweethearts? I knew this would happen—that is why I had guards around them from morning itself...” The street play had broken down completely. The villagers stood bewildered. Thandavan, true to his name, continued his *tandavam*.

As Ambalam wondered what to do, Raman caught his eye. At once Ambalam stirred up the villagers and told them to seize Thandavan and hold him. Ambalam too confronted Thandavan. Surrounded by the villagers, Thandavan quieted down, threw away the knife in his hand and stood still. The villagers seized the opportunity and tied him up.

When Thandavan's wife Anjalai learned what had happened, she came running. Falling upon the bound Thandavan, she cried, “You sinner! You have unjustly killed my daughter!” Then she fainted and collapsed.

Taking the opportunity, Ambalam slipped away in the direction from which Raman was coming. Raman was walking towards him carrying a vessel in his hand. The hands holding it were trembling. Ambalam ran up to him and asked anxiously, “Rama! What is this?”

With a shaking voice Raman said, “Master! Thandavan finished the work before we could.”

“I know that! Are both of them really dead?” Ambalam asked.

“He hacked them to pieces, master, hacked them to pieces,” Raman said, shedding tears.

“Sabash!.... What is this?” Ambalam asked, looking at the vessel.

“This is the blood of both of them—ayyo! Blood poured from both their necks like water from a spring, gushing and gushing! I couldn't bear to look, master! While the two of them were happily kissing each other in the bedroom, that sinful fellow sliced off both their heads like palmyra fruits!”

“Leave that aside! Why did you bring this here?”

“Old Mayandi told me to bring it, so I did. If we wait a little, the blood will clot and then we won't be able to sprinkle it! He told me to bring you to the wellside right now. He also said he would gather all the men and be there ready to take out the treasure, master.”

“Is that so? Come, let's go!” Ambalam said, and set off with Raman towards the well.

## At the well

Raman followed behind him, trembling, with the vessel of blood.

Just as Raman had said, Ambalam's men were standing there. Ambalam looked at them and asked, “Where is Mayandi?”

“Here I am. Victory! Victory!” Mayandi came there shouting joyfully and stood beside Ambalam.

“Did you see, old man? That Thandavan himself has done the work we were supposed to do!” Ambalam laughed.

“Yes!” Mayandi nodded and looked at the vessel in Raman's hand. His face changed.

Noticing it, Ambalam laughed and asked, “What is it, man? Has compassion entered your heart too, like this Raman fellow?”

“No, no! Still, he had been with me for at least a little while—poor fellow, this Durai!” Mayandi said, wiping his eyes. For some reason his body trembled.

“No lovers anywhere in the world could have met an ending like this! Even Ambikapathi and Amaravathi are not said to have died like this. The blood of both of them in a single vessel! For true lovers, this is a fulfilling end! I believe that because we are going to receive such a *punyam*, we shall be freed from the sin of having killed them.” The old man spoke in this troubled manner.

“Why should the sin of murder come to us? It is going to Thandavan, isn't it?” Ambalam mocked, then hurried him: “Let's attend to what has to be done.”

“What has to be done? Only the sprinkling,” Mayandi said. He took the vessel from Raman and handed it to Ambalam.

Looking at his men, Ambalam said, “Hey! The moment I sprinkle this into the well, the treasure will burst out with a bang—all of you fellows be careful!” As he spoke, he scooped blood from the vessel and sprinkled it into the well.

Nothing suddenly burst forth as he had expected. He poured the entire contents of the vessel into the well. There was no response. He looked at Mayandi. Mayandi shook his head as though telling him not to be impatient. Slowly, eagerly, Ambalam leaned over and peered into the well. Something was glittering brightly. Ambalam's eyes widened, and he told everyone to look. They shone a torch into the well. There it was—a large box gleaming brilliantly. Everyone stood peering eagerly into the well.

“What shall we do, Mayandi?” Ambalam asked.

“Only now has what we were expecting begun to emerge. I think if we take this out, the next thing will come,” Mayandi said happily.

Ambalam gave orders to his men. Four or five of them climbed down into the well. “Silver box! Silver box!” the men below shouted. Ambalam and the others kept staring into the well without blinking, their mouths hanging open. The men slowly lifted the box and tied it to a rope lowered from above. Ambalam and the others hauled it up and set it down beside the well.

Unable to contain his excitement, Ambalam ran his hands over the box. Contrary to what the men had shouted, it did not seem to be silver—it must have been lead, or perhaps some other metal. He did not care about that at all. All his concern was for what lay inside. With difficulty he opened the box. Inside it was another box. He opened that. Inside it was yet another box, along with the words: “Open this too—you will see the treasure!” Ambalam's joy knew no bounds. In great haste he opened the third box as well.

Inside it was an ornamented palm-leaf slip. He picked it up and looked at it. Ambalam's face suddenly changed. His face and eyes reddened so fiercely that they seemed to outdo even the red blood still clinging to the vessel. Rubbing his eyes, he read the writing on the palm leaf again. The message was this:

“Human being! Do not wander in search of gold! First make your own heart golden!”

Ambalam could not even remain standing there. “Where is that old Mayandi? He has cheated me! Kill him at once!” Ambalam shouted.

Everyone looked around asking, “Where is Mayandi?” Mayandi was not there. He had gone somewhere. Had he simply left? Or had he too been deceived along with Ambalam and fled in fear that if he stayed any longer they would kill him? Such a question arose in the minds of those who stood there.
