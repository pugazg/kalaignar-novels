# 11

When the old man saw the lovers he had met the previous night, he could hardly believe his own eyes. The police surrounding him and the two of them looking at him with affection seemed to him a great puzzle. Though an overpowering desire rose in him to touch his lap once and make sure everything was there, he did not attempt it. During the sea journey, touching it in that way had earned him Ayyappan's hostility and had almost led to the object in his lap being taken from him. So the old man controlled his feelings and gave the lovers a dry smile. After that smile, Durai began to speak. Sitting close beside him, he asked affectionately, “How is your body feeling?”

“I'm fine—why, are you thinking of carrying me off to the police station? Don't worry. In a few moments I'll be able to get up and walk slowly by myself!” Mayandi said, pressing a hand on Durai's shoulder as he raised himself into a sitting position.

<!-- source: scan 102 → 103 -->

“So you have put Ayyappan in handcuffs, I suppose!” said the old man.

“No. We forgave him and sent him away!”

“You let him go? Then perhaps none of you are policemen at all!”

Durai smiled softly, but without showing anything he said, “We are police! Ayyappan's group begged us. They fell at our feet saying they would never do this again, so we let them go.”

“Oh-ho! Then you won't let me go, because I am not going to beg!” the old man said grandly. Had these people really been police, those words would have earned him a proper reward the moment they left his mouth. The others looked at one another and privately admired the old man's courage. For a little while there was silence. The old man seemed to want to tell the young woman standing before him to sit down.

“Why are you standing, amma?” he asked.

“It's all right,” Parimalam replied in a trembling voice. “Sit down, amma!” the old man said in a single word that conveyed his insistence. Parimalam sat beside him. Now the old man himself did not know what to say.

It was very difficult for him to look at the faces of the lovers whom he himself had attacked and made suffer on Manora's upper level the previous night.

<!-- source: scan 103 → 104 -->

He seemed both to want to recall that incident and to feel embarrassed to recall it. The pain caused by those waves of thought could be seen clearly on his face by both Durai and Parimalam.

Thinking he should change the subject, Durai asked him, “Yes—who is that Ayyappan? Have you known him for a long time?”

“Have you begun the police interrogation right here?” the old man laughed.

“No—if it's troublesome, don't say anything. Take some rest!”

“Rest! This old block will have rest only from now on, it seems—rest, build up its body, and then go save the world!”

“In every one of your remarks there is a thread of weariness with the world. Why?”

“Why ask the reason? Dry leaves hanging toward the earth will naturally speak wearily of the tree's life until the wind snaps their stalks.”

“I sense a difference between the weariness of other old men and yours.”

“You have only now seen an old man like me, which is why you say so. All the old men you have seen until now must have been men playing with grandchildren—or men yearning because there is no grandchild in the house; men tired after living eighty or ninety years, dreaming that if they must live any longer their youth ought to return. Those are the old men you have seen. I am not that kind of old man—I am a mad old man. You know the sort they call a kinnaara old man? That is no one else—it is me!”

<!-- source join: scan 104 → 105 -->

Durai still wanted to talk. But if time passed here, dawn would come and the police disguise would be exposed in daylight. Parimalam was with them too. So he decided they must leave at once. He said to the old man, “All right, come, let's go.” “Where?” the old man asked eagerly. “Nowhere serious—you needn't go to the police station as you imagine. Our house is nearby at Puduppattinam. You can go there and rest.”

“Uh-huh—I don't want to go anywhere.”

“You mustn't speak so firmly. It will be better if you come with us!”

“What trouble is this, ayya? You save a man who was going to die and then stand here taking his life!”

“We are police—we have to do our duty!”

The old man spoke with firmness, and Durai spoke in the same way. Durai understood that unless he spoke like this, he would not be able to take the old man where he wanted. Parimalam felt somewhat distressed at troubling a man of his age.

<!-- source join: scan 105 → 106 -->

“Get up quickly!” Durai ordered.

“Am I the only fool here? Why did you let all the others go and keep hold of only me?”

“We have business with you.”

The answer threw the old man into confusion. Durai had meant something else, but the old man became alarmed. Without realising it, in his agitation his hand felt his lap.

“Hmm—must I really come?”

“Yes. Isn't it best to obey a police order?”

“Is this some new teaching?—you could write a new Aathichudi: ‘Obey the police order’!”

The old man tried to stand. Durai helped him. “Can you walk a short distance and reach that boat?”

“Oh—another sea journey? Hmm—I can walk myself.”

“The sea journey is not very far—only as far as Puduppattinam!”

“Who knows what all this is! You caught the clandestine boat—you beat Ayyappan and drove him away—and now you're dragging me along—and if I ask whether it's to the police station, no! To Puduppattinam!”

<!-- source join: scan 106 → 107 -->

“Your head won't be lost. Come!”

“Only the head mustn't go, appane! As long as I'm alive, I don't care where you throw me, bury me or burn me,” the old man cried.

“All right, all right, come!” Durai said. He led the old man toward the place where the boat lay; Parimalam and the others walked behind. Durai first helped the old man into the boat. All his friends climbed in. Parimalam stood on the shore.

“Hmm—you too!” Durai called. She came closer. He lifted her and dropped her into the boat with a thump. The action itself showed how delighted Durai was. The boat began moving along the coast toward Puduppattinam.

* * * *

The next morning, in Pattukkottai's bazaar street, a voice kept asking everyone who came and went, “Where is Chakravarthi Street?” Whose voice could it have been? It was the voice of Velliyambalam, the man searching for the treasure of gold!

A shopkeeper gave Ambalam very detailed directions to Chakravarthi Street. Ambalam went all around the town and finally found the street. It was the road to the school. At a house on that street he asked, “Where is Dukkaram's bicycle shop?” When the residents told him the shop was at the end of the street, Velliyambalam hurried away in search of it.

<!-- source join: scan 107 → 108 -->

Ten or fifteen bicycles of many colours—red, green, yellow, black, white—stood in a row outside. Some had no brakes; a few stood there missing a wheel. From outside it looked like a flashy shop. A large sign reading “Dukkaram Cycle Company” extended beyond the very width of the shop.

Inside, Dukkaram had seated a few friends on two or three chairs and was talking to them.

He was tall, lean in build, and had a foolish-looking face: those were Dukkaram's identifying features. Thinking he had dressed himself in great splendour, he wore a muslin jibba; it was so thin that the black sleeveless undershirt beneath showed through. A fine thread-like gold chain adorned his long neck. Holding and fondling a puppy in his hand, he talked to his friends.

<!-- source: scan 108 → 109 -->

“What, brother! Life is totally dull!” said Dukkaram.

“Yes, appa—since she left town, Dukkaram has turned into dukkha-Ram, sorrowful Ram!” said one friend.

“Without Parimalam walking down this street, the street itself looks ruined—it looks like a desert!”

“Very soon you'll be struck by Parimalam madness!”

“Even if madness doesn't strike me, I feel as if I'd be satisfied just shouting ‘Parimalam, Parimalam!’”

“They say Arjuna rubbed a stone crying ‘Alli, Alli’; you too rub something like that!”

“This is the sort of advice you fellows give! You say I should rub something like Arjuna did, but will any one of you come and help me the way Krishna helped that Arjuna?”

“That's true, appa! We did what we could—we found out her village for you. Can we carry you to her, the way Arjuna was taken to Alli?”

“Why not? Just admit that you don't have the skill!”

<!-- source: scan 109 → 110 -->

“We'll take you—but then you have to turn into a snake like Arjuna!”

“Get lost, you useless fellows! All right—all right—I'm just going as far as the bazaar and coming back—hey, boy! Watch the shop—hello, helpless friends! I'm going. We'll meet this evening,” Dukkaram said, mounting his bicycle with his puppy and riding away. The bicycle had gone only a little distance when, opposite him, Ambalam asked a boy where Dukkaram's shop was. The boy pointed at Dukkaram and said, “There he is, coming on the bicycle!” and went away.

Ambalam hurried forward and raised a hand for Dukkaram to stop. Dukkaram got off the bicycle and asked eagerly, “Who are you? What do you want?”
