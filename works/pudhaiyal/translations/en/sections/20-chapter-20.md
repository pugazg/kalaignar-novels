# 20

“The swami and the two of us crossed the sea by clandestine boat and reached the southern coast. At Saluva Nayakkan Pattinam, my sister and I rejoiced at seeing that seven-storeyed tower rising high by the shore. Thangam did not even feel like leaving it. I wanted to climb it and look around. The swami carefully protected us and took us all the way to the upper level. Periyavare! My sister and I stood on that very tower where we first met you and looked out at the sea. Thangam jumped with delight. The swami kept joking with us and encouraging us. Even a butcher feeds goats and cattle, because they must remain alive until the next day when he cuts them up and sells them. The swami's many forms of hospitality toward us were just like that. Then he slowly led us away on foot. We followed.

“I walked holding my sister with one hand, blessing the man in front of us with the thought that this holy man had come to give life to the two of us who had been abandoned as orphans in Ceylon. Thangam was only a little girl—a ten-year-old shoot. Could she compete with us in walking? She staggered. Seeing it, the swami lifted my sister onto his shoulder with affection and tenderness. Yes, Periyavare, he placed Thangam on his shoulder. I did not then know that the sinner was going to place my treasure upon a sharp blade! We travelled enjoying the fields where green shoots waved, the young trees stretching out green hands, and the little channels flowing here and there through sluices. The swami told story after story about the surrounding villages and their temples and tanks.

“He said there was a monastery in a nearby village called Nadiyam, that the monastery belonged to northerners, that religious leaders from the north had served there as heads of the monastery one after another for many generations, and that all of them were known to him. It is true that there is a monastery in Nadiyam village. It is also true that it is under the authority of northern heads. How true his claim that the head was his friend may have been, I do not know. Talking all the way, we reached Marungappallam village. He said we would stay that night in the Siva temple there, go to the Nadiyam monastery in the morning, and then, with the head's recommendation, he would obtain a good job for me and make arrangements for Thangam's education.

“I worried that the two of us had become a burden upon an ascetic who had renounced the world. ‘Swami! Why should you show us such concern? Who are we? Who are you?’ I asked sadly.

“‘What did you say, appane! Who are you? Who am I? We are all Lords! We ourselves are gods! One life living in many cages! Prr! The milk of one cow poured into many vessels! If one living being suffers, that suffering affects every living being. Only holy men realise this. I am a sadhu who has realised it. That is why I have set out to give you a life,’ the swami preached.

“Then all three of us went to the Siva temple there. Though it was dark, a lamp burned with a small flicker. Standing at the temple entrance with folded hands, the swami sang devotional songs. He danced by himself and acted as though he had merged with God. He asked us too to pray. Little Thangam folded her tender hands, looked toward the sanctum, and in a baby voice dripping with sweetness asked, ‘God, mother! Give me back our grandmother, our amma, our appa—all of them!...’ Even in that dim light I saw tears fall and scatter down the slopes of my Thangam's cheeks like drops of dew breaking upon lotus petals. I consoled myself.

“We ate a little each from the food the swami had bundled and carried. ‘Keep some left—we need to eat in the morning,’ my little parrot said teasingly. Ayyo, the parrot did not know that what she was eating then would be her last meal. After eating, the three of us lay down on the platform opposite the temple. Because of the tiredness of our walk, sleep quickly began to overcome us. The swami took down the long large bundle he had carried on his shoulder and lay beside us. The music of frogs setting out to wander at night, the calls of birds, and the noises of field insects all sounded frightening. I lay embracing Thangam. I knew only that she was afraid. I did not ask, ‘Are you frightened?’ because I thought the question itself might frighten her more. I asked the swami whether we might sleep at someone's house nearby.

“‘This is a wicked village, appa! You must not go into those houses! At night especially it is very dangerous,’ the swami replied. Thinking of all sorts of things, I fell asleep.

“It must have been midnight when I woke with a start. Thangam, who had been sleeping in my embrace, was gone. Shocked, I raised my head and looked. The swami too was gone. I sat up. I felt like screaming. I screamed, called out, cried aloud—but even I could not hear those cries. I understood that only my mouth was opening and no sound was coming out. I shut my eyes tightly and tried to give myself courage. My young body trembled violently. I could clearly hear my heart beating ‘tak, tak!’ I rose and tried to steady myself, asking whether I was really standing. I took a step. I was able to open my eyes wide and look. I looked. I heard a sound and listened. ‘Between the riser and the worshipper—under the shadow of the crow's beak—pure gold! Pure gold!! Pure gold!’ The words kept sounding again and again.

“In that night, silent and terrifying at the same time, could a young man standing alone and orphaned possibly bear hearing such a sound? I strengthened my mind and moved slowly toward where the sound came from.

“‘Devi! I have given your offering. Why still this delay? Even after a human sacrifice, will you test this dog? Let the treasure come! Bring the treasure and show it! Mother! Mother!! Where is the treasure? Where is the treasure?’ The words shook me. I stepped a little faster. A lamp was burning. I looked sharply.” Durai now struggled, unable to continue the story. His lips trembled; his eyes became pools. Parimalam's soft, flower-like body trembled. Old Mayandi, who had been pacing here and there while listening to Durai's entire story in deep thought, stood motionless like a statue. The old man and Parimalam waited eagerly, and at the same time with weeping hearts, for what Durai was about to say. Durai continued.

“Beside the dim lamp the swami sat. In his hand was a blood-soaked sickle. Perhaps that was what he had hidden in the large bundle. Thinking, what is done is done—what now? I went behind the swami and looked more carefully. My sister's body lay in two pieces. I saw Thangam's head on one side and her trunk on the other, blood dripping from them. What brother could endure the sight of the beautiful picture that was his precious sister lying mutilated like this? I screamed—but as before my mouth closed upon itself; my eyes dimmed; I could not take even one step.

“The head Amma used to comb and decorate so lovingly lay severed. Those rose-red cheeks reddened again and again by grandmother's kisses, the beautiful eyes and their mischievous glance—everything—yes, my sister, my casket of treasure—had been shattered. Thinking I was walking forward, I had moved far backward and struck a tree. Only then did awareness return. I stood and thought. My sister could no longer live—she had been cut apart. What could I do now? How could I punish this traitor? I kept thinking. ‘All right! Cut him down here in the same place!’ With that decision I walked toward him. He was then placing my sister's mutilated body in a pit and covering it with earth.

“I stood as her brother and watched my sister's final rites. What remained now? Only me! With the decision that I must destroy him—or die in the attempt to destroy him—I crept toward him like a cat. He had buried Thangam well. In front of him stood an image—perhaps Kali, perhaps some other terrifying figure; I could not tell clearly. He spat at its face. ‘You wretched creature! Trusting you, believing you would reveal the treasure, I wandered in search of a human sacrifice and brought one—and you cheated me!’ He threw the image down and kicked it. Shouting, ‘Treasure! Where is the treasure?’ he smashed the image to pieces. When all his attention had turned toward the image, I ran to him and lifted the cruel sickle lying there. The swami was startled. He ran; I chased. One blow! I burned to finish him!

“The fleeing swami screamed, ‘Ayyo!’ By then I had reached him. Escaping my sickle, he lost his footing and fell into a ruined well. I stood above the well and stared. I listened to see whether any sound came. I heard a groan: ‘Ayyo! Ayyo!’

“After that, even that stopped. I lifted the large stones lying outside and threw them into the well. Then I came to the place where my sister had been buried. I cried and kissed the soil. I did not want to dig up my buried jewel again. I kissed the blood of my sister stuck to the sickle and pressed it to my eyes. Then I threw the sickle too into the well where the swami had fallen.

“After that I did not remain there. I walked wherever my feet carried me.”
