# 7

Night is the best time for clandestine boats that want to reach the coast of Ceylon. The Ceylon shore may be roughly forty miles from Manora. If the wind blows favourably, one can reach shore in two hours. Otherwise the journey is delayed according to the wind. The clandestine boat in which old Mayandi travelled received no help from the wind. Ayyappan struggled desperately to reach shore somehow before dawn. He flew at the men in the boat and shouted in his naturally frightening voice.

However much he fussed, the boat could not move quickly. Was it an ordinary matter for a boat that had to go east to cut through the sea at speed against the wind?

Ayyappan decided they could not reach the Ceylon shore before daylight. At once he issued an urgent order to the men.

“Hey boys! Your showing-off won't work any more! Stop the boat right here properly—we'll leave as soon as it gets dark—”

At his command the boat was stopped.

“How many miles would it be from here, appa?” old Mayandi asked.

<!-- source: scan 69 → 70 -->

“About ten miles—why? Thinking of swimming there?” Ayyappan laughed.

“What strength is there in my body to swim? If you wished, you could swim—and leap too. Can I?”

“You mischievous old man! Here—eat this—” Ayyappan opened a bundle.

It contained packed rice and fried dried fish.

“Excellent dried fish! See how splendidly it smells! Hold on to the smell and push some of this rice into your stomach—hmm!” he said, holding it toward the elder.

The hungry old man began eating. The men aboard also hurried to fulfil the duty of filling their stomachs. The boat stood in mid-sea, rocking like a swing under the blows of the waves. Ayyappan ate until his belly was full and gave a huge belch. Even while the belch escaped him, his sharp gaze remained fixed on Mayandi's lap. While telling the story of the blacksmith's daughter being overturned into the sea, Mayandi had unconsciously felt his own lap.

Ayyappan, who had noticed this, burned to learn what was hidden there. Curiosity kept driving him. Because his suspicion had settled on that object, he hesitated greatly before suddenly asking, “What is in your lap, ayya?” He controlled himself somehow and began indirectly.

<!-- source: scan 70 → 71 -->

“Mayandi—give me betel leaf and areca nut, ayya!”

“Where would I have betel leaf and areca nut?”

“You've kept some in your lap, haven't you?”

“That isn't betel and areca nut!”

“Then what is it? Let's see!”

Ayyappan reached toward his lap. Before he could touch it, the elder recovered himself. “Ayyappa! Don't start extending your hand into all that. You wanted betel leaf and areca nut, didn't you? I don't have any. Leave it there!”

“Why? Am I not allowed to put my hand near you?”

“Ayyappa—if you misunderstand the fact that I behave with you as an equal, I will get angry. That is all I have to say.”

“Oh, great man! What has happened to you because you mixed with me as an equal, ayya? We mustn't even stretch out a hand and touch this gentleman! If you die and fall into the sea, then who is going to touch you and lift you? As though you have endless caste relations, kin and children!”

“What if I have no children or relatives? Do we need caste, kin and children merely to cry when we die? Since the day the world began, human beings have gone on crying one after another. Let even that custom disappear in my death.”

<!-- source: scan 72 -->

“Oi, Vedanti—why are you stretching the talk? What is that weight in your lap, ayya? If I ask, tell me........”

“There is no need for me to tell you—and no need for you to ask—”

“You speak like one chop, two pieces!”

“The need has arisen for me to speak that way! So I do!”

Ayyappan did not want to probe the elder further. But he understood that some important object was in Mayandi's lap. So that Mayandi would not begin suspecting him, he changed the subject.

“Hmm—we have to lie here until ten tonight!”

“Ayyo—that long?” asked the elder.

“Yes. Only if we reach shore in the middle of the night can we land without being seen by anyone—” Ayyappan said, leaning back on an onion sack. Old Mayandi too sank into thought. Though the heat of the sun troubled him, he pretended not to mind it. The other men in the boat also leaned on the bundles. Seeing everyone begin to rest, Ayyappan shouted angrily,

<!-- source: scan 72 → 73 -->

“Hey, hey! If every one of you curls up and sleeps, what happens? At least one fellow sit up and keep looking around!”

“I'll watch—I won't lie down—don't be afraid, Ayyappa!” the old man said.

Ayyappan leaned back on the bundle again. All his thoughts revolved around the object in old Mayandi's lap.

Mayandi's thoughts, however, circled around the lovers he had met the previous night.

Remembering again and again how circumstances had forced him to trouble those love-doves who had crossed his purposeless journey, the old man kept sighing. The whole of that day passed for him in mid-sea, amid thought and sighs.

Night came. Marungappallam had sunk into sleep. In the peaceful night Parimalam lay sighing, thinking that only her own eyes remained unclosed. She did not know then that many eyes in Marungappallam were awake that night because of her. In her own house two eyes were flashing frighteningly without her knowledge. Parimalam asked herself how tangled her future might become. Thandavan, meanwhile, growled to himself that he had already reached a good decision about her future. Parimalam's mother Anjalai was sleeping soundly.

<!-- source: scan 73 → 74 -->

Thandavan had become certain that his daughter was ruined. His daughter had formed a relationship with some fellow and destroyed the family's honour; to stop this from growing, he decided it would be best to kill her. A gleaming knife lay beside him. He took it in his hand. He rose to cut the crystal-statue-like Parimalam into pieces. Where had the blood-bond between father and daughter gone and hidden itself? People say there is no affection greater than blood affection—had that become false in Thandavan's case? Yes—it had. The gleam of the knife itself declared it.

Thandavan came with the knife to the room where Parimalam lay. One second—and her beautiful body would be cut apart.

His body had become a pool of sweat. He gripped the knife properly. He placed a foot inside the room. Enter—one stroke—murder—the mind would become peaceful. That was Thandavan's decision. He entered with the knife raised—but what did he see there?

Some men were tying Parimalam up and lifting her. Seeing Thandavan enter, they sprang upon him at once and bound his mouth, hands and legs. Parimalam's mouth too was tied so that she could not speak. A rough old man hoisted Parimalam onto his shoulder and ran. Before Anjalai, awakened by the commotion, could rise screaming, the rough men had fled with Parimalam. In great haste Anjalai untied her husband. Thandavan understood nothing.

<!-- source: scan 75 chapter-7 portion -->

“What is this! Where is Parimalam?”

Thandavan did not answer.

“Did the Mohini spirit carry her away again?” Anjalai screamed.

“Chi! Keep quiet—” Thandavan barked.
