# 46

When Raman heard that the kudukuduppai fortune-teller had been taken away to jail, he was desperate to find out what condition Shokasundari was in. Ambalam was pacing the whole house, absorbed in some thought, so Raman could not immediately do what he wanted.

Ambalam looked at Raman and said, “Hey—take some snacks to the woman in that room!” The moment Ambalam went towards the front veranda, Raman sprang forward and picked up the plate of snacks. He told himself that Ambalam was asking him to feed his beloved snacks just as one might feed a goat that was going to be sacrificed. Had that fortune-teller told Shokasundari about the danger awaiting her? What had happened to Shokasundari after hearing it? He went on lamenting over such thoughts. “Ayyo! Why did my beloved have to come here and get trapped? Could she have come here out of a desire to see me? I only just saw her a little while ago—could she not bear even that much separation? She came here and got caught like this!” Wailing in this manner, he set off carrying the plate of snacks.

Ambalam, who had gone towards the veranda, came back towards him and handed him the key to the room. “Hey, Rama! Open the room and do whatever she needs. She is new here! She might be shocked if she thinks she has been shut in. Then the whole matter will be spoiled. You must behave as though she does not even know she has been locked in,” he said, and went back towards the entrance. Thoughts and plans were bursting inside his head.

Raman was delighted at the sight of the key. He rejoiced that he now had an opportunity to go near his beloved and speak to her. Since the key was in his hand, he also began planning somehow to send Shokasundari out during the night itself. He sighed with happiness at the thought that Ambalam had been fooled where he was concerned.

With the key in one hand, the snacks in the other, and his whole heart carrying the burden of thoughts of his beloved, Raman ran towards the room.

He hurriedly opened the door. “Darling!” he called as he looked inside.

Parimala stood there wearing Shokasundari's clothes and jewellery. Raman recognized Parimala. Even so, he was caught in bewilderment. Those were the very clothes his beloved had worn! Those were the very jewels she had worn! But how had the face alone changed? Perhaps, in the intoxication of love, had he failed to look at her face properly? he asked himself.

Parimala was startled when she saw Raman. But she recovered and asked, “What is it, Rama? What's the news?”

“Am I dreaming?” Raman said, absent-mindedly letting the plate of snacks in his hand fall to the floor and standing like a tree. It took him several minutes to regain his senses.

“Did you come looking for Shokasundari?” Parimala asked. At once Raman cried, “Yes! Where is my beloved?”

“Didn't you plead with Malayalam Bhagavathi to save Shokasundari?”

“Yes! How do you know that?” He sank into astonishment. Where had Shokasundari gone? How had Parimala come here? He could find no answers to those questions. His small head did not have the strength to bear such enormous confusion.

“Where is my beloved? Where?” he kept asking, overflowing with anxiety.

“I set her free. Your beloved escaped!” Parimala reassured him.

“How did she escape?” Raman was desperate to know.

Parimala had no choice but to tell him the whole story. She first laid the groundwork for it.

“Rama! I am the one who saved your beloved. If I had not been here, your beloved could not have escaped with her life. So you must do me a favour. It is not a very great favour. You must not reveal any of the secrets I tell you!”

Raman agreed, saying “All right,” and stood eagerly waiting to learn how his beloved had escaped.

Parimala told Raman that she herself had come as the kudukuduppai fortune-teller, that she had come that way in order to free her Durai, that after arriving she had found it necessary to free Shokasundari, and that for that purpose she had put the fortune-teller's disguise on Shokasundari and sent her outside.

The moment Raman heard that Shokasundari had gone out in the Kudukuduppandi disguise, he jumped as though he had stepped on fire. “Ayyo! Was it my darling who went away in the Bhagavathi disguise? Aha, disaster has struck!” he lamented.

<!-- SOURCE ODDITY: the audited Tamil first says Shokasundari was sent out in the fortune-teller/Kudukuduppandi disguise, then Raman calls it the Bhagavathi disguise. The inconsistency is preserved rather than silently repaired. -->

Parimala understood nothing. She calmed Raman and asked him for the details. Raman revealed that Ambalam had tried to place the blame for Menaka's murder on the fortune-teller so that he himself could escape, and that he had succeeded—that Shokasundari had now fallen victim to it. He sobbed uncontrollably as he spoke.

“Did Shokasundari already know that they were going to accuse the fortune-teller?” Parimala asked Raman.

“She knew! She knew! I was the one who told her!” Raman cried.

Parimala understood what had happened. It became clear to her that Dukkaram had once again come forward to sacrifice himself in order to free her. She did not want to explain to Raman that Shokasundari was Dukkaram. The fact that Dukkaram, who had escaped from prison, had become Shokasundari and had now gone back to prison as the fortune-teller shook Parimala's heart.

Raman, meanwhile, suffered at the thought that his beloved had been shut inside a prison. Parimala consoled him.

“Rama! It was for your sake that I wanted to save your beloved's life. I am going to die together with Durai; I tried to send her out because she must not die. It was only because you were crying and lamenting that I put the Bhagavathi disguise on her. I had absolutely no idea that such an accusation was going to fall on Bhagavathi! Had I known, I would never have done it. Rama! Do you think I deliberately betrayed your beloved? If that had been my intention, would I have saved her from death? Would I have intended to die in her place? Think about it, Rama; all this happened without my knowing!” Parimala wept.

Outwardly she asked, “Would I betray your beloved?” But inwardly she groaned, “Ayyo, I have betrayed my brother Dukkaram!” Dukkaram's spirit of sacrifice submerged her in a sea of tears. In her mind she prostrated herself in the direction he had gone, worshipped him and blessed him. He alone is a man! He alone is a man!—the lips of her heart kept uttering.

Raman believed Parimala's words. He sympathized with her. The hatred he already felt towards Ambalam grew even greater because of what Parimala had said. And when he thought that Ambalam was also the reason his beloved had been sent to jail, Raman's fury crossed all bounds. “No matter what, I will free my beloved. My beloved is not the criminal; Ambalam is the murderer—I will lay down the cloth and cross it. I will swear it! I will even embrace the heat!” Raman cried, dancing with blazing, unbearable rage.

Parimala calmed his anger a little and asked him to tell Ambalam a lie about her.

“What is so difficult about a lie? I'll free you from here tonight itself—don't be afraid!” Raman said, showing her the room key in his hand.

“I do not want to be freed. I want to die together with my Durai. Rama! Do not try to free me! I am going with him! Somehow free only your beloved! One more thing, Rama. I said you must tell Ambalam a lie—do you know what it is? Tell him that the person we thought was Shokasundari is actually Parimala, and make him believe it. Do you understand? Tell Ambalam firmly that it was Parimala who came in the disguise of Shokasundari! Will you tell him, Rama?”

Parimala pleaded with Raman. Raman nodded. “I will say exactly that.”

“It will be good only if you tell him so. Otherwise your beloved will be in danger. If they begin searching for where Shokasundari has gone, I will be forced to tell the truth—won't I?” Parimala again spoke craftily.

Raman said that he would tell Ambalam exactly as Parimala had instructed and moved away from there. His heart, which had been spoiled through association with Ambalam, was transformed by Parimala's tears and the memory of his beloved. How could he rescue his beloved? How could he expose Ambalam? With these thoughts Raman came running to Ambalam.

Seeing Raman run, Ambalam asked anxiously, “What is it?”

“The one in the room—Parima—Parimala!” Raman shouted.

“What? Parimala?” Ambalam was startled.

“Yes! Yes!”

“Don't babble, fool! Isn't it Shokasundari who is there? How could Parimala have come here?”

“That is the mistake! You did not look at the face properly. It is Parimala herself! She has cunningly come pretending to be Shokasundari in order to free Durai.”

When Raman insisted on this, Ambalam too became confused. He wondered whether perhaps, in his haste, he himself had been fooled. Taking Raman with him, he ran towards the room.

Yes; it was Parimala who was there! He stared closely at her face. She tried to hide it. Ambalam's suspicion grew stronger.

“Don't hide your face! Look at me!” he shouted like a wild buffalo. As though startled and trembling, Parimala stood without hiding her face.

It was Parimala herself! Ambalam's doubts vanished. He patted Raman approvingly. Parimala too was satisfied that Raman had done as she had told him. Ambalam began spreading his web of trickery.

“Parimala! Why must you come in disguise and tell lies about being Shokasundari—Keekasundari and all that! Don't you know that my mind has changed regarding both you and Durai? Is it not as atonement for the hardships I caused you that I have been running around trying to get the two of you married? Never mind; the matter is about to succeed. The time has come for you and your lover to live happily from now on. God has somehow brought you here. Durai, who is pining for you, need worry no longer. I am going to make the two of you husband and wife very soon. Don't worry!” Saying this, Ambalam locked the room door, took the key and left.

* * * *

That evening the villagers were gathered in the village public hall under Ambalam's chairmanship. Ambalam and Mayandi whispered something into one another's ears. Thandavan too was at the meeting. Not knowing why everyone had suddenly been summoned, the villagers sat wide-eyed.

Ambalam began explaining why he had called the villagers together. “Thandavan's daughter Parimala and a young man named Durai are lovers. Because Thandavan would not permit that love, Parimala ran away from the village itself. Had she remained in the village, Thandavan would have hacked her to death. Yesterday I caught Parimala, who had run away, and Durai, and I am keeping them in my house. The two of them wish to marry. I too want them to marry. We need Thandavan's opinion.”

The moment Ambalam said this, Thandavan rose in a fury and roared, “Impossible! Impossible!! Where are the two of them? Show them to me! I will chop those dogs into pieces and throw them away!”

Ambalam went close to Thandavan. “Thandava! What is this? Has your anger still not cooled? Have you forgotten that Parimala is the only daughter born to you? The man she loved by her own choice—”

He had not finished before Thandavan interrupted. “Absolutely impossible! What justice is this? You tell me yourselves: just because she wants him, am I to give her to some wandering fellow who came running from who knows where? Thandavan may die if he must, but he will never become father-in-law to that brute!” Thandavan jumped so violently that even his topknot came loose.

Ambalam was not one to give way either. Would his heart, which had waited so long, agree to lose the treasure now within arm's reach merely because of Thandavan's anger? “What is this, man! You still behave like an old-fashioned innocent who knows nothing of the world! Do you think this is the age when you and Anjalai were married? Think about it, man. Parimala must be given to Durai. Otherwise you will see her only as a corpse hanging from a tree branch—or floating in a flooded river—understand? They are only youngsters; don't spoil their desire. Yes, I am telling you plainly!” Ambalam said, carefully remembering a line he had once heard and enjoyed in some street play.

Thandavan stood without speaking.

“Anger cannot do anything to love, man! Do you know the story? In the land of London, they say, there was a man named Laila who loved a woman named Qais. A hot-tempered father just like you stopped them. Do you know what happened in the end? Both of them jumped into the sea and died, they say…… I will not allow such a situation to happen in Parimala's case; yes!” Ambalam spun out some such story.

The jungle king spoke of the flavour of love. Mayandi too approved. The village people sitting there open-mouthed went “tch-tch” in sympathy. They were people who did not know how to contradict Ambalam's speech. So they said, “Getting them married is the right thing.” But, just as expected, Thandavan did not yield even a little. He beat his chest and declared that his next task would be to cut Durai and Parimala to pieces.

“Who are you to stop love? I will conduct their marriage no matter what!” Ambalam thundered.

Unable to argue against Ambalam, Thandavan walked out of the meeting.

Ambalam explained to the villagers the pure love between Durai and Parimala and asked everyone to cooperate with their marriage. That flock of sheep nodded its heads. He also warned the villagers that Thandavan might cause some danger to Durai and Parimala, and that the two should be watched carefully.

The meeting dispersed. Ambalam told Mayandi that they could conduct the marriage immediately, kill the couple that very night and offer them as the sacrifice, and then place the blame for the murder on Thandavan. He said that this was precisely why he had called the villagers together and made Thandavan's opposition public.

Old Mayandi too praised Ambalam's idea and expressed his delight.
