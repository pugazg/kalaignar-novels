“I am your father! I am the father who was separated from you for so many years. When you and your beloved Pamala stood on that tower by the blue-curtained seashore, the man who suddenly attacked and terrified you was your father. This mysterious old man who is an incomprehensible riddle to you and to your woman is your appan, my son! I am the father who filled diary after diary and was betrayed by those very diaries. I am the head who remains of that helpless family which once lived in happiness amid enormous wealth, attendants, men and influence, then fled to Ceylon without even a place to stay and met suffering after suffering in life. My sprout of the clan, I am the father who begot you—this man standing here in the garb of a mendicant!”

Durai grieved that old Mayandi had not embraced him and spoken those very words.

He longed, thinking that even if the old man had lied and said, “I am your father,” his mind might have found a little peace at that moment. With longing piled upon longing he asked in a trembling voice, “Then where, how and when did my father die? At least tell me that.”

The old man looked at him with affection.

“Durai! You ask for medicine for a wound. Do not ask me to pierce it with a spear. I cannot!”

“What are you saying, elder?”

“The picture of your life that you painted before me is itself a great flood of sorrow. Must I tell you the story of your father's death as well and double that sorrow? No. Your father Kumaravadivu is dead. Leave it at that.”

“What is this, elder? When news of a warrior's death comes from a battlefield, they at least tell his family what happened. You give me only the ending in a single line!”

“Yes. Kumaravadivu's life too was a battlefield. But he did not stand bravely and fight. Like a coward he feared the world, kept withdrawing and withdrawing, and in the end became a corpse.”

“Please tell me how my father died. Let his death not remain a whirlpool of mystery unknown even to his son. Do not think I will collapse in grief. I have read every chapter of the serial tragedy that happened in my family. I saw my mother, grandmother and sister as corpses. My heart has gone numb. You may tell me boldly how my father died.”

“You are ready to hear it! The difficulty is for me to make myself ready to tell it. You are Kumaravadivu's son. I was his friend. In prison and outside it, he and I lived together. I was beside him when he died. I cannot reveal why he died. My heart heaves even when I remember it. If I begin to tell it... that will be the end of me!”

“Instead of suppressing my curiosity you are provoking it even more! You must tell me!”

“I will not break the promise I gave Kumaravadivu. I will not turn from friend into traitor. His death was his own affair. A supreme secret that should never become public is buried inside that death. If I reveal it, I will earn a place among those who destroyed the good code of friendship.”

“Elder—you are going to tell his son, not some outsider!”

“To say even more, after we left prison Kumaravadivu and I did many things together. We did nothing that does not happen in the world. What we involved ourselves in was far less than the measure in which such things occur. Even so, merely because he is dead now I will not tell them outside and fill my own heart with poison.”

“You needn't tell me all that, ayya. I know what sort of things must have happened.”

“What do you think happened, Durai? I can understand your imagination. Do you suppose Kumaravadivu thought: Valliyamma suspected me for no reason, then stubbornly refused to speak to me, and because of that an entire family's direction was changed; she suspected me anyway and died without ever having that suspicion cleared, so now I may roam as I wish and circle every woman I see? That is not it! Then what? Are you thinking that perhaps he sought liquor as medicine for peace of mind? Not that either!”

“Whatever it was, I do not care! Just tell me how my father died. That is enough!”

“A snake bit him and he died. Is that enough?”

“A snake?”

“Are you asking what kind of snake? I will tell you that too—a cobra!”

“How did the snake bite him?”

“Mad fellow, don't you know? It raised its hood, pressed its fangs into his leg. The poison in the snake's head came down through the hollow in the fang into his leg, then went up to his head... faint...”

“Enough, enough, elder! What secret is there in saying my father died from a snakebite? Why hide it so much?”

“What—looks as though you will not let go.”

“I won't. Tell me the truth. My father did not die from a snakebite. There is some trick here. Do not hide it from me.”

“It was a snake, appa—and a snake from a Shiva temple!”

“A Shiva-temple snake! Elder, why torture me like this? If you cannot tell me the truth about my father's death, keep quiet. Do not throw useless riddles at me.” Durai spoke somewhat harshly, his voice mixed with grief, and moved away.

“Look, Parimalam—your darling keeps his anger right on the tip of his nose. Mm. How are you ever going to run a household with Durai?” the old man said calmly to Parimalam.

Parimalam, herself caught in the storm, looked at the old man and said, “What is this stubbornness of yours at such an old age?”

“Argument and stubbornness come only in old age, don't they, Parimalam? If it were something I could tell, wouldn't I have told it already?”

Hearing this, Durai said angrily, “Yes—a thing that cannot be told, such a shameful thing! People have written and published whole books full of things more shameful than this. Some wandering sort of people take pen in hand simply to make four coins by writing things that never happened. Yet he makes such a fuss about telling something that actually happened!”

“Then do you want me too to join that middle-of-the-street crowd you mentioned, Durai?”

“I am not asking you to write a Ramayana about my father. There is nothing wrong in telling a son about his father. Am I even asking for the whole story? I am saying the final chapter alone is enough.”

“There is a difference between saying a house fell and saying a house burned. Whether it falls or burns, it is true that the house is ruined. But if one says the house fell, one may simply say that some old house collapsed in wind or rain. If one says the house burned, questions arise: How did it burn? Why did it burn? Did someone deliberately set fire to it? Did fire spread from the stove in the wind? At night did a lamp-flame leap and catch the thatch? Many questions are born. A burning creates more questions than a fall, and has more connected events. Kumaravadivu's death was like a house that burned. It had many connected events. If I do not tell them, you cannot understand the details of his death. And telling them is difficult for me.”

“I am asking for the last time. Are you going to tell me or not?”

“What sort of trouble is this? Didn't I already say a snake bit him and he died?”

“You said it was a Shiva-temple snake. Why did my father go to that temple?”

“For darshan of the deity!”

“I never saw Appa go to a temple. He wrote in his diary that the heart itself is the temple.”

“I know that he worshipped. His devotion to God increased after he came out of prison.”

“That cannot have happened! When that wretched God kept his eyes closed while a man like my father was shut in prison, there is no reason my father should have developed devotion toward him!”

“If he had not become devout, would he have run from temple to temple...”

The old man suddenly stopped. He realised that his tongue had slipped.

“What? Why did you stop halfway?” Durai asked eagerly.

The old man cleared his throat, steadied himself and said, “Durai! It seems to me now that the just thing is to tell you what really happened.” His trembling was still plainly visible.

“Durai! I will tell you. I no longer have this stubborn insistence that I will not. But if you and Parimalam reveal the secret I tell you...”

“We will not reveal it. We will preserve it as a secret in our hearts, elder!”

“All right. Without worrying about that, I shall tell you the details.”

“Tell us.”

“It is true that a snake in a Shiva temple bit Kumaravadivu. It is also true that he died there. Do you know which Shiva temple? The Shiva temple at Marungappallam!”

“The Shiva temple at Marungappallam? The same Marungappallam where my sister's body sleeps?”

“Yes. Your father fell dead there. It happened at midnight, at the hour of deep sleep. I was there with him.”

“Why did you go to the temple at night?”

“No special reason. He and I went there to steal.”

“Ayyo!”

“For four years he and I had already earned a name as thieves. Great idols would move when we put our hands to them. By idols I mean that our work was to take the gold bars and diamonds buried inside or beneath them. Images would be moved. Coins buried under idols would be swept away. Kumaravadivu was highly skilled at it. He was the one who taught me this trade of theft. We even stole images and sold them in shops. But do not imagine that we alone ate and fattened ourselves on the coins we took.”

“Is that so?”

“Yes. What was wrong with it? We had no money in hand. We asked God. He had no hands with which to give it to us, so we took it ourselves.”

“Mm—and then?”

“What next? After our living expenses, we gave the remainder to the suffering poor. One day Kumaravadivu made a plan: we would overturn the idol in the Shiva temple at Marungappallam. I tried to stop him. He would not listen. ‘If we move the idols in Mari and Kali temples, we get only a little. The Marungappallam temple was built in a king's time; there must be much more beneath that image. Come, let's go,’ he said. I argued as much as I could. I could not win.

“‘People in the village say it is a powerful temple. They say merely touching the idol would burn a man into water,’ I told him.

“‘We shall see that power too,’ he said and set off. I had no choice but to go with him. We went. Breaking the lock, he entered the Shiva temple. I remained outside on watch.

“Suddenly Kumaravadivu screamed, ‘Ayyayyo!’ I went inside. A snake had coiled around him. He shook it off and ran outside. ‘I feel faint,’ he cried, fell down—and died.”
