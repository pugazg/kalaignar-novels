# 10

Ambalam stood eagerly, delighted that Parimalam was about to reveal her lover's name; yet his eyes did not fail to look through the window toward the sea. The boats were coming very fast. Ambalam grew restless and shouted, “Tell me! Tell me!”

“Promise that no danger at all will come to him,” said Parimalam.

“I swear by the king of the sea—no danger will come—oh, you Saturn, hurry up and say it......?”

“I'll tell you. Hey, king of the sea! This great man has sworn upon you. If he breaks his word, if any danger comes to my lover—then I'll come asking you.”

“Just tell me quickly!”

“Don't hurry—his name!”

“Hmm—his name? What......?”

“He....... oh...... ayyayyo...... I've forgotten! Yes—is it proper to say one's husband's name?”

“There's no sin in an emergency!”

“What emergency has come to me now?” Even in sorrow she did not fail to tease him.

“You have no danger—I'm the one in danger!” he said, looking through the window at the sea. He saw that the boats would reach the shore in only a few minutes. Turning back to Parimalam, he said, “Tell me quickly, amma! Before marriage there is no sin in saying it.”

“Oh-ho, is there such an exemption? Then must I necessarily tell you the name of my beloved Durai?”

“Yes—yes—yes—” Ambalam said, striking his own head. Even amid her sorrow, the sight made Parimalam laugh.

“Are you going to tell me—or die?” he flared.

“I'll tell you—don't get angry. His... his name... the first letter—... ‘Thu!’... Hmm—the next letter...?”

“His name... Thu... thu... thu... thu...”

“Enough spitting—tell me!”

“His name is Tukaram!!”

“Tukaram—Jai Jai Vittal!”

Ambalam could barely contain his happiness.

“Fine! What is his address?”

“Address? Tukaram—Cycle Company—Chakravarthi Street—Pattukkottai. That is his address.”

Ambalam muttered the address to himself two or three times so that he would not forget it.

“All right, Parimalam! From now on you have no trouble. You're a good girl... listen to what I say! If your life is to remain safe, stay at least in my house for a few days. I'm telling you the truth—your appa has his knife sharpened and ready to cut off your head! Yes—be careful!”

“I can't! I'm not going anywhere.”

“Fool—don't be stubborn! I'll bring your lover there himself—”

“No. Let me go!”

“Listen to me—don't die for nothing!”

In the middle of Ambalam's speech his men came thundering upstairs. “What, what?” he asked in alarm.

“The people coming in the boat are police,” they said, trembling.

“Ah—the police?”

“Yes—they've seen us too.”

<!-- source: scan 95 → 96 -->

“Ayyo—did they see you coming upstairs?”

“They did! They definitely did!”

“Fine—fine—come, let's run!” Ambalam looked through the window as he spoke. The boats moved slowly near shore. Though there was some distance between them, both reached land at almost the same time. Ambalam and his men prepared to run downstairs. He hurried Parimalam to run with them, and she too prepared to go.

They rushed down. The exhausted Parimalam could not run quickly. Just then a sudden gunshot sounded. Ambalam and the others ran fast and began disappearing behind thick cover. Startled by the shot, Parimalam tripped over a stone, screamed and fell. She could not get up. By then Ambalam's group had gone far away. The “police” who had landed on the shore ran toward the place where Parimalam lay. Durai recognised who was on the ground and cried, “Parimalam!” She was stunned. Even if, on a directionless dark road on the night of a new moon, the moon had suddenly appeared and offered light, she would not have been more amazed. Now she drowned in astonishment and could not speak. She longed to embrace Durai and pour her swelling tears onto his cheek.

Others were nearby, so she became still. Durai called his companions. “Go to the boat, carry that old man and place him inside Manora!” he said. They nodded and asked, “Yes—but what shall we do with that Ayyappan gang?”

“Frighten them and drive them far away,” he said, turning toward Parimalam. Thinking how all his urgency and all his plans had changed the instant he saw her, his friends laughed inwardly and left. The moment they were gone, Parimalam and Durai embraced on the bed of silver sand and remained motionless for a few seconds. Durai had told her many times, “If only you do not get angry, I could forget the world and remain until life leaves me, stroking your cheeks with my fingers.” Her cheeks were that beautiful—mango curves pouring out light. To lie in the lap of that beautiful woman was a greatness beyond his power to describe. So many people suffer in so many ways trying to reach the sphere of the moon; but I live inside the sphere of the moon itself, Durai had often said while gazing at her face, and as punishment she had wet his lips with hers.

In that one instant of embrace, all those feasts of memory came and went. They gave the two strength. Before sweetness could become cloying, that uncloying clear nectar separated her body from his and said,

<!-- source: scan 97 → 98 -->

“Darling!”

“My nectar!” he called her inside his mind, showing the name through a gentle kiss. “How did you come here, darling?” she asked.

Only after this question did Durai become aware of himself.

He ought to have asked Parimalam the same question, but until then had not done so.

When a woman sees a flower, she first thinks of wearing it; only later does she ask how it came there.

When one sees fruit, the mouth waters. The machine in the human body that asks how it was obtained starts working only after a delay.

So too, when Durai saw Parimalam, he became entranced—he tasted—then remembered to ask, “How did she come?” And he asked.

“A continuation of yesterday's events,” she said.

“The same for me—this too is a continuation of yesterday's events!” Durai replied.
