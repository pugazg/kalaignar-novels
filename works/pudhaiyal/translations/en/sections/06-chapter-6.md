# 6

It was Velliyambalam's hand that stopped the sickle-blow that was about to fall on Parimalam. Walking along the street, Ambalam heard the noise from Parimalam's house and hurried inside. The timing between his entrance and Parimalam's escape from danger was exact. Wherever quarrels took place, wherever happy or sorrowful events occurred, Velliyambalam was the sort of “great man” who wanted the leading position to belong to him. So there was nothing surprising in his stopping Thandavan's hand.

<!-- source: scan 60 → 61 -->

Sometimes even the actions of bad men end in good. If Velliyambalam had simply thought “what is it to us?” and walked away, would Parimalam not have lost her life? There is another side too: the act of a bad man can make goodness flash like lightning—and from that very lightning can pour evils all the way through thunder, rain, storm and earthquake.

Velliyambalam's help to Parimalam was like laying the foundation stone for just such an evil situation.

“What is this, Thandavam—why, ayya, are you rushing about with a blade?” Ambalam demanded.

Even in his fury, Thandavan gave Ambalam the respect due to him and stood silent.

“Anger must cool, ayya—anger must cool! You finish the matter with a sickle today, and tomorrow won't I be the one who has to go weep and get you out on bail? It looks as though you people won't let a man in this village live peacefully for even a little while! What happened now? Why did you leap like a tiger?”

Thandavan sat down without replying.

“What, ayya—a man is asking you, and you've shut your mouth without an answer!”

Afraid Ambalam might become angry because her husband would not reply, Anjalai began, “I'll tell you!”

“Tell me! Tell me!” Ambalam asked eagerly.

“While my girl was sleeping last night, a Mohini spirit carried her away—she only came back this morning—and he won't believe it, so he's jumping around like this!”

“A Mohini spirit!”

<!-- source: scan 61 → 62 -->

“Yes—that blacksmith's daughter, the one who died before her maidenhood passed and roams around as a Mohini—that one!”

“Oh-ho—why did it carry this girl away? Strange!”

“Isn't this the girl who studies at the big school in Pattukkottai?”

“Yes—this one! What else do we have? One and only one, the apple of our eye! And even she nearly came to danger like this!”

Ambalam began to feel confused. Though he had often heard that a Mohini spirit wandered around the Marungappallam region, doubt arose in him as to how such a spirit could actually have come and carried this girl away.

Looking at Parimalam, he asked, “What is your name, amma?” Wiping her troubled eyes, she said, “Parimalam.”

“Don't cry, amma! About what time was it when the Mohini spirit carried you away?”

<!-- source: scan 62 → 63 -->

“That's why I've been crying for six months—Appa said he wouldn't buy me a wristwatch. Otherwise I could have told you the time so exactly now.”

Parimalam had always disliked Velliyambalam. Now she liked it even less that he had begun asking for an explanation about the Mohini spirit. So she decided to answer Ambalam's questions wildly. Though he had saved her from her father's hand, she feared that some new danger might yet arise from Ambalam himself. Ambalam piled on the questions.

“What did the Mohini spirit look like?”

“Like a spirit. But very beautiful—”

“How old would it be?”

“I asked it. It said its horoscope was in its father's box and it would look and tell me.”

“Oh—you spoke to it too?”

“Yes, I spoke to it!”

“What language does that spirit speak?”

“Don't you know the language of spirits? It spoke Sanskrit!”

“Sanskrit? Did you understand it?”

<!-- source: scan 63 → 64 -->

“Oh! As it kept speaking, someone hidden nearby kept translating it into Tamil—?”

“Who was that? A man or a woman?”

“That was exactly what I couldn't see—from the speech I decided it was a woman, and from the voice I decided it was a man.”

“Yes, why did the spirit carry you away?”

“So that things like this could happen!”

“You are a mischievous girl! Yes—the Mohini is the blacksmith's daughter, isn't it? You could have asked her where her father buried all the gold!”

“I asked that too—and it answered!”

“What did it say? What did it say?”

“When she began telling me where her father had buried the gold, strangely my ears became blocked. At once I woke up. I regained consciousness!”

“Ada, ada! That is what you should have heard! All right—where did the spirit carry you?”

“On the road to Manora there's a large sluice, isn't there? When I opened my eyes, I realised I was there!”

Velliyambalam began to place a little faith in Parimalam's words. To people who already believed a Mohini spirit could do such things and that such a spirit wandered around Marungappallam, Parimalam's lie naturally looked like truth. So Ambalam tried to calm Thandavan.

<!-- source: scan 65 -->

“Don't think wrongly of your daughter, ayya! Haven't we ourselves heard that a Mohini spirit roams here? What merit is there in sharpening a knife and charging at her without investigating fully?” Ambalam said rather sharply.

“I don't know—go on! I don't believe it! She's an educated girl—she's trying to escape by saying something! I've been watching her walk, dress and behaviour for days—I have my doubts!”

“You foolish man, even if you have doubts you mustn't say them outside! If you rise like a storm before your doubt is even settled, the whole village will spit at you! I'm a big man in this village—I myself believe your daughter's words! Why are you boiling like fire?”

“She says the Mohini spirit carried her to the big sluice. I'll ask one question. Let her answer.”

“What do you want to ask? Ask.”

“Let her name an important plant or creeper at that big sluice. I'll go and check. If it's there, I'll believe what she says,” Thandavan concluded.

<!-- source: scan 65 → 66 -->

“Why, Parimalam! What your appa asks is fair, isn't it? The spirit made you sleep at the sluice, didn't it? Name some important plant or creeper there—let your father's doubt end!” said Ambalam.

“In that dangerous moment, did I look at all that and remember it?” she began—but suddenly a memory came to her. The pirandai creeper! The one that had frightened them at the sluice by looking like a snake. New courage rose in Parimalam.

“I'll tell you—I remember now—there are plenty of pirandai creepers there,” she said.

Thandavan hesitated a little before speaking.

“What, ayya—what doubt is left now?” Ambalam mocked.

“Pirandai creeper—fine. You yourself are witness. Come with me, please. I'll look in front of you and see whether there is pirandai there! If there isn't, I won't look upon her as a daughter; I'll turn her into a corpse—you are witness! Come!” Thandavan burst out and left. Velliyambalam went with him.

Parimalam's fear disappeared. Appa would certainly see the pirandai creeper there—he would believe all her lies. Temporarily reassured, she privately blessed both the poovarasu leaf that had frightened them like a snake and the pirandai creeper.

<!-- source: scan 66 → 67 -->

Thandavan and Velliyambalam walked toward the sluice Parimalam had named. Velliyambalam himself did not know why he had become so interested in her affair. Parimalam's Mohini spirit—Thandavan's secret lover—which was true? The desire to know surged in Ambalam's mind. If the Mohini spirit were real, he might bring some sorcerer, bind it and learn through it where the gold was buried. Or, if it were true that Parimalam had a secret lover, and if that lover were the young man he had met at the temple the previous night and chased, then through him too Ambalam could learn where the gold lay. Hadn't that lover been the one who said, “There, under that mango tree, is where the gold was buried”? What if he himself were Parimalam's secret lover? Thinking like this, Ambalam reached the sluice with Thandavan. Thandavan looked eagerly. The pirandai creeper Parimalam had described was there.

“Did you see? What Parimalam said is true!” Velliyambalam shouted.

A little distance away from the pirandai creeper, a single sandal lay on the ground. Both men's eyes leapt toward it. Ambalam stared. A definite truth had come to him. He did not reveal that truth<!-- source join: scan 67 → 68 --> to Thandavan.

<!-- source: scan 68 -->

The sandal lying at the sluice and the sandal found at the temple the previous night were a pair! Both belonged to the same man. When Ambalam chased him, Durai had left one sandal behind in his haste; Ambalam had picked it up that night. Now its mate had appeared. Yes—he was Parimalam's lover. If Ambalam got hold of Parimalam, he could get hold of him; through him he could discover where the gold was buried. Reaching this conclusion, Ambalam stood silent. He began walking with the resolve that somehow he must bring Parimalam to his own house and learn the secret. Thandavan's mind too had become a battlefield. He concluded that his daughter must have spent the night with some man. He picked up the orphaned sandal.

“What, ayya—have you set off like Bharata, carrying the sacred sandal!” Ambalam mocked. Thandavan was in no state to enjoy the joke. As for Ambalam, though he mocked him, all his thoughts were drowned in how to question Parimalam about the secret. Every sight he had seen at the temple the previous night played like shadows before his mind's eye. The two returned toward the village, each privately carrying a terrifying decision of his own.
