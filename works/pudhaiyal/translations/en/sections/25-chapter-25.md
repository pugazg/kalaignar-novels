# 25

After telling them of Kumaravadivu's end, old Mayandi fell silent. Durai's face too had gone pale. Parimalam sat looking at him. Some confusion churned in Durai's heart. He rose and paced back and forth. The disturbance showed in his eyes; it took him a little time before he could speak. Then, looking at the old man, he asked in a tone mixing mockery and anger:

“At first you said my father lived as a sage preaching to the village and as a physician. There is a great deal of contradiction between that and the story you are telling now, isn't there?”

“Yes. That is what I said first. I did not know then that you would keep digging and digging with questions until you grasped the truth.

“It is true that someone lived as a sage and physician. But that was not Kumaravadivu. It was this Mayandi! I hid the truth at first because I did not want to tell you of your father's faults. The medical profession that I took up after his death—I lied and told you that he had practised it. What can I do? Will you let me keep lying? You have made me vomit up every truth.”

“You said earlier that Marungappallam village turned the course of my father's life. What about that?”

“That? Can't you see? What I meant was that the village turned the robber Kumaravadivu away from the road of life and sent him onto the road of death.”

“Leave that aside. You said some treasure secret came into Appa's hands, and that he worried about whom he should take the treasure for. Was that a lie too?”

“The worry was real. The treasure secret coming into his hands was real as well. At the very threshold of death, Kumaravadivu handed that secret to me before he closed his eyes. As he gave it to me, he cried and said:

“‘Mayandi—even if the treasure truly can be found according to this clue, for whom am I to take it? I am going to die in another moment. Here, you keep this. If you can take the treasure and do some good with it, try. If you cannot, at least make sure it does not fall into the hands of selfish people. Mayandi! If you ever meet my son or daughter, do not tell them my story.’

“With those words his breath stopped. The copper plate that reveals the treasure and the small stone are in my hands. I do not know what to do with them.”

Durai interrupted. “Yes—but how did that copper plate and little stone come into my father's hands?”

“That is precisely what I am coming to. When we went to move the idol in the Marungappallam temple, Kumaravadivu found the secret. I sent him into the temple while I stood at a distance keeping watch. Inside, perhaps to see his way or for some other reason, he struck a match. These words caught his eye:

“Between the one who rises and the one who is worshipped......
Beneath the shadow of the crow's beak......
Along the path by which thieves enter......
Though it be found, in the eye of the Kammalar......
Seventy crores in pure gold!”

“That was the inscription. Kumaravadivu thought about it. He looked all around. He separated the meaning word by word. ‘The one who rises’ meant the festival idol—the image that is taken out in procession during festivals. ‘The one who is worshipped’ meant the main fixed idol. So he understood it as ‘between the main idol and the festival idol.’

“Then he considered ‘beneath the shadow of the crow's beak.’ There was an image of Saneeswaran seated on his crow vehicle. Using the light of the match in his hand, he watched where the shadow of the crow's beak fell and marked the places.

“‘Along the path by which thieves enter’ needed no explanation at all. The way by which he himself had entered and the way by which the priests entered were the same, were they not? So he easily identified the thieves' path.

“‘Though it be found, in the eye of the Kammalar’ confused him a little. Then he resolved that confusion too. He concluded that the secret must once have fallen into the hands of people possessed by caste obsession. With the crowbar in his hand he struck the place he had marked.

“There was a ‘tang’ sound. He dug again. A small box—a very small box. Inside it, a little stone and a copper plate. On the copper plate were the directions for finding the treasure: take the small stone to the indicated place and set it down; the stone would be drawn toward the place where the treasure lay; dig there and a vast quantity of gold would be found. Such a tremendous secret had come into his hands. It was while returning with it that the snake bit him. He cried out. I ran inside. He shook the snake away and came running out. I asked what had happened, and he told me.”

Parimalam interrupted eagerly. “Why did the snake bite him?”

“Do you think the snake bit because of Bhagavan's anger or because of the treasure secret's miraculous power? Nothing of the sort. If that were so, the snake should have bitten me too. The secret Kumaravadivu gave me is still with me, and to this day no snake has touched me.

“If the snake bit Kumaravadivu because he stole coins by moving temple idols, then men who are greater robbers than Kumaravadivu—men who swallow temple property under the names of trustees, priests and poosaris—ought to have one snake bite each limb! There was no reason under the old beliefs for the snakebite, Parimalam. It was an ancient temple, a place with little traffic, surrounded by bushes and trees. Snakes had made the shrine their dwelling. In his haste Kumaravadivu stepped on one of them, and it bit him.

“If the God dwelling in the temple sends snakes to punish offenders, why did he merely watch when a sinful swami murdered a green little child in front of his own shrine? There is no old or new supernatural reason connecting Kumaravadivu's entry into the temple with the snakebite. It happened naturally, by circumstance. That is all.”

Tears gathered in Durai's eyes. The knowledge that his father had been reduced to living by theft, that while stealing he had found a rare fortune, and that he had died without being able to take and enjoy it, scorched Durai like fire.

“Appa,” he moaned faintly. His eyes showed that lamentation and mourning had begun inside his heart. Parimalam too was distressed by her beloved's state.

Old Mayandi looked at him with affection. “See? This is why I said I would not tell the truth. I told you not to ask me for any story. You said you would not let go. Now you are grieving,” he lamented.

“Elder! Couldn't you draw out the snake's poison and save Appa?” Durai asked sorrowfully.

“I could not. I brought all sorts of herbs and tried, but I failed. He too became certain that he was going to die. Half-conscious, he told me how he had obtained the treasure secret. Then he left me. The villagers said, ‘The temple thief is gone.’ But nobody knew that Kumaravadivu himself had been that thief.

“The thefts from temples stopped. Because of that, people in the village concluded that the thief had disappeared. Without a proper companion I stopped the trade of entering temples at night. That was not the only reason I stopped. I had no desire to keep doing it. If the police caught me, the treasure secret in my possession might also be taken away. That was why I was so frightened when you caught me in police uniform.

“So I looked for other ways to make a living. I took up the medical profession I had falsely said your father practised. It gave me a way to fill my stomach. I began to think of myself as a siddhar and to conduct myself accordingly.

“The turns in my own life and the startling events in your father's life shaped new and newer thoughts within me. Experience is the greatest teacher, isn't it? With those experiences as a background, I voiced the ideas that arose in my mind.

“Right or wrong, I became stubborn that what seemed right to me must seem right to others as well. All this happened after Kumaravadivu died—yes, after the robber died—after my shadow, my dearest friend, died.

“Plain Mayandi became Mayandi the physician. Then I became Mayandi Swami as well. I possess the key to a great treasure. The treasure itself exists. Yet I am afraid to open it and bring mountains of gold out into the world.”

“You mean the Marungappallam treasure?” Durai asked.

“Yes. That is what I mean. I doubt whether I would retain the pleasure I now have in wandering alone from place to place after taking that treasure. And if news spread that I had taken it, I am afraid of how many knives might fly at my face. If I hand the secret to someone else, the thought interrupts me that, just as Kumaravadivu warned, it might fall into selfish hands and the treasure be wasted.

“I have realised that a few people already know I possess the treasure secret. Before matters go further, I decided to flee this place and go to Ceylon. It was on that journey that I met you at Manora. Then circumstances made me turn back and sit here telling stories with you. And I gained the happiness of finding my friend's son. Now I have no worry. I can give this property to the person to whom it belongs.”

The old man sighed.

“Elder! Why was that secret buried in the temple?” Durai asked.

“They tell a long story in Marungappallam about a blacksmith's family. They say that blacksmith was the owner of the treasure. His entire family was swallowed by the sea and destroyed. Not knowing that the family would be wiped out unexpectedly, he may have hidden the treasure secret in that temple. That is my guess. Whether the story is true or false, it is true that a treasure is there. It is true that the verse beginning ‘Between the one who rises and the one who is worshipped’ exists. I also believe that if this stone is taken and placed where the copper plate indicates, the treasure can be found.”

“Have you tried it?”

“How could I? Suppose, when I try, mountains upon mountains of golden gems suddenly burst out. How am I to protect them? If our own government were ruling, I would take the secret to them and tell them to use the treasure for the public. If I said it, it could be done. But what rules now is foreign government! I doubt that my life will last until this ends and our rule comes. That is why I wander without knowing what to do. I suffer without knowing whom to give it to.

“Durai! I need wander no longer. I need search for a person no longer. I have found you—a responsible young man. Keep safely the property your father discovered. When the time comes, use this to take the treasure. Let us wait until there comes a condition in which it can be used for the public. Even if a good government does not flower in my lifetime, can it fail to flower in yours? When it does, hand this over to that government and use it to help the country prosper. Here—take it!”

Speaking with emotion, Mayandi took the copper plate and the little stone from his fold and held them out to Durai.

Durai stretched out his hand to receive them.

Suddenly, with a terrifying noise, a rough man sprang inside from the backyard. It was the same dark figure that had been listening through the window all this time.

There was a knife in the ruffian's hand. Murderous frenzy was on his face.

“Mayandi—hand that over here properly,” the ruffian shouted.

“Who? You sinner—Ayyappan?” Mayandi cried out. Parimalam screamed and began to run.
