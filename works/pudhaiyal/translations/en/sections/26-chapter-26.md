# 26

“Is it that sinner Ayyappan?” the old man cried.

“Yes—Ayyappan himself! Hey, old man! Where are you trying to run? Stand properly!” Ayyappan shouted terrifyingly and began laughing aloud.

Parimalam, trembling all over, ran this way and that, unable to understand what she should do. Durai stepped back a little and prepared to resist Ayyappan. The old man too bent and straightened his body, recovered from Ayyappan's first rush and joined Durai.

“Old man—you cannot escape! If you value your life, hand over what is in your hand. And you, boy! Move away too. Don't buy useless trouble for yourself!” Ayyappan roared in his harsh, rasping voice.

His brutal gaze and the gleaming knife in his hand were signs that some shocking outcome would come very quickly. Durai kept edging backward until he slowly reached the bureau. Ayyappan followed both men, advancing slowly, planting his thick legs firmly as he moved.

Once Durai reached the bureau, he suddenly opened its door and took out the handgun inside. Seeing it, Parimalam felt as though life had returned to her. Old Mayandi too regained courage.

“Ayyappa! Be careful! I am turning your own words back on you now. If you want to live, run away this very moment!” the old man shouted with deliberate force and anger.

“I know, da, that it is a stage pistol!” Ayyappan said, laughing like a fox howling.

Durai's anger surged. He burned with rage that a man he himself had forgiven and driven away now stood before him threatening him. Pointing the pistol straight at Ayyappan, he growled, “Are you going or not?”

Ayyappan did not answer. He began laughing again.

“I'll shoot you down. Be careful!” Durai shouted once more.

“Shoot, da! Let's see it! Son of a heroic tiger, shoot!” Ayyappan said as he began moving closer.

The knife in Ayyappan's hand came almost directly in line with Durai's chest. Waiting for his moment, Durai knocked the knife aside and struck Ayyappan's hand hard. The knife fell. When Ayyappan bent hurriedly to pick it up, Durai saw his chance and gripped his neck tightly.

The two began fighting without releasing one another. Blows landed alternately on both faces. Seeing that Durai could not easily withstand Ayyappan's brutal assault, Mayandi began striking Ayyappan wherever he could, on the head and chest. Ayyappan dealt with Mayandi's interference with ease. Whenever the old man came near, he kicked him with all his strength and sent him rolling, while still continuing to attack Durai.

Parimalam's fear increased as she saw how hard her beloved was struggling against the brute and how helplessly the old man was trying to assist him. Ayyappan kept kicking the old man while smashing his rough face into Durai's face. Durai became badly trapped in his grip. Ayyappan began moving him toward the fallen knife in an attempt to seize it.

Realising this, the old man ran to push the knife farther away. A chair caught his feet and he fell headlong. Parimalam lifted him and ran toward the knife, but by then it had already come into Ayyappan's hand. Durai gripped the arm holding the knife with all his strength. If his grip weakened even a little, he would lose his life.

How could Parimalam bear to watch? She wondered what she could do to finish Ayyappan. Looking around, she saw the handgun lying in a dark corner. She ran and picked it up. She concluded that if she was to save her beloved from Ayyappan, there was no way except to kill him.

“Hey! Will you let him go? Shall I shoot?” she shouted fiercely.

Her shout did not reach him at all. Seeing that the situation was becoming disastrous, Parimalam aimed properly at Ayyappan and pulled the trigger. There was only a noise; no bullet came out. Smoke rose. Parimalam understood that it really was a stage pistol.

Ayyappan laughed cruelly and barked like a rabid dog. Through that laughter and barking he made it clear that it was only a stage pistol. The old man too stood there in disappointment.

He could not even get close to Ayyappan. Mayandi tried throwing the chairs, planks and every object in the room at him. Some missed and fell elsewhere. Some struck Ayyappan, caused small wounds on his skin, and destroyed themselves in the process.

Realising that matters had gone beyond control, Parimalam opened the front door and ran outside.

“Parimalam! Don't run anywhere in the dark!” the old man shouted.

“Stay there. I'll be right back,” Parimalam said as she kept running.

She had rushed out with the decision to call someone from the street for help. Since there were no houses very close to Durai's house, Parimalam raised her voice and cried, “Ayyo! Danger—danger! Somebody come!” Only her own voice echoed back. No answering voice came. In that darkness, at that hour when people were deep asleep, who would come? Parimalam ran a little farther and shouted again: “Ayyo... run here... danger! Danger!”

A light came toward her from the direction in which she had run. The moment she saw it, courage arose in Parimalam without her even realising it. She ran toward the light. She could see four or five people approaching quickly. Her courage increased further.

She ran rapidly toward them.

With the aid of the light, she recognised the approaching men as policemen.

“Sir! Sir! Run—there is great danger! Quickly, quickly!” Parimalam cried as she reached them.

“What might have happened by now? What has that criminal brute done to her dear beloved? What condition will he be in when she goes back? Will she see him alive or not? Those lips of her beloved that had shed sugarcane-sweet words—‘My gold! My flower!’—and sung love songs: would her own lips ever again be able to touch them, or not?”

With waves of such thoughts surging through her, Parimalam struggled through the darkness, half running and half walking. Who were those “saviours in danger” standing before her?

They were policemen. Parimalam felt a surge of relief. Was it not something she had never expected—that the police themselves had come to help?

A new light appeared in her eyes.

One policeman raised the hurricane lamp in his hand toward her face and asked, “Who are you, amma?” Before he could finish, another voice said, “This is Parimalam!” The speaker was not a policeman, but someone who had come with them.

“Who?” Parimalam exclaimed, startled as though she had stepped on fire.

“Who? It is me—your appan, Thandavan,” came the answer.

Yes, Parimalam's father Thandavan had come with the police. The policemen surrounded Parimalam. Could her state of mind be imagined? The beautiful young woman became like a fish thrown onto the shore.
