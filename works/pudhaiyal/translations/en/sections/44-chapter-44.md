# 44

Menaka would die one way or another. If she died, word would spread through the village that she had died from a knife wound. If that spread, Ambalam feared that he might be caught by the police. So he decided, as a precaution, to inform the police himself. He called his servant Raman and sent him running to Peravurani Police Station to tell the Sub-Inspector that Menaka had been stabbed.

Raman raised a doubt: “If they ask who stabbed Menaka, what am I to say?”

Ambalam thought for a little while. “Why are you asking me, fool? Don't you know? It was that fortune-teller who tried to kill her! Instead of asking me, go tell them at the police station! Tell them too that we have caught and are holding the Kudukuduppandi who tried to commit the murder!”

When Ambalam said this, Raman understood. He knew that it was Ambalam who had put Menaka on her deathbed.

“Ayyo, poor man—that fortune-teller has been trapped!” Grieving at the thought, Raman ran towards Peravurani to carry out his master's order.

As he passed the Nadiyam monastery, a voice called, “Rama!” from behind it. He turned. A woman stood there, beckoning him closer with her hand. Not understanding anything, he went to her. She wore a beautiful white sari, had covered half her head with a blue cloth, and concealed her face below the nose with a black gauze-like veil. Her wavy hair had been rolled like a horse's tassel and hung down. Only her forehead and eyes could be seen clearly. She even wore glasses over her eyes.

“How are you, Rama? Well?” she asked, moving closer to him.

Raman stood coyly embarrassed and asked in a melting voice, “Who are you? How do you know me?”

“I know you very well—you are Velliyambalam's servant, aren't you?” she asked with a laugh.

“Yes, it's me! Oh! Who are you, amma?”

“What? You're calling me amma! Don't I look like a young woman to you?”

“You do, you do. Yes—but who are you? How do you know me?”

“My name is Shokasundari! I am from Ceylon. I have often seen you when you come with Ambalam to the Puduppattinam market. But you have never seen me. You walk beautifully beside Ambalam like a swan, while I stand aside, watch you from the corner of my eye and yearn!”

“You yearn? Why?”

“Don't you understand even this? How much more plainly can a woman reveal such a matter to a man?”

“Oh-ho-ho! I understand! Shokasundari! You……” Raman drew out the words as though in a convulsion.

“Yes, Raja! I love you,” she replied and stood there.

Raman was overwhelmed. He stood outside himself. “You love me?” he cried. He could not believe even himself. She looked at him and said, “My beloved! Ask my eyes; they will tell you! Ask my heart; it will tell you! Come, open your heart and give me your consent! Shall the two of us live like nail and flesh, inseparable? Tell me, kanna!” Thus Shokasundari began pouring out her passionate yearning.

“What is this? It's all one great wonder! Am I dreaming?” Raman rubbed his eyes and looked Shokasundari up and down. He rejoiced again and again at the luck that had come seeking him. “Aha! A woman has been doing penance for my sake all these days!” he praised himself. He gently took her hands.

She too looked at him lovingly and stood coyly. “Swear to me that you will marry me!” she insisted.

“I'll swear a thousand oaths!” Raman said, striking his hand into hers as a pledge.

“Appada! My birth has attained fulfilment,” she sighed. Raman felt as though he ought to remain right there beneath the monastery with her.

Even so, he could not forget his master's order. He struggled to take leave of her. But she kept drawing Raman into conversation. When she asked, “Where is Parimala?” Raman was startled.

“Oh! You know even that? How do you know?” he asked in shock.

“My only work is to make inquiries about you. While doing so, I heard that your master had shut up a woman named Parimala, kanna!” she said, speaking with affectionate coyness.

“That Parimala fooled even me one day and ran away. Now we've brought that Durai and locked him up,” Raman announced heroically, trying at the same time to pinch her cheek. She was quite tall and Raman was very short, so he could not reach her cheek. He climbed onto a stone for help.

Shokasundari's face changed when she heard that Durai was confined. Raman did not notice the sudden change—poor fellow, he was bound and suffering in the intoxication of love. Teasing and teasing him, she asked how Durai had been caught and why he had been confined. Sunk deep in his new intoxication, Raman babbled that he did not properly know the reasons. He also told her that Ambalam had cut Menaka and intended to put the blame on a fortune-teller, and that this was why he himself was going to the police station. Leaning against Raman's lap, Shokasundari learned from him a certain amount about Durai, Menaka and the fortune-teller.

Raman had no time to investigate who she was or why she had suddenly begun showing affection towards him. Though the young man had no wish to lose an opportunity that had come to him of its own accord, he realized that it was getting late to go to the police station as ordered by his master and asked to take his leave.

She stood sulking. “You are leaving me. I cannot bear my tears,” she said, removing her glasses, wiping them with a handkerchief and putting them back on.

Promising that he would certainly meet her the next day, Raman separated without wanting to separate. She stood watching until he was gone, then Shokasundari began walking from Nadiyam along the road towards Marungappallam.

“That traitorous fellow has locked Durai up! I will free Durai no matter what,” her mouth muttered. Her feet flew towards Ambalam's house.

* * * *

Ambalam, expecting Menaka's death every moment, was sitting on the outer veranda. Shokasundari went and stood before him. Before he could ask anything, she introduced herself. She humbly said that she was an orphan who had come from Ceylon and that she hoped the great philanthropist Velliyambalam might give her some help. “You alone must bring light into my darkened life,” she pleaded.

After hearing her entire story, Ambalam at last seemed to think about something and asked, “Are you married?”

“Not so far. Even if you yourself marry me, that's fine!” she said, then looked at him shyly, lowered her head and stood scratching the earth with her toe.

The moment Ambalam heard that Shokasundari was unmarried, he was delighted. He swelled with satisfaction, thinking that everything needed to obtain the treasure was falling into place. Menaka's death could not be avoided; she would die somehow. The fortune-teller was there to bear the murder charge. And now a Shokasundari had appeared. He could marry her to Durai and offer the sacrifice required for the treasure. His long-standing dream too would come true. After that only old Mayandi would remain. If he put Mayandi in a grave after obtaining the treasure, he alone would become king over all the gold. Thus castles of desire and palaces of conspiracy arose in Ambalam's brain.

He decided that if he spoke cleverly to Shokasundari and locked her in a room too, he could conduct the marriage within another day or two and finish everything.

“Stay safely right here! I will keep you securely in a room. Within a few days I'll get you married to a good bridegroom and provide you with the comforts you need. Amma, helping helpless people like you is my work!” he told her in courteous words.

Shokasundari stood thanking Ambalam. Taking her inside the house, Ambalam said, “I'm going to keep you safely in a room here. Don't be afraid of anything!” and went to open Durai's room. Then, thinking of something else, he did not open it. Instead he put Shokasundari in the room where Malayalam Bhagavathi was kept and locked the door. Shokasundari understood nothing and stared. “Don't be afraid because I'm locking the room! Good times are coming to you,” Ambalam reassured her and went away.

Seeing Shokasundari, Kudukuduppandi gave a long sigh and went to sit elsewhere. Shokasundari looked Kudukuduppandi up and down once, then went to another side and sat by the window. “Ayyo, poor man—this Kudukuduppandi is the one in danger! Ambalam is going to make a false accusation and hand him over to the police,” Shok thought, stroking the bars of the window.

Delighted that the sacrifice for the treasure was ready, Ambalam ran to old Mayandi and announced his happiness.

“Old man! The wealth we have wandered searching for is certainly going to come to us through luck that arrives without our searching for it!” he cried.

Raman returned from the police station to report what had happened. Seeing Ambalam speaking with Mayandi, he hid and began listening.

“A woman named Shokasundari has fallen into my hands. She is an unmarried virgin. I promised to marry her and provide comforts and have locked her up so she cannot run anywhere! **As the plate says**, we must find a bridegroom for her, marry them, then immediately kill them and fulfil our sacrifice.”

Hearing this, Mayandi asked, “Whom are we to get as the bridegroom?”

“Oh, old man! I haven't told you yet, have I? You said Durai had run away. I am the one who has locked him up.”

“Is that so?”

“Yes. He too knows the treasure secret, doesn't he? That is why I caught him and put him away. Now that has turned out well.”

“Yes! True, Ambalam! If we finish Durai off, the two of us can divide the treasure between ourselves. But there is one thing—that fellow Durai will say he will marry only Parimala.”

“She cannot be found. What can we do? We will have to force Durai and Shokasundari to marry and offer them as the sacrifice.”

Hearing Ambalam's decision, the old man remained silent. Listening to Ambalam's words, Raman writhed, trembled and wilted. He wept on hearing that Shokasundari had fallen into Ambalam's hands and that she was to be sacrificed. An uncontrollable anger against Ambalam arose in him. He grieved, not knowing what use there was in showing that anger to him. He cried that danger had come so suddenly to his precious beloved.

Showing nothing outwardly, he went to Ambalam and said that the Sub-Inspector was not at the police station and that they would come immediately when he returned. Grief choking his throat, Raman moved away. Sitting in a corner of the house, he sobbed and sobbed thinking of his sudden beloved. He wailed aloud. Then he walked sadly towards the room where she was locked.

Shok was sitting by the window. Raman wondered how he could reveal such dangerous news to her. Unable to bring himself to approach her, he slowly went to another window. There he saw the fortune-teller sitting. He decided that through him he could warn his beloved of the danger coming to her. Going close to the fortune-teller, he pleaded, “Hey, Bhagavathi Saibu! Will you do me a favour?”

Parimala in Bhagavathi's disguise, thinking Raman might provide some help, asked, “What do you want?”

With tears in his eyes Raman said, “See her sitting over there—Shokasundari. She is my beloved friend! The two of us are life to life! My master is going to bring great danger upon her! He is going to marry her to a man called Durai, immediately kill both of them as a sacrifice and take the treasure. My beloved Shokasundari must be saved from that danger. Bhagavathi, will you yourself tell her this?” Raman pleaded.

Hearing those words, Bhagavathi's heart began to tremble. Knowing that her lover Durai could no longer escape, her heart melted like wax in fire. She wanted to die together with Durai.

After consoling Raman and sending him away, she collapsed in a corner and wept. Shokasundari watched the fortune-teller crying with concern. She thought perhaps he was crying because he was going to be made the culprit for stabbing Ambalam's wife.

“If only my wish to be sacrificed along with Durai could at least be fulfilled—that would be enough,” Parimala lamented within herself. She yearned: why should she not receive the good fortune being given to this Shokasundari who had come from somewhere? At the same time she felt sympathy and tenderness towards Shokasundari. She grieved that an innocent, foolish woman who knew nothing should have to die at the hands of a greedy rhinoceros. Thinking that she could at least hint to her about what Raman had said, she went towards her.

“What is it, Saibu? Why are you sad?” Shok asked.

Without answering, Parimala said, “Saibu is going to tell you a fortune; going to tell you a fortune.”

“What fortune?” Shok asked.

“You have a hero named Raman.”

“Oh, all right! Leave that aside, Saibu! You say you're a great fortune-teller—I'll ask one thing. Answer me! Am I a man or a woman? Tell me and let us see!” Sundari asked.

“Isn't that obvious? It's plain for everyone to see that you're a girl!” the fortune-teller mocked.

“A woman, am I? That's exactly what I'm not,” Shokasundari said with a laugh, pulling aside her veil.

In rapid movements she removed the disguiseing clothes. Parimala stood astonished.
