# 17

“Dog Durai had long treated the women labourers as instruments for satisfying his lust. A few innocent women who made the mistaken calculation that the proprietor's favour might help them advance in life also submitted to his game. Grandmother began to feel fear inside. That sinful proprietor's gaze, they say, began falling often on Amma as well. Grandmother would keep telling her, ‘Valli! You must be careful! Dog Durai's behaviour frightens me—be cautious.’ Amma was courageous. She had the nature of fiercely condemning whatever anyone did against justice. Whenever Dog Durai came near Amma grinning, ‘What, Valli?’ Amma would turn her face sharply and say angrily, ‘What or not-what—go away!’ Dog Durai would turn back, realising his trick would not work. Yet both Amma and grandmother could understand from his behaviour that he was waiting for some rare opportunity.

“When the day's work on the tea estate ended, Appa and Amma would sometimes work for a few hours at Dog Durai's house in the evening, believing they could earn a little more if they also attended to his household work. Amma accepted the work because Appa too would be there. Otherwise, how could our family of three or four members live without hardship? I was a child and had to go to school. Grandmother kept saying that in another month or two I would have a younger brother or sister. Once that child was born, there would be five members in the house. Could all that be managed unless they somehow struggled and earned? Was it enough merely to sigh that once we had lived grandly? If the present were used only to shed tears over the past, would not the future too become dark? So, forgetting what had happened, my parents sweated not only as plantation labourers but also as servants in Dog Durai's house. Amma was eight months pregnant when she accepted that work there. My beloved younger sister Thangam was then growing in Amma's womb. Amma ran about working and earning. Seeing her hardship, grandmother would say, ‘Valli! Pregnant women will have an easy delivery only if they do plenty of work!’

“This same grandmother said that only after becoming poor. Had she remained a wealthy grandmother, she would have said, ‘Valli, your health will be spoiled. Rest. A pregnant woman must not struggle!’

“Somehow, Amma took on excessive hardship and looked after the family. ‘At least let the children be happy,’ she would often say, shedding tears. It was that precious Amma whom I lost while still a child. With these eyes I saw the mother who bore me lying in a flood of blood. Ayyo, did even the wish of that noble woman, who endured so many troubles and worked so hard, come true? No. She worked so that at least the children might live well. She went away without seeing even that.

“Until Appa and Amma returned from working at Dog Durai's bungalow, I would stand at the doorway of our house. Amma would lift me and kiss me. Appa would pinch my cheek and fondle me. Even now those childhood moments seem like a dream.

“‘Amma! When will the baby be born?’ I would ask in baby Tamil. Grandmother would scoop me into her arms and tease me. She told me all this later while crying. In a family living in such a peaceful situation, one naturally asks what confusion could have arisen great enough to lead to murder. The confusion itself was nothing very important. But was that confusion the cause of the murder? No, not entirely. Though it was not wholly the cause, in one way it created the circumstances for the murder. Your look seems to ask, ‘What are you babbling?’ Periyavare! This is merely the confusion caused by explaining the confusion! That is all! It is true that quarrels arose between Amma and Appa, grew worse, and that they lived for several years without speaking to one another. After my sister was born, they became two poles. Their being husband and wife remained only as a matter of duty; affection and tenderness were ruined. My father began speaking like a philosopher about life, death, love and such things. A disgust with the world, like the disgust that arises in ascetics, arose in him too. Why did this disgust arise? His diary entries show that the many incidents he encountered were responsible for it. The love-confusion of Kuppan and Seetha created confusion in Appa's mind too. From Kuppan's conduct he calculated how low the importance a man gives to love can be. Likewise, to show what value women give love, he described another true incident in detail in his diary. This very incident was the root cause of the quarrel between Appa and Amma. I want to tell, as briefly as my memory permits, the interesting passages he wrote in the diary and the incident itself.

“Dog Durai had a daughter. Her name was Mary. She was born and raised in Ceylon. She could speak English and, to a fair extent, Sinhala and Tamil. If she was a white household's daughter, need one ask about modern fashions? Mary in particular was a young woman eager to discover new fashions. She would carry out great research into shortening the half-garments she already wore into quarter-garments. Because the fierce speed of modernity was surging without restraint, Mary stood in that region as a good example of how it dragged people back toward the savage age in which people lived without clothes. She had a lover named Joseph—also a relative. The dances and entertainments in which the two held hands in the middle of the street were obscene to watch. They wandered about at the very height of such love. Dog Durai kept saying that they would soon be married. Suddenly Joseph obtained a job in Madras. His parents there sent him an urgent letter: ‘This is a good job! Don't let it slip away! Come immediately!’ Joseph took a sorrowful leave of his beloved Mary, said he would return soon and complete the marriage, and went to Madras. At the time Joseph left for Madras, my parents were still working in Dog Durai's house. I told you Amma was suffering through pregnancy—that was precisely then. Within four or five days of her lover leaving for Madras, Mary became the heroine of the incident that sent a storm through our family.

“Joseph sat in Madras sighing, believing that Mary regarded him as her very life. But she, here, set out like her father to loose arrows in the hunt of lust. She was terribly eager to earn the name ‘the daughter is just like her father.’ Did she see how much turmoil the result of her desire would create in our family? One evening, after the tea-estate work ended, grandmother returned home to look after me. Appa and Amma went to attend to the durai's household work. Appa was arranging the flower plants in the garden. Amma was scrubbing vessels. Mary then came toward my father. As usual Appa asked, ‘What, amma, are you well?’ Mary, whose Tamil came out in a muddled fashion, said, ‘Yes, well,’ then called, ‘Kumaravadivu—light the lamp in my bedroom! I don't know how to light it.’ Appa thought nothing of it and went into her bedroom to take the lamp and light it. Suddenly Mary pulled the curtain across the room and came close to Appa. He understood nothing. ‘What is it, amma—?’ he asked, standing motionless.

“‘Nothing—I have very much love for you!’

“‘What—love?’

“‘Yes—don't you know love? Love—love,’ she explained.

“‘Love? What a curse—toward me? I am a man with wife and children, amma!’

“‘What if you have children? You may be forty—but you're still young! I have very much love for you!’

“‘Ada—toward a servant.........’

“‘That is a taste for me!’

“‘Taste or mud-clod—amma, Mary-amma, listen to what I say. Don't betray your lover Joseph. That is a sin!’

“‘Betrayal? I love Joseph too—and I love you too! If one day I eat hotel food, is that betrayal of home food? If there is no home food, must I not eat hotel food? What is this! Nonsense!’

“Appa copied these conversations exactly into his diary. During idle moments I read this passage again and again until I memorised it. Now listen to the rest of the story.

“However much Appa advised Mary, she would not listen. What could sermons on chastity amount to before her hunger of lust?

“‘Mary-amma! Listen to me. Love is a living arrangement of letters. Don't throw it into the dust like this. They say your English books contain great works of love. I have heard of the great love epic of Romeo and Juliet and have even wondered whether such a story could really have happened. You are an example proving that so sacred a story could not have happened!’ I told Mary. She replied, ‘Oh? You know the story of Romeo and Juliet? Knowing that, you still cheat me? Come, give me a kiss! Come on! My dear! Romeo and Juliet are stories that cannot happen. Have you heard of Cleopatra? She had two lovers—Caesar and Antony! I am like her! Joseph—Caesar! You—Antony!’ the white woman babbled.

“I began moving out of the room, afraid that she seemed capable of being a Panchali who surpassed even Cleopatra.

“‘Uh-uh—don't go, man! If you go, I'll tell my father that you loved me and have you dismissed from the job!’ she said angrily.

“I turned back to advise her again—but without giving me the chance, she sprang onto me and embraced me. I could not free myself from her. Like a mad dog she wounded my cheek. I decided to strike her hard and throw her to the floor and tried to do so. But at that very moment my wife Valliyammal entered the room—no, a storm entered my life.’

“That is how Appa described the incident. The moment Amma came in, Mary left Appa and ran outside. Before Appa could call, ‘Valli!’ Amma angrily said, ‘Enough—our relationship ends today!’ and walked out.

“This incident too became one foundation for the circumstances that led to Amma's murder.

“Are you asking why I am circling everywhere instead of briefly proving that Appa was not the murderer? Or are you teasing me for stopping incidents at important points, like an author of serial stories, merely to stir curiosity? Periyavare! Do not think wrongly of me.

“Only if I explain my story—and my family's story—in detail will the indifference you feel toward me disappear and sympathy for me arise in you. Therefore you will have to listen patiently to my story.”
