# 50

The east had begun to brighten. Old Mayandi's rekla came to a halt beside the sea waves washing the shore near Manora. Thandavan and he climbed down in agitation. A small boat stood unattended by the shore. Mayandi ran towards it. He thought for a moment, then climbed in and sat down. An oar suited to the little boat was lying inside it.

“Thandava! This is the boat waiting for Durai and Parimala! Never mind—they will somehow escape with Anandan's help. The urgent matter now is that my life must be protected! All right; I am going to Puduppattinam—take care of Parimala and Durai!” Mayandi said, and began rowing the boat away.

As Thandavan stood on the shore, he heard the sound of a rekla approaching and warned Mayandi, “Quickly! Quickly!!” Mayandi too began rowing faster.

Seeing Ambalam's cart approaching in the distance, Thandavan pulled the rekla aside and hid it, then concealed himself nearby.

Travelling in the boat, Mayandi sighed with relief that he had escaped Ambalam. He opened the fold at his waist and looked at the “treasure secret” he had carefully guarded for many days. With joy he pressed it to his eyes.

“You have escaped! Not only have you escaped from the greedy man—Durai and Parimala have escaped too! It is all your power! I have found many good people who can use you for good purposes. Through you I will recover the slabs of gold lying buried and bring prosperity to families that have been left barren. The time has come for my idealistic dream to be fulfilled,” the old man told himself. From the boat he looked up at Manora.

Once, standing at the summit of that Manora and looking at the herds of clouds grazing across the sky, he had cried out longingly, “Clouds! Your tears of death help a land to live; sometimes they help destroy it too. Yet long live your tears of death! In the same way, will my tears of death not help anyone to live?” In those days circumstances had forced him to think of somehow fleeing abroad with the treasure secret. Cruel men, selfish men and men consumed by greed were all eager to learn the treasure secret. Mayandi feared that if it fell into their hands, his ideal would never be fulfilled. So he had decided that it must fall into no one's hands; perhaps before he died some way would appear by which his ideal could succeed; until then this country offered no safe way to protect the treasure secret. With those thoughts, he had prepared to travel to Ceylon by a clandestine boat route.

Before that could happen, storm after storm had swept through his life. Thinking over all of it as he rowed towards Puduppattinam, the old man saw Ambalam's rekla arrive at the shore.

Seeing Ambalam climb down in a fury, Thandavan hid himself more carefully. The gleaming knife in Ambalam's hand frightened him all the more. Ambalam called out to old Mayandi and shouted, “Stop! Stop!” The boat had not gone very far, so Ambalam's cries reached the old man's ears. Ambalam stepped into the sea and began walking towards the boat. Fear arose in Mayandi. Could Ambalam actually walk all the way to the boat? He pushed the oar vertically into the sea and was startled to discover that the water beneath the boat was less than the height of a man. Ambalam, forgetting that he was wading through the sea, kept advancing towards the old man with blazing eyes.

Mayandi used all his strength to row away. Wading through water up to his chest, Ambalam realized that Mayandi had moved beyond his reach. He understood that if he went any farther he would be submerged.

Mayandi kept manoeuvring the boat this way and that so that Ambalam could not seize him, but he still could not get very far away. Ambalam shouted at him, “Turn back properly!”

“I am not afraid of your threats, Ambalam! Enough of all the games you have played! At least from now on, act with some sense. The durbars of a jungle king never last very long! You are this furious because you think I deceived you, aren't you? Did I deceive you? No, my boy, no! You deceived yourself! Even in that deception you have unknowingly done a few good deeds—why are you staring? Are you wondering whether we could ever do anything good? The well you dug beside the school while searching for the treasure will be useful to the villagers, won't it?” the old man said with a laugh.

Hearing this, Ambalam flared up. “The well! I'll ruin it tomorrow itself!” he growled.

“Ambalam! You may ruin the well—but there is another good deed you have done that you can never destroy again yourself! I mean the marriage you arranged between Parimala and Durai. In order to accomplish a purpose, good people sometimes enter bad paths. In the same way, bad people too can be forced into circumstances where they end up doing good deeds! In just such a situation, you have completed an excellent good deed. Is it an ordinary thing to unite two lovers of one mind and make them husband and wife? Is it not a deed of ‘merit’ that seeks atonement for all the ‘sins’ you have committed?” the old man said, and laughed. Still laughing, he continued rowing.

“Lovers! Marriage! Good deed! Hey, old man! Just wait until tomorrow and see what happens to them!” Ambalam roared.

“Don't shout for nothing! I'm going!” the old man replied, and tried to row faster. His terror had not subsided. He was able to move the oar only because of the single reassurance that he was in the boat while Ambalam was in the water.

Ambalam stood looking in the direction the old man was going. Realizing that he could not seize the man with his own hands, Ambalam took the knife he carried. Gathering all his fury into one motion, he hurled it at Mayandi. The knife flew hard into old Mayandi's side. Crying “Ah!”, Mayandi picked up the treasure secret that lay in the boat. The oar fell into the sea. With the knife lodged in his side, Mayandi slumped towards the sea. The hand holding the treasure secret hung outside the boat.

“Durai! Parimala! Durai! Parimala!” his lips murmured. The boat rocked on the waves. Mayandi lost consciousness. His eyes closed. The heart that had laid the foundation for his idealistic dreams stopped its work. The head that had always looked upward towards the sky bowed. Mayandi became a corpse. As though announcing his death, the treasure secret he had bound and guarded so carefully slipped from his hand and sank beneath the sea. Even at the moment of death he remained wary where Ambalam was concerned: he sent the treasure secret into the lap of the sea so that it would not fall into Ambalam's hands.

Ambalam stood watching with fiery eyes as Mayandi's dead body lay slumped in the boat. Thandavan, though he witnessed the old man's death, could do nothing and stood hidden with tears in his eyes. From the summit of the Manora standing majestically nearby, it seemed as though the old man's voice echoed again:

“O! Herds of clouds grazing in the sky! Do you imagine your lives are eternal? Do not forget that the cool wind itself is your Yama. Clouds! Your tears of death help a land to live; sometimes they help destroy it too. Yet long live your tears of death! In the same way, I long to know whether my tears of death will not help someone to live. Creatures of the clouds! Give the power of your tears of death to the tears of death of this poor human being—come—quickly! Before that cold wind blows! At once! At once!”

Only the cold wind was blowing over the old man's corpse. But the treasure he cherished had been soaked in his tears of death and had sunk beneath the seawater. As though helping him into his great sleep, Mother Sea rocked that boat-cradle with her wave-hands.

With the satisfaction that “Mayandi is finished,” Ambalam turned towards the shore. Durai and Parimala were approaching in a rekla. Thandavan ran towards them.

A desire for revenge surged up inside Ambalam. “Caught you!” he shouted as he began walking towards the shore. Running up to Durai, Thandavan cried, “He has killed Mayandi!” Durai and Parimala wailed “Ayyo!” in anguish.

Ambalam walked quickly towards them. The three stood on the shore watching him approach.

Four or five reklas came to a stop behind the three of them. Around twenty thugs climbed down and surrounded them as though laying siege. In delight, Ambalam kept shouting, “Don't let them go! Don't let them go!” as he waded through the seawater towards shore. With tears in his eyes, Thandavan said, “At last all of us have been trapped.” Suddenly the sound of an approaching car drew everyone's attention. The car came at great speed and stopped by the shore. More than ten policemen and officers got out. Anandan came with them. Raman was there too. At the sight of the police, the thugs scattered and ran. A gunshot rang towards the sky. All the thugs surrendered. Ambalam, who had been walking towards shore, turned and began walking back towards the sea. The police surrounded him and tried to capture him.

“Velliyambalam! Come! Come!” they called, coaxing him. Ambalam did not listen. He kept walking towards the sea.

“Leave him alone—our master doesn't know how to swim—he'll come back somehow!” servant Raman shouted mockingly.

When Ambalam did not turn back, the police hurried after him, intending to bring him back to shore. Ambalam walked towards the sea even faster than they did. Perhaps he considered it better to disappear into the sea than to fall into the hands of the police. The sea covered his entire body. The police began searching for him. They brought ashore the boat that was rocking the lifeless Mayandi. Durai and Parimala pressed their faces to the peaceful face of the old man and wept. A police officer took a roll of paper from the old man's lap. He read it there on the spot.
