# 21

“Amma was not at an age to die—yet she was killed. Death took away grandmother, who had gone on labouring without tiring even after becoming dry as a fallen leaf.

“I knew only that my father was wasting away in prison—where, and how? I did not know. For affection I had only one precious sister—and that buffalo of a man had thrown even her away as manure for a treasure. It seemed not merely that the world but my entire future lay covered in darkness.

“Midnight—‘Where are you going?’ I asked myself. I could ask the question, but could not answer it. Even the night insects crying in the darkness to announce coming rain must have had some purpose—I did not have even that. The sky began to growl. The weight of darkness kept increasing. A light drizzle—ah! Do you know what I felt at that moment? The raindrops struck my face, burst and scattered; my clothes slowly grew wet. It gave me a strange feeling of despair.

“In earlier days, whenever Thangam and I saw rain, we would leap like peacocks. We thought there was no greater joy than standing among raindrops. Until grandmother lovingly became angry and said, ‘It isn't good for your health; come inside,’ we would play and sing while getting drenched. Even after coming indoors we would sit on the veranda and enjoy the rain. How many paper boats did we float in the rainwater winding down the street like little channels? Our boats would move majestically; raindrops would fall on them; then, heavy with water, they would lose their shape and dissolve back into old paper in the stream. Thangam and I would click our tongues in sympathy. Those are things I can never forget—not today, not ever. I, who once regarded rain as a feast, trembled at the drizzle that midnight.

“That day Thangam and I had competed in launching paper boats. My boat overturned first. Do you know how she danced with delight? While she was still dancing, she suddenly stopped. Without even looking at the street, I looked at Thangam's face and said with certainty that her boat too had overturned. Now it was not a paper boat—my golden boat had overturned before me. I walked on; raindrops fell rapidly upon me too. I did not know when this orphaned boat would overturn. Waves of thought struck and struck until all the banks of my heart were being ruined, and I kept going wherever my feet showed the way. Terrifying lightning—thunder that shook the heart—heavy rain—a ghostly wind! There was nowhere to shelter. For a long distance there were not even trees or brush. I stood motionless on the ridge of a field. Mother Nature was bathing my head. One must bathe the head in mourning for a sister's death, mustn't one? I stood thinking that she was performing that duty for me. It was as though I stood beneath a waterfall; the rain was that heavy! My sister was no longer there to enjoy it with me. She was asleep deep beneath the earth.

“The rain did not stop until dawn. After that the sky showed a little mercy. The east began to pale. I opened my eyes wide and looked. A short distance in front of me I saw a building. I had stood beside it all night, but had not been able to see it. Such was the darkness! It was the Nadiyam monastery that sinful swami had spoken of. I had not seen it because it had been drowned in darkness. People say that monasteries in general are so wrapped in darkness that they give no light to those without support; in my case that saying became literally true. I walked toward the monastery. Opposite it a great peepal tree towered into the sky. At its top an ochre charity flag, soaked and torn by the rain, hung its head and lay curled. I took shelter at the monastery entrance. A few local goats and cattle had already taken shelter there before me. Two or three donkeys with sores all over their backs stood there as well. In that place filled with dung and goat droppings, I folded my arms and crouched, trying to suppress the cold. Morning sunlight began to bloom. After all the suffering of the night I sighed, ‘Appada,’ and, still seated, dozed for a while. Someone tapped me on the back and woke me. I sprang up. He spoke in a language I did not understand. He looked like a northerner. The monastery at Nadiyam belonged to northern swamis, did it not? Perhaps he was one of their men.

“‘Ayya—my whole body is shivering—let me sleep a little,’ I said.

“He replied roughly in words I did not understand. He also struck me on the head.

“Why did I need to understand his language? I understood his heart. The pain in my head told me what kind of nature it possessed. I left the monastery. Looking at the charity flag hanging from the tree opposite, I laughed inwardly and walked on. The torture of hunger began as well. I asked for rice at the houses in Nadiyam. I received a little from each house. I ate. There was even some left over. I did not throw it away. I took off my shirt, spread a few poovarasu leaves inside it, poured the remaining rice onto them, tied it up well, and kept it—because I would need it that night!

“The next day I travelled to Peravurani. Peravurani was an example of the kindness of village people. If a beggar arrives a little before mealtime, householders have the refrain, ‘The food isn't ready yet.’ If he comes after they have eaten, ‘All the food is finished!’ If he arrives while they are eating, ‘You've come while we're eating—come later!’ These are refrains householders keep ready for beggars. I did not hear that refrain in Peravurani. Are you asking, ‘Why do you talk like a hereditary mendicant?’ This is the experience gained from living as a beggar for nearly six months! I did not stay long in Peravurani, where a loving way of life protected those who came seeking refuge as though they were children. I had to meet many who spoke kindly and said, ‘Find some work, appa—you can earn plenty of wages.’ It was not laziness that made me unwilling to work. My mind would not settle on anything. Sometimes I myself wondered whether I had gone mad. I had no confidence that I could do a job properly. So I left Peravurani in the middle of the night.

“The loving nuisance of ‘Go and work’ disappeared. I came to Marungappallam. Many months had passed since I had seen my sister's grave. I wanted to shed tears there. I went toward the temple and sat where my sister had been buried and cried until my desire was exhausted. I wet with tears and kissed the earth that formed the blanket over Thangam's body. Rain came again. Heavy rain, just as I had hoped. I took the papers I had stuffed into the torn pockets of my ragged shirt and made boats. One in my name—one in my sister's name. I floated both boats in the rainwater. Watching the boats move, I jumped and danced—I laughed—I cried. Then I ran from that place toward the village. I lay down on the veranda of a house. The householder was a rough man, quick-tempered. He drove me away. ‘Ayya—you seem to be the only bad man in this whole area,’ I answered back. That was enough—one blow. He struck me on the head with a thick stick. Blood flowed. Taking a palmyra-leaf umbrella, he went out on some business. I jumped out of the house as though to run, but staggered, unable to do so. Then a little girl from that rough man's house—perhaps eight or nine years old—ran out, brought me inside, told her mother to put medicine on my cut, and gave me food.

“While I was eating, her father returned. He sprang at the mother. ‘Why did you let this beggar fellow inside?’

“‘No—the child brought him in!’ the mother said, placing the blame on the girl.

“‘The girl brought him in, did she—the girl! Is he to be a resident son-in-law or your daughter's bridegroom, that you keep him inside and serve him rice with such hospitality?’ He struck his wife hard. The little girl too received a blow across the back with a loud slap.

“‘A resident son-in-law? A bridegroom?’ he had asked. I did not know then that I would indeed become a son-in-law of that very house.”

Durai allowed his story to slacken for a moment and looked at Parimalam. A new light shone on her face. She looked at him eagerly. The old man remained motionless like a corpse.

Durai began again.

“I have not told even Parimalam this secret until now. The little girl who gave me shelter and food—and even received a beating from her father because of me—was Parimalam!” said Durai.

Parimalam's heart swelled with delight. Had the old man not been there, she would have cried “Darling!” and showered him with kisses as she leapt upon him. Her eyes overflowed with tears of joy.

“Poor thing—she was an eight-year-old child then! Only seven or eight years later did love arise between us. How could Parimalam have recognised me from that time? Even after Parimalam's father drove me away, I did not immediately leave Marungappallam. My sister's grave and seeing Parimalam gave me some comfort. Parimalam would secretly bring me rice. After eating it we would play together. This went on for a month. Then Parimalam too had to leave me. Her father arranged for her to go to a relative's house in Pattukkottai and study from there. Parimalam cannot properly remember those events now—she was only a little girl then!

“Somehow this beggar youth became attached to that little girl's great heart. I would comfort myself by thinking: what an enormous temple of affection has been built inside that tender heart, and even this orphan has been given a place in that temple. I felt that Parimalam had been separated from me. Until then I had wandered as a wasteful boy, caring nothing about life or work. I began to care about the future—yes, because I thought of Parimalam! Her father had asked, ‘Are you going to become a son-in-law of this house?’ I wanted to make that true. Parimalam had gone to Pattukkottai. I decided to straighten out my life and meet her afterward. That is how I met her, seven or eight years later!

“By then Parimalam had forgotten me. I too dealt with her as though I were a new person. ‘What, Parimalam! Isn't that right?’ Durai asked. Parimalam understood nothing. ‘Darling!’ she cried. The old man still said nothing.

“After Parimalam went away to study, I set out to find work. I wondered whether I should return to Peravurani. Then I thought the work I might obtain there would perhaps not give me the standing needed to meet Parimalam and become her husband, so I wandered through other towns. During this period I suffered greatly. Many days of hunger—faintness—exhaustion—I withered like this. Some town or other. There was a temple festival there. I think the place was called Rajamadam. I wandered all day. I did not get a single grain of rice. Everyone was concerned with going to the temple, watching the festival, and worshipping God. I could no longer bear the hunger. I staggered along the street. At the entrance of a Perumal temple I cried, ‘God! Have you no eyes?’ My eyes spun. That was all; I fell. After that I knew nothing.

“‘Devotee! Get up!’ I heard a voice. Slowly I opened my eyes and looked.

“A short distance away I saw a green radiance. ‘Get up, devotee!’ the voice came again. Mahavishnu stood there with conch and discus. I sprang up in shock. ‘Bhagavan! God! Save me!’ I cried and ran toward him.”
