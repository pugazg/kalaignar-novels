# 36

“Where are we going along this road?” Parimalam asked the old man.

“We will go toward the Manora and then to Puduppattinam,” he replied.

“How is he? Where is he?” Could she keep herself from asking? She asked.

“He is in Puduppattinam,” Mayandi replied.

He answered her, but his mind was full of confusion. He feared that Ambalam's schemes might already have brought some danger upon Durai. Since Parimalam had been in the bungalow, it had become clear that Ambalam's promise to hand her over to Durai at the Manora was false. The old man concluded that Ambalam must have planned to deceive Durai by using Parimalam's name.

He did not want to frighten her by telling her this. So he walked silently, sunk in thought, while Parimalam followed him.

Once they reached Puduppattinam, she decided inwardly, she would tell the old man and her beloved every one of Ambalam's deeds and arrange for him to receive the punishment he deserved. She also resolved to find some way to release innocent Dukkaram.

She wanted to tell old Mayandi about Dukkaram immediately. But she did not wish to disturb his silence and thought. Because of the respect she had for him, she simply followed behind him.

“When we reach Puduppattinam, the first task is to free Dukkaram,” she told her own heart as she walked with him through the dark road.

After a long silence, Mayandi looked at her and threw out a question. “Parimalam! Where—and how—did you and Durai first meet?”

It was clear that the question was not very important to him, for he continued walking forward without even waiting for her answer.

Parimalam had not expected that question then. She walked on, hesitant to answer.

“Oh-ho, are you ashamed to tell me?” the old man asked as he walked.

She was indeed ashamed. Would a young woman not feel shy when an old man unexpectedly asked such a question about love? A shyness capable even of driving away pain surrounded her. That shyness brought old events back before her heart.

The old man's question received no answer, but on the screen of that spear-eyed woman's heart the sweet memory began to play again.

* * * *

That day was the annual day at the Pattukkottai school. The newly arrived headmaster had, unusually, arranged a grand celebration. We students could hardly contain our excitement. In a life spent studying from morning to evening, news of the annual day brought a welcome change.

The new headmaster valued revolutionary ideas. Old notions—moss-covered beliefs—were things he could not bear. So he was eager to conduct the celebration in a new way.

In the morning came the students' parade. In the evening there were fancy-dress competitions, games and prize-giving. At night there was a play.

There had not been enough time to train the students themselves to act, so another drama troupe had been engaged. A student volunteer corps had been formed to attend to people coming and going and to manage the crowd properly. I too was one of the girls in the volunteer corps.

The night play began. Though I was working as a volunteer, all my attention remained on the drama. Its title was “Nur Jahan.” A beautiful woman played Nur Jahan. Ah, how could I describe her acting!

“Is this really the Nur Jahan of history—or is she even more beautiful than that Nur Jahan?” the students wondered.

When her husband Sher Khan was killed, the flood of grief that overwhelmed that beauty, and the tears she shed when she thought that her own beauty had been the cause of his death—ah! Even now the memory makes me shiver.

Then came the stage where she became the enchantress of Emperor Jahangir's bedchamber—the ruler who had bound himself to chase life through dance, song and intoxication—and afterward her skill in governing the land. Everything was marvellous! Marvellous! Not only in history, but even on the stage!

The beautiful performer who played that flower-bearing maiden received endless praise. Again and again came cries of “Sabash!” and applause.

Some jealous people there did not like seeing the annual day become a success.

“This new headmaster is doing all sorts of things. With so many mythological plays available, was this historical drama the only one he could find?” they raged, waiting for an opportunity.

According to their wicked plan, the pandal was set on fire while the play was in progress. The people scattered. Fortunately, the place where the play was being held stood some distance from the school building, so the flames did not reach the school.

The disorder created by the hired rowdies began. The actors fled in different directions. I was a volunteer, so I ran here and there trying to give emergency help.

At that moment the woman playing Nur Jahan was running alone.

“Ayyo, she is running all by herself!” I thought, and ran to help her. I led her toward the school building.

By then the disturbance in the pandal had grown worse and the police had begun a lathi charge. Fire on one side! Riot on the other!

The beautiful actress and I entered a classroom in the school. I struggled hard to protect her from the rioters. We went inside and bolted the door.

‘Nur Jahan’ looked at me and asked, “You seem such a good girl. Are you from this town?”

“Marungappallam is my native village,” I said, and told her about myself.

She asked my name and my father's name two or three times. Outside, it seemed the disturbance had ended. The fire too had been extinguished.

“Shall we go out?” the beauty asked.

“Some of these classrooms have been left in the charge of the volunteer corps. So let us spend the night here and leave in the morning,” I said.

She agreed. I began talking with her. I had found a good chance to praise her acting to her face.

“You look exactly like the real Nur Jahan,” I said.

“Is that so?” she smiled playfully.

We kept talking to each other.

“You were born for the role of Nur Jahan!” I said.

“Oh? From the way you praise me, it looks as though you wish you had been born as Jahangir!” she teased.

“I certainly do think so,” I replied.

Within that one night, within only a few hours, a close friendship had formed between us.

Then she asked something that makes me shy even now when I remember it.

“What would it be like if I were Jahangir and you were Nur Jahan?”

“Am I that beautiful?” I asked shyly.

“Yes!” she nodded.

Then we lay down on two benches and began to sleep.

* * * *

Dawn! I woke at the sound of the rooster. Nur Jahan was asleep. I went to wake her. She was sleeping deeply.

I felt like playing a prank. I tied her long braid to the leg of the bench, sat at a distance and woke her. I expected her to rise, the braid to stop her, and then I could laugh.

I woke her. She rose. The braid did not stop her!

But I discovered there was no connection between the braid and her head. Nur Jahan stood up with beautiful short, curly hair.

The person who had played Nur Jahan was not an actress, but an actor! Yes—the actor who would forever perform upon the theatre of my heart! My handsome Durai!

At first I became angry. But for some reason that anger did not last. I had given my heart to Durai in a woman's costume and had become close to him; after learning he was a man, I could no longer break off that relationship. That strange meeting of a single day became meetings that grew sweeter day after day.

Durai became mine. I became his.

This was what Parimalam should have told the old man as her answer. But she did not. How could she say it? So she remained silent.

By then they had reached the vicinity of the Manora. The old man was eager to learn news of Durai. They went nearer.

There they saw seven or eight men rolling about on the ground. Mayandi ran over and looked. They were all Durai's friends. Their hands and feet were tied and they had been thrown down.

The old man was stunned.

“Where is Durai?” he cried.

No one answered—their mouths too had been bound.
