# 40

Malayalam Bhagavathi seemed to think that he had become an obstacle preventing her from speaking freely with Durai. So, to see what would happen, he closed his eyes and pretended to be asleep.

After a little while, Durai, who had been pacing about the room, went and stood by the window, sunk in thought. The fortune-teller who was pretending to sleep slowly opened his eyelids and watched with interest what was happening in the room.

Menaka came to the window. Unexpectedly Durai too was caught in her gaze and stood motionless. Menaka had a pomegranate in her hand. Holding it out to Durai, she said, “Eat this pomegranate.”

“It's all right—no, thank you!” Durai replied indifferently.

“Why? Don't you like pomegranate?” Durai could not answer this question, so full of Menaka's yearning. She stood there holding the beautiful pomegranate, shining like a jewel, while her own cheeks were themselves two pomegranates! What could he do? He had no need of that pomegranate. So he did not become a poet, comparing her with that lovely, gleaming fruit.

Menaka stood like a marble statue, looking at his youthful, vibrant face. Then she broke open the fruit in her hand. Just as ruby beads scattered when Kannagi broke her anklet, coral-red seeds scattered from the pomegranate. She gathered them in her palm and held them up before his face. He merely shook his head to show that he did not want them. “Please eat them—for my sake!” she pleaded, hoping to receive words of endearment from him in return.

“What is this, amma? This is becoming a great nuisance! What you're putting me through is worse than wasting away alone in this room! Why are you behaving like this?” Durai asked, speaking with some anger.

“Shh! Speak softly—the fortune-teller is here,” she said, covering her mouth with her hand.

“He's asleep. Leave that aside—you are a married woman. How can you behave wrongly like this?” Durai asked her with a sense of right.

She decided that this was the moment and began her tale of tears:

“I am a married woman! I don't deny it. But does a woman's life end with the single ritual called marriage?”

“How could it end there? Marriage is a turning point in a woman's life! Isn't it only after that that women become teachers in the university called a good family?”

“You speak beautifully! But those who preach about womankind as a whole seem unable to understand the troubles of one individual woman! Isn't that so?”

“How could I fail to understand? A circus-man can feed goats to satisfy a tiger's hunger! Can he offer himself as the prey instead?”

“The goats you refer to……?”

“Advice!”

“Sugar written on a page isn't sweet.”

“The sugar I give you is not for the tongue, mother—it's for the heart!”

“?”

“Yes—the community of women is the community of mothers!”

“Hmm—you call me a tiger! Ayya, circus-man! I am not a tiger—I am a dove beating its wings to fly in the sky of pleasure! I am not a mother, young man! I am a dog tied up with the licence called the thaali! Once a woman is married, you untie your goats—your warnings that she must not take a wrong path……”

“What—the goats?”

“Yes, the advice! I am not a woman who has never read all this advice. I have read it—not only in school, but even in thick books! And I am continuing to read experiences that could themselves be offered in book form as evidence that such learning has no power to suppress the pulse of youth. Now! Young man! Look at me! A young woman full of vitality like the pearls of this pomegranate! Should a piece of sandalwood lie in the mud? If it did, would you simply look at it and walk away?”

“What else should I do? Can I pick it out of the mud and smear it on myself?”

“Forgive me—the piece of sandalwood was not the right example! I am a gomethagam gem thrown into a rubbish heap! Will you at least agree to that?”

“I agree. But I don't want to stir through the rubbish to pick up this gomethagam!”

“Why?”

“The gomethagam does not belong to me!”

“Oh! So you care only about private property?”

“In matters like this, amma, there must be no common ownership!”

“Don't waste time giving answers! Give this sinner a decision!”

“What decision do you expect from me?”

“What would a woman expect from a man?”

“Menaka!……” Durai could not speak. He could understand her pitiable condition. He truly grieved to see an educated woman caught in the hands of a rustic petty king and wandering in distress. Through her eyes he could see the flame of her desire to move through the brighter regions of the world rising within her. She longed for a life that would not be merely a cage of feeling but a flower-garden swaying with higher thoughts. The tears running from her eyes showed him that longing clearly. Menaka spoke again:

“Think whatever you like about me. I will tell you what I have to tell you. Think for yourself: am I a suitable wife for him? No, no—is he a suitable husband for me?”

“That question should have arisen in your heart before you sat in front of the homa-kundam!”

Menaka stood without speaking. There had come a moment when she had to sacrifice herself for her father, and she had become the sacrifice. Velliyambalam could have shown mercy without demanding such an enchanting breeze as an offering; but if he had done so, he could not have been Velliyambalam!

Wiping the tears that rolled down her cheeks, she looked at Durai and said with deliberate emphasis, “If you are willing to accept me, I can arrange a way for both of us to escape from here.” She kept her eyes on his face.

There was no sudden change of any kind on Durai's face. The only sudden change appeared on the face of Malayalam Bhagavathi, who lay in the corner pretending to sleep.

“Aha! Amma has set a wonderful condition—that's right! That's right!” he shouted, running to the window and standing beside Durai.

Seeing him get up, Menaka began to move away. At once Malayalam Bhagavathi called to her: “Wait, amma—Chinnamma has said with her own mouth that we are to be freed! Good times are being born—good times are being born! All that's left is for appan to nod his head to what amma is asking!” He kept talking on and on.

“Hey, Bhagavathi! Stop it!” Durai snapped.

“Appan mustn't try to frighten us with all that! Malayalam Bhagavathi—Sakkamma—Kaaliyayi—says our freedom will come through a woman! If Amma Bhagavathi says it, it will not prove false! Appane, tell this Chinnamma you'll give what she asks! Say it quickly!”

It comforted Menaka that the fortune-teller, seeking his own freedom, was speaking on her side.

“Ayya Bhagavathi! You tell him, ayya! Make his heart soften!” Menaka pleaded with him.

Malayalam Bhagavathi too poured out strong recommendations to Durai on Menaka's behalf. He began describing Menaka's beauty feature by feature. He even warned him that the sin of wronging a woman was dangerous.

“Yes—that is exactly why I am afraid to betray the woman who trusted me,” Durai replied.

Malayalam Bhagavathi stood smiling. Menaka too stood waiting for a decision.

At last the fortune-teller told Menaka firmly, “If this appan won't agree to become Chinnamma's bridegroom, Saibu will look and find the right match—the right match!”

Hearing that, she too pleaded, “Saibu! Somehow, if only I can get freedom from here, that is enough! I must escape from the hands of this brute and go away to some land where no one can see me.”

“Good times are being born! Freedom is being born for us!” Bhagavathi shouted.

Just then they heard Velliyambalam approaching, and Menaka ran away. Velliyambalam opened the room door and led Malayalam Bhagavathi outside.

Bhagavathi told Ambalam that the vashiyam was not yet complete, and that whether or not he obtained the secret from the man who was confined, Sakkamma Kaali had said one thing was certain: an old man would dig for the treasure that very night.

If that was so, Ambalam decided that they should go to the Sivan temple that night with his men. He put the fortune-teller back into the room, locked it and left.
