# 4

The boat carrying the old man cut through the sea. Since dawn was still a few hours away, the cruelty of the cold land-breeze was severe. He had curled his body like a chicken among the bundles stacked in the boat. There were several others aboard as well. One frightening-faced old man had a body that proclaimed, “Once, this frame was powerfully built.” It was hard to bear listening when he spoke; his voice was that harsh. The hair he had grown as a beard merged into his moustache. The two large ends of his moustache hung as though trying to reach the deep hollows at his shoulders. The greatest noise on the boat was the voice of that terrifying old man. Several young men were attending to their work. They struggled greatly to turn the sail according to the direction of the wind<!-- source join: scan 40 → 41 --> and keep the course right.

<!-- source: scan 41 -->

“Hey! Irulappa, careful! Careful!—don't overturn the boat—don't stand there lazily—if you slip up—that's the end........ Don't think we can just swim back! The owner will kill us! Yes........”

The frightening old fellow kept repeating the same thing, shouting in a grotesque voice. His speech sounded like an owl that had learnt a buffalo's tone and was trying to hoot with it. Could he at least have kept that dreadful throat shut? No. However energetically he ran about and worked inside the boat at that age, he spoke with equal energy. Even great waves in the sea take a little time to follow one another. His loud voice, however, sounded without stopping.

“Hey—Irulappa!”

“Hey! Kombaeri, don't fall asleep!”

“Hey—Pattani!—why the hell did you come? Idiot! Pay attention to the work, you ill-fated fellow!”

The old man continued his journey while letting his thoughts rest on that commanding voice. The fellow kept shouting enough to test even his patience.

<!-- source: scan 41 → 42 -->

“Appa! Ayyappa! Just keep quiet for a little while!” the old man said with some irritation. At once that grotesque old figure—yes, Ayyappan—looked at him and let out a laugh. Nothing shone in that laughter except coarse dryness.

Walking toward the old man, who was crouched among the bundles, Ayyappan shouted foolishly, imagining he was teasing him: “Oi, Vedanti! What are you shouting about? What's the news, ayya? Huh?”

“My name isn't Vedanti, appa! Mayandi......... is my name.”

“Old-man mischief still hasn't left you, ayya!” Ayyappan laughed and sat on a nearby bundle.

“You seem to imagine that you're a fine young man,” the elder said softly, shaking off his weariness and sitting a little straighter.

“If you're suffering like this already, ayya, how will you cover the rest of the distance? Will you die on the way?”

The old man's thoughts floated for a moment in Ayyappan's mockery.

“What if I die? Good! If I die at sea, at least this body will become food for the fish—”

<!-- source: scan 42 → 43 -->

“All the sea fish are starving without food! And this gentleman is going to donate his body! How will a fish eat your corpse, ayya? Fish don't know how to chew bones like dogs do.” (Mayandi laughed.)

“You speak like that in the arrogance of the flesh clinging to your body, old man! Am I a man made only of bone? All right! All right! You don't know the value of these bones!”

“Elephant tusks, are they? Worth a thousand gold even after death!”

“A thousand? If these bones alone die properly when I die, they'll be worth many lakhs in pure gold!”

“What does ‘dying properly’ mean, ayya?” Old Mayandi tried to turn the conversation elsewhere.

“That is, Ayyappa! I mean dying peacefully, without suffering.”

“Hmm—even the great Ramana Rishi got cancer and suffered before he died! Who knows how you and I are going to die? Yes, Mayandi! Suppose you do die without suffering—what does that have to do with lakhs upon lakhs of pure gold?”

“I just said it for fun. I keep saying that I'm a madman...”

“Oi, oi! Big man! Lie! Lie! Careful—tell the truth—or—the sea!—will remember—!”

<!-- source: scan 43 → 44 -->

“Eh, eh! What will you do?”

“I'll overturn the boat with the man in it! Yes! You've heard, haven't you? Right here—they say the whole family of the Marungappallam blacksmiths was overturned into the sea.”

“Oh, you know even that! Did you see it yourself?”

“How could I see it? Am I Maharaja Dasaratha, to live sixty thousand years and personally watch the stories of six hundred generations? They say the story of the Marungappallam blacksmith happened five hundred years ago!”

“Yes—yes! That's what I've heard too.”

“Then, Mayandi! People say all kinds of things about the treasure of Marungappallam—what is the truth?”

“What truth is there? It is true that the sea is blue—using that truth, they manufacture many invented stories! Solar and lunar eclipses occur—that is true! Using that as a basis, they have written many mythological stories! It is true that there is a continuous line of rock between Ceylon and India. Using that truth as their foundation, they build a story that monkeys constructed a bridge! It is true that a crow sees with one eye! On the screen of that truth, mixing the colours of imagination and using a divine brush—they painted a fabricated picture: Indra's<!-- source join: scan 44 → 45 --> son Jayanthan took the form of a crow and tasted Sita's body; angered by this, Rama turned arugam grass into an arrow and struck Jayanthan's eye; and from then on the whole race of crows had only one eye!

<!-- source: scan 45 -->

It is true that pale stripes like a faded rainbow run along a squirrel's back!

The explanation offered for that truth is the fabricated story that they are the marks of Rama's fingers when he stroked the squirrel! They say the squirrel too joined Hanuman's people and helped build the bridge—and therefore Rama praised it! In this way, stories have arisen in abundance—in abundance—by taking truths visible to the eye, invisible imaginings, and conjecture as their basis! And if there are fools like you ready to listen, what more need be said—the deceiver has a hunting ground!”

“So then—everyone who tells a story—is a cheat?”

“Not everyone. There are those who tell stories for the sake of stories. There are also those who tell stories precisely to deceive. But this story of Marungappallam—many may have been deceived by believing it, but it cannot have been invented by someone for the purpose of deceiving. If you look deeply<!-- source join: scan 45 → 46 --> enough, there is room to believe that such a story may indeed have happened!”

<!-- source: scan 46 -->

“Mayandi!... Tell me that story now. I've heard it from many people—let me hear it from your mouth too.”

“You can't hear it with my mouth! Listen with your own ears, appa!”

Ayyappan laughed foolishly, untied a bundle beside him, took out an onion and bit into it.

“Are all the bundles onions?” the old man asked.

“Yes—tell the story, ayya!” Ayyappan said impatiently.

“I'll tell it—hmm!........ What are you going to bring back in exchange for the onions?”

“In barter for onions—we'll bring coconut oil! Oi, old man! Don't change the subject. Tell the story!”

“I'll tell it,” Mayandi said, clearing his throat.

“Hey, boys! All of you watch properly as you go—don't overturn us!” Ayyappan barked at the men once and settled down to hear the story. The old man began.

Four or five hundred years ago, they say, the whole Marungappallam region was forest country. A goatherd would regularly take his goats into the forest and cut foliage for them. One day, while up a tree cutting leaves, he realised that the edge of his sickle had gone blunt. Since it had become too dull to cut branches or foliage easily, he sharpened it against a stone lying at the foot of the tree. But the more he sharpened it, the blunter the sickle became instead of gaining an edge. The herdsman understood nothing. He took the sickle to the house of the village blacksmith. “The more I sharpen this sickle, the blunter it gets!” he complained.

Whether the blacksmith replied, “Yes—some people's minds grow duller the more they study,” no one knows. He took the sickle and began heating it red-hot in the forge. As it heated, he saw a golden colour appearing all along its edge. The perceptive blacksmith realised that gold had adhered to the sickle. So he cleverly told the herdsman to return the next day and sent him away. Once the herdsman left, he heated the sickle properly until it turned entirely into gold. The blacksmith's joy knew no bounds. He hid it away, made another sickle just like it, and gave that to the herdsman when he returned the next day. The herdsman accepted it happily.<!-- source join: scan 47 → 48 --> The blacksmith then gently began a conversation with the happy herdsman and asked which stone he had used to sharpen the sickle the previous day.

<!-- source: scan 48 -->

The herdsman took the blacksmith with him to show the stone. After some difficulty they found it. The blacksmith looked at the stone casually and returned home. Once back, he could not sit still. That very night he entered the forest, secretly took the stone the herdsman had shown him, and brought it into his house.

From the next morning on, the blacksmith's work was collecting iron. He rubbed the iron he collected against the stone and then heated it red-hot until it became pure gold. This became his daily occupation. In this way he began accumulating gold by the lakhs. No one knew the secret of how the blacksmith's gold was multiplying. The blacksmith who made gold also had a beautiful daughter. Once he had become a wealthy man, he wished to marry his daughter into the high-caste group called Karai Vellalar and establish kinship with that community. The Karai Vellalars certainly desired to gain possession of the blacksmith's gold. But they detested the condition that, in order to do so, one of them would have to marry his daughter. That disgust sowed the seed of a violent idea. They decided somehow<!-- source join: scan 48 → 49 --> to plot against the blacksmith, kill him and seize his heaps of gold.

<!-- source: scan 49 -->

As people of a high caste, they raged that it was a great sin—an enormous insolence—for an ordinary blacksmith to imagine forming a marriage alliance with them. Somehow a conspiracy took shape. They went to the blacksmith and said they agreed to marry his daughter to their bridegroom, but that—according to the custom of their caste—the wedding had to take place at sea. The blacksmith, unaware of the plot, agreed. The groom's family and the bride's family set out in two or three boats. In one boat were the blacksmith, the young vine he had raised—his daughter—his son and several relatives; in another were the bridegroom and his family; and in a third were men arranged to carry out the conspiracy. Thus the three boats travelled out over the sea. The bride's family believed that, in accordance with caste custom, the wedding was going to take place at sea. The three boats stopped. A man from the Vellalar family called out loudly:

“Hmm! Let the wedding begin!”

Before he had even closed his mouth, the men in the third boat leapt into the bride's family's boat and overturned it straight into the sea.
