# 42

Menaka tried in every way to make use of the time available before they returned home. She could not. Malayalam Bhagavathi pretended to sleep, snoring, as though solely to see whether Menaka's attempt would succeed. Menaka stood at the window and called and called to Durai until she grew tired. Determined that, whatever happened, she would not leave without winning him over, Menaka went to her bedroom and fell heavily onto the cot. Durai too kept tossing without sleeping. The snoring of Malayalam Bhagavathi, who lay rolled up by the wall, was another disturbance to him. Malayalam Bhagavathi did not stop with snoring; he began rolling about as well. Poor fellow—perhaps he needed a long veranda to roll about freely in his sleep! Instead of rolling this way and that, he kept rolling steadily in one direction. Even in his sleep he muttered his kudukuduppai refrain, ‘Good times are being born,’ as he rolled.

Durai moved closer to the wall to make room for him. Before long weariness closed Durai's eyes. He had stepped through the first doorway of sleep. He felt as though saliva had fallen on his cheek. Half asleep, he touched his cheek. It was slightly wet. He looked back. Bhagavathi was snoring close beside him. “You sinner! Looks as though even this whole house isn't enough for you,” Durai muttered, and moved to lie down on the other side.

He began to sleep again. It seemed as though something had fallen on his cheek. Yes—a little dream in half-sleep! It felt as though two rose blossoms had fallen on his cheek. He raised a hand to pick them up. There were no flowers. His cheek was wet. He looked up at the roof. Wondering whether it might have been some lizard or something, he turned. Malayalam Bhagavathi lay rolled up close by.

Durai felt laughter and anger rise together. “What is this? Why are you rolling around like an animal?” he snapped, shoving Bhagavathi away with all his strength. Bhagavathi, as though drunk with sleep, muttered something and rolled to the other side. Durai shut his eyes tightly and prepared to sleep. He sensed Bhagavathi rolling closer again. He lay still and watched.

Bhagavathi, having rolled near, slowly rose and closed the window shutters. Suspicion arose in Durai. He waited eagerly to see what would happen. Bhagavathi took a gleaming knife hidden at his waist and placed it on the floor. Durai's fear increased. He wondered whether he should seize the knife. He concluded that Bhagavathi was about to endanger his life.

Bhagavathi must be part of Ambalam's arrangement—or else some villain who had come sworn to finish him off. A storm struck Durai's heart. If he delayed even one more second, death was certain—so he thought. In the midst of the thought itself he sprang at the knife on the floor, took it in his hand and stood fiercely before Bhagavathi.

Bhagavathi merely threw him a foolish smile and asked, “What do you intend to do with me now?”

“I am going to send you, who came to kill me, to the world of the dead.”

“Is that so? Such a great tiger, are you? Come then—let the knife plunge into my chest and let us see!” Bhagavathi said fiercely and stood there. Durai too stood motionless with the knife raised.

“Hmm—go on, then. Why the delay?” Bhagavathi asked, removing his beard and moustache.

At once Durai dropped the knife and embraced Bhagavathi. Whether he said, “My dear—Parimala!” or not, no one could say.

But every time, he received kisses from her in return. He also now knew who had been wetting his cheek while he slept!

For a few minutes they forgot the place in which they were trapped and the dangers waiting before them. Then they returned to the world burdened with memory.

They heard Ambalam and his men returning. The two separated, opened the window again and lay in different corners pretending to sleep.

Ambalam shone a torch inside. Both appeared to be asleep. Only Parimala, in Bhagavathi's disguise, looked up as though her sleep had been disturbed. Ambalam stood by the window and signalled to her. Bhagavathi went to him and asked, “Well? Was what Saibu said right?”

“Very right! Very right! But Saibu—you mustn't let this Durai go! Because he too may know something. Well? Have you tried anything?”

“I am trying. Bhagavathi says that even if the treasure is found, the secret of how to manage it is with this man! He is no ordinary fellow. He won't spit out the truth. Within four days Saibu will somehow get the secret out of him and give it to Ayya. Ayya mustn't forget us. An old cloth—an old sari—a veshti……”

“Hey, Saibu! I'll give you plenty of all that. You finish the matter successfully!”

Saying this, Ambalam moved away. Durai went close to Parimala. She closed the window shutters again. Ambalam went to the bedroom and pinched with his rough fingers the cheek of Menaka, who lay curled up there, and woke her.

He happily told her that signs had appeared that the treasure would be found. Old Mayandi had been caught properly. The kudukuduppai Bhagavathi would also draw out from Durai whatever secrets about the treasure still remained. Once the treasure was obtained, Mayandi would be the first sacrifice! Durai the second! Malayalam Bhagavathi the third! None of the three could be left alive, because all three knew secrets about the treasure and could betray them. Fortunately that fellow Dukkaram had been locked in prison. Even if he were released and came back, he too could not be spared. One way or another, four murders had become unavoidable!

Menaka was frightened merely to hear Ambalam's terrible plans. She wanted to ask: “Should we alone live after taking four lives? Is it right for such a terrible desire to arise? To collect honey, people kill thousands of bees. You say you will turn human beings into corpses to obtain treasure! What harm have those human insects done to you?” She wanted to ask all this—but she did not. If she asked, it would not have been surprising if she too had been given a place in that row of corpses.

With her heart burning, Menaka leaned against Ambalam's broad chest. Her face looked like a lotus flower lying upon a rough mountain rock. Embracing in memory the beautiful body of Durai locked in the room, she lay leaning against Ambalam. Mistaking her tears for tears of joy, Ambalam stroked her cheek with his ‘Negro lips’.

“What all will happen when tomorrow dawns?” With such thoughts roaming through his mind, Mayandi lay on the veranda of Ambalam's house surrounded by guards. Dawn came. Ambalam and Mayandi went in the direction indicated on the rolled metal plate. The clue brought them to the entrance of the local school. They marked with a line the place specified on the plate.

“If we dig here, the villagers will find out,” Ambalam said doubtfully.

“We can say we are digging a well to provide water for the school, and finish our own business,” Mayandi suggested.

“Splendid idea! We'll do exactly that!” Ambalam nodded.

“Ambalam is going to dig a well for the schoolchildren. He has taken up a charitable work,” the villagers began saying. Men gathered. A broad, deep well was dug. A well in a single day! The next day they would have to begin lining it with stone. As they dug, water kept seeping in, but there was no sign at all of treasure, and Ambalam fretted. “Let it stop here for today. We'll come at night and examine it without anyone knowing,” Mayandi advised.

The villagers came in crowds and happily looked at the well being dug as a charitable work. They praised Ambalam's service too. Ambalam and Mayandi looked at each other and smiled knowingly. How could the villagers know the meaning of that smile?

Waiting impatiently for night to come, Ambalam set off towards the school with Mayandi. Menaka, who had been waiting for him to leave the house, came to the window of Durai's room. Durai welcomed Menaka. That itself surprised her. Menaka realized with delight that his usually stern face had changed. Thinking that she had won, she swelled with pride. Just then Malayalam Bhagavathi too came to the window, shouting, “Good times are being born!”

“Chinnamma! You've won! Saibu's word has come true! Ayya has taken a liking to you! Would Saibu let Ayya escape?” Bhagavathi rattled on.

“Truly?” Menaka asked.

Durai nodded, “Yes.” Menaka could not contain her excitement.

“Shall the two of us leave tonight itself?” she asked.

“Be sure to take me along too, Chinnamma! Merit will come to your children and little ones!” pleaded Parimala in her Bhagavathi disguise.

“Aha! A new turn has come in my life! Fulfilment has come! Freedom, freedom! Malayalam Bhagavathi, long live! Wait—let him come. We'll get the key from him and all of us will run away. I too will prepare what we need for the journey. There are plenty of jewels, ornaments and money. I'll take all of it and hide it right now,” Menaka said, unable to contain her happiness, and bounded away.

Here, Parimala's cheek grew wet.
