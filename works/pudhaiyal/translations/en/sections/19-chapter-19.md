# 19

“Appa was brought before the court. People in the street cursed him as a great sinner who had murdered his wife. Grandmother would say that the pitying way people in the village looked at her felt like torture inflicted while keeping her alive. When the villagers said, ‘Ayyo, poor thing—at this helpless age this old woman has lost her daughter-in-law to death and her son as well, and now she staggers on,’ it brought her both tears and anger. Grandmother, who kept looking at me, her ten-year-old grandson, and at my precious five-year-old sister and crying, waited anxiously for the course of the case. She kept yearning that somehow her beloved son might at least escape the death sentence and receive life imprisonment instead. How could grandmother, already an old thing sitting at home like overripe fruit, bear to see the lamp of her family fall dead before her? We were only buds! Grandmother, who bore the responsibility of protecting us, was a ripened leaf. If she too suddenly died, who would protect the two of us—what would become of us? How could grandmother keep from sighing? The day came for judgment in Appa's case. Grandmother took both of us to court. Appa stood in the dock. My sister Thangam struggled to go to him. I stood there shedding tears. Grandmother stood like a statue.

“Appa looked at us once, then never turned toward us again. Judgment was pronounced. Appa was not given the death sentence. He was given life imprisonment—fifteen years of his life were to roll away in prison. Without looking at us again, Appa left the court with the police.

“The words ‘Valli must die, or I must die’ and the words ‘Valli must be cut on the first day’ were used greatly to make Appa a criminal. When grandmother visited him in prison, Appa apparently wept and told her what had happened.

“Weeping and choking, Appa explained to grandmother what he had meant when he wrote, ‘On the first day Valli must be cut.’ I have already told you that along with his work on the tea estate Appa had leased a plot and grown tubers there. What he had written in his diary was that on the first day of the month the valli tubers in that field had to be cut and taken out. Without even considering whether a man planning a murder would write it down beforehand in a way that would trap himself, they used those words to ruin a man's life. For the sake of winning garlands of praise for skill in argument and cleverness as a lawyer, fifteen years were cut out of an innocent man's life. The words ‘Valli must be cut’ helped the government prosecutor. In the same way, if Appa had possessed money and obtained a good lawyer, that lawyer too could have used the same words cleverly.

“His argument might have been: ‘It says only that Valli must be cut. But this Valliyammal died from stabbing. Therefore the diary entries cannot be evidence making Kumaravadivu guilty!’ Then Appa might have been acquitted. Appa did not have the means to conduct the case with such a lawyer.

“They say that in many cases like this my grandfather had poured out gold and wealth to save the lives of many innocent people. Now judgment had been given that his beloved son, an orphan with no one, must lie in a cage for fifteen years. They kept troubling Appa by transferring him from prison to prison. Grandmother struggled terribly to raise us.

“Years rolled by. I may have been about fifteen. Thangam had grown to around ten. Grandmother's health failed and she fell bedridden. After that she never rose again. For about a month grandmother fought with nature.

“She longed greatly to remain alive a little longer to protect us both. She struggled desperately with the wish that she might entrust the two of us into someone's hands and then close her eyes in peace. In that aged heart, ripened by time and scored with the cuts of experience, waves of thought about us surged and roared.

“I still cannot forget the sight of grandmother clasping me and Thangam with trembling hands and crying. When grandmother's arms, reduced almost to bones, rested on me, they gave me so much comfort. I would think: these are the same arms that have cherished, praised and raised the two of us all these days. I would embrace Thangam and cry; Thangam too would wail. During the days grandmother suffered in the agony of death, Thangam and I hardly ate properly. Many days we went hungry. The kindness of the neighbours kept us alive. At last the day came when grandmother was to close her eyes. That morning signs of death began appearing on her face. The moment approached when that family tree—hardened like diamond after experiencing the joys and sorrows of the world and meeting both poles of life—was about to fall from the root. That day grandmother kept both of us close and held us. We sat on either side, overcome by the feeling of her touch and lost to ourselves. Amma, decomposed where she had been buried—her body returned to earth—had prepared the ground for our grandmother. Appa—whether he was being beaten by some warder in some prison, whether he was suffering through some hard labour, whether he was even alive or dead—we did not know. We sat in anguish knowing that in only a few hours we were going to lose the one support we still had. Grandmother said nothing. She looked from one of us to the other and drew our faces against her own. Tears gathered in her eyes. Who knows what she thought? What do old people desire when death comes? That their son should light the funeral fire and their grandchildren should hold the ghee lamps, is it not? How could grandmother bear the thought that her innocent son, unable to perform that final duty, was somewhere suffering torture in prison? Whether it was tears for her son or anxiety about our future, it pierced grandmother's heart like the holes of a sieve.

“Whatever the thought was, grandmother kept crying. All the neighbours gathered to send her on her way.

“Suddenly grandmother reached a state in which she could no longer speak. I understood that she would never speak again. But Thangam did not understand and cried, ‘Grandmother! Grandmother!’ Grandmother signalled with her hands that she could not speak and held us tightly. ‘Her life will go in a little while,’ the neighbours said freely. Whether those words reached our precious grandmother's ears, I do not know. She pressed us hard against herself. Our faces sank into her face.

“Grandmother's tears and the tears of us tender shoots mingled together. Her embrace grew stronger and stronger. We too clung to her without moving. Then the strong grip slowly loosened. At last grandmother's hands fell away from our bodies. Ayyo—the death that had taken our mother now pecked away the beloved grandmother who had raised us even more dearly than a mother.

“‘Rock, little one, rock!’—that affectionate song from the toothless mouth would never again be heard in that hut.

“‘Clap, girl, clap! Clap for five or six coins!’—the lips that had sung play-songs to my sister closed and would not open again. The neighbours hurried through the duties they had to perform, gave grandmother to the earth, and returned to their homes. We were alone. Our house had become dark. I, fifteen or sixteen years old, and my ten-year-old sister were driven into running a household. In that pitch-dark darkness where storm and thunder were rising, could two tender shoots like us possibly conduct a journey? Who was there for us? We asked ourselves this question. We asked it aloud, again and again. No answer came. If two young birds like us had been left alone in a wealthy house after its important elder died, relatives from every corner would have rushed in first, surrounding the place while planning who could take what share. But who was there to stand beside orphans like us? Question marks multiplied. We sat on the street side of the house, looking toward the direction in which grandmother's body had gone. That alone gave some comfort to our broken hearts. Compassionate people of the street gave us little bits of help. But what could they do? They too lived from day to day.

“While we stood bewildered without knowing a direction, a man arrived saying he would show us a good path. His saffron robe and the mantra ‘God is our help’ announced him as a swami. The swami pitied our condition. He was not a resident of Ceylon; he had come from India. He approached us, asked us to come with him to India, and said he would arrange for both of us to live comfortably there. The people of the street too praised the swami to the skies and advised that it would be good for us to go to India with him. I did not like the idea of my sister growing up on the edge of the tea forests. I had concluded that my sister too living in a region of wild animals would become a terrible problem that had to be somehow managed. I gained courage from the thought that we could come to India and survive somehow. A good job for me; a good husband for Thangam—if only those two things could be obtained, that was enough. With that hope I left Ceylon...... I did not then know that the swami was taking us along with an evil intention. They said a human sacrifice was needed to recover the treasure at Marungappallam—and that it had to be a very young girl. They believed that only if the girl's blood was sprinkled on the earth would the place containing the treasure burst open. Only later did I learn that with such a belief that sinner had taken me and my beloved sister away.”
