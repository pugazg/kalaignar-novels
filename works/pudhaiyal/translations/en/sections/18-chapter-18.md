# 18

“Amma, not understanding Mary's improper act, formed a wrong opinion of my father. She believed that her husband had committed against her an unbearable cruelty. She suffered in her heart, thinking that the companion for whom she had poured out a rain of love and whom she had cared for day and night had brought an indelible disgrace upon their marital bond itself. Once she concluded that the white woman had captured his heart, Appa too decided that no amount of reconciliation would change her. Amma's anguish was: I placed my very life upon him, and he has deceived me like this!

“Appa's anger was: I granted her alone the right to rule my heart, and she has hurried into suspicion of me like this!

“So Amma did not come to reconcile with Appa. Appa too did not wish to reconcile Amma. Grandmother understood none of these details. She knew only that her son and daughter-in-law were not speaking to each other. She assumed it was a quarrel between husband and wife. As time passed grandmother too became suspicious and tried to learn the reason. Neither of them told her.

“‘She is pregnant, da! You must not let her mind be troubled!’ All the advice grandmother gave Appa proved useless.

“For Amma, who was carrying Thangam in her womb, I alone was some comfort. They say she would hold me in her arms and stand staring somewhere in thought. Only after grandmother came near and called, ‘Valli,’ would her thought break and she turn in a start. What else could she have been thinking about except the sight she had seen that day in Dog Durai's bungalow?

“In this condition my younger sister too was born. Amid the stench of poverty, the estrangement between Appa and Amma, and grandmother's anxiety, my sister and I grew without worrying about any of it.

“Years rolled by, one and then another. Appa and Amma did not reunite. Those who had not spoken to each other now began speaking indirectly and through hints.

“But those exchanges served only to widen the split between them; they did not create a peaceful condition again. Indeed, one could say that the conversations began specifically to wound each other's minds. Grandmother did not know what to do. The family struggled, trapped in the mud of confusion.

“We have read in the story of Nalayini that she carried her husband to the courtesan's house!

“But my Amma, without investigating fully, suspected Appa and became a cause of the storm.

“The three incidents Appa encountered in life led him toward new doctrines. He concluded that the love of Kuppan and Seetha was a false drama played by heartless people.

“He saw the love of Mary and Joseph as a pleasure-show for satisfying bodily lust.

“Finally, taking the quarrel between husband and wife in his own family, he concluded that women were hasty creatures of suspicion who gave no value to truth. Using those three events as the centre, he wrote new grammars. He had the habit of keeping a diary, but instead of giving much space on particular months and dates to things that were to happen or had happened, he gave more of those pages to explanations of the new philosophies he believed he had discovered.

“‘One grain of cooked rice is enough to test a pot of rice,’ says the proverb. As far as one pot of rice is concerned, the proverb is correct. But can one say that the single grain from this one pot is the test for the rice in every pot? Periyavare! Since you too hold ideas about love similar to my father's, I want to ask a question like this. Please do not take it wrongly. Everything found in a gold mine will not be either a solid bar of gold or mere stone. The two will be mixed, won't they? In one family one man may be good and another wicked. Can we test the good man like a single grain of rice and declare that everyone in the family is good? Or use the wicked man to conclude that the entire family is a tent filled with cruel people?

“Without giving even a little work to thoughts like these, Appa took two or three events as his basis and hurriedly wrote a commentary upon the relation between all men and women in the world. Those commentaries may have been composed in excellent chains of words in the diaries he piled up, but they did not become useful counsel. One thing can be said: Appa's explanations may be left as writings made for his own peace of mind. But I do not much like the idea of preaching those opinions to others, as you do.

“Periyavare! Do not be annoyed that I have wandered somewhere away from my story. Here I am back in it. The diaries Appa wrote year after year were not thrown into the rubbish; he himself kept them carefully. So that Amma would not see them, he bundled all those old diaries together and hid them on the loft. When Amma was killed and Appa arrested, only two diaries were found.

“One diary had been completely written. Another had only then been bought and was new. The completed diary was the diary Appa had finished when I was nine years old. The words in that diary, and the information he had written on its last page about something to be done on the first day of the month, were what trapped him in the hands of the police.”

*     *     *     *

Even while quarrels between Amma and Appa kept increasing, there was no deficiency at all in their work on the tea estate. After sending me to school, Amma, Appa and grandmother would carry little Thangam and go into the tea fields. They would work as usual. They would return home. At first Appa did not go to work in Dog Durai's house. Later, after Mary and Joseph married and she went away with him, Appa again did work in the durai's bungalow. Amma too suppressed her anguish and worked in Dog Durai's bungalow. Dog Durai's wicked thought toward Amma never changed. Though many women were available to him without hesitation—through Kangani Kalimuthu and through Seetha, his kept woman—the attitude took deep root in him that there must be nothing in the world he could not obtain.

The anger spread its roots in him that a mere labourer on his own estate could not be made to submit to his wishes. It was not like a child taking one bite from the fruit in its hand, throwing it away, and insisting that someone bring down the moon from the sky. It was like a bear that, not content with hunting and killing goats and cattle, approaches the honeycomb without fear though thousands of bees swarm around it in resistance. Thus he wandered in frenzy. We have heard the story that the little fox jumped and jumped, then finally walked away saying the grapes were sour. But this white fox forgot the saying, ‘If it cannot be had, forget it at once,’ and took the advice ‘effort brings achievement’ as support for this brutal action. He bound himself by a vow that somehow he would bend my mother to his will.

One day—yes, that was the day our family began to be ruined from the root. On that day of suffering, Appa and grandmother had gone to work on the estate. Amma was unwell. Thangam and Amma were at home. I had gone to school. Dog Durai had gone around inspecting the tea fields. Amma was not there. Through Kangani Kalimuthu he learned that Amma was at home. From there Dog Durai set out toward our house with Kangani Kalimuthu. Yes, that frenzied beast set out toward our hut in search of prey for his fangs. Was it not a law on that jungle king's estate that a woman who survived on wages paid by him must be ready to surrender her honour as well? What authority did a poor man's wife possess to raise a voice against it? With that arrogant question-mark dancing in his heart, the sinner went toward my mother. Appa, who noticed Dog Durai and Kalimuthu speaking together, grew suspicious. He came to grandmother and said, ‘Amma! I am going to the garden, I'll dig up the tubers and return in half an hour,’ and left.

Appa had a garden he had taken on lease and planted with tubers. Grandmother thought that was where he was going. Appa instead hurried toward home.

By then Dog Durai had reached Amma. Appa ran quickly and entered the house. What did Appa see there? He saw Amma floating in a pool of blood. The lamp of Amma's life was slowly going out. Stab wounds in her side, back, chest and face, and blood gushing out, were dragging her rapidly toward the doorway of death. For five years Appa had not called her ‘Valli’ with flowing affection. Now he gathered Amma into his arms, laid her on his lap and cried, ‘Valli! Valli!’ Even then Amma behaved toward Appa with a hard heart. Without speaking directly to him, as though answering someone else, she said, ‘Dog Durai came to plunder me. I resisted and told him not to come near. When he saw the knife in my hand he became frightened and drew back. Kalimuthu sprang in to help him. That was all! The knife changed into their hands! After that...... But...... unlike a man who lost firmness before a white woman, this woman has won the pride of protecting her honour from a white dog. It is certain that I am going to die. I must send my final respects to Athai. She will not come before then. So...... if you tell her—to take good care of my children, that will be good.’

These words came out worn and faint. Amma closed her eyes for the last time. Little Thangam fell upon Amma and rolled about crying, while Amma lay motionless, unable even to touch my precious sister. Appa too stood there unable to act.

At that moment the police force entered. It was Dog Durai's arrangement, a service performed through Kangani Kalimuthu. The police searched the house. Appa's old diaries did not fall into their hands. But the two diaries that would trap Appa did.

Those diaries lying in a broken cupboard helped betray Appa.

One diary contained the words: ‘Either Valli must die; or I must die. Only then will there be peace in this family!’

Another line on the last page of that same diary greatly favoured the government prosecutor.

‘First day—Valli must be cut.’ That too was in Appa's handwriting.

Was that not enough evidence for the police?”
