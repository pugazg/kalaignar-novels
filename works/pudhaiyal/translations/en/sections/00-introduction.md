# Introduction

There are landscapes where the footprints of history have faded without clarity. Ruined halls tell the story of antiquity with pathos. Trees stretch out branch-arms to hide the sun. Palmyra groves bend and rise as though to touch the sky, hanging down their fruit-laden heads and silently declaring their defeat. Amid all this stands that beautiful seven-storeyed tower, high like the valiant Napoleon unshaken even in defeat; at the lap of that tower the queen of the sea comes bounding and playing. By day, as though competing with the crescent in the night sky, the blue sea-queen displays sailboats floating upon her.

**Saluva Nayakkan Pattinam** is the name of that place. It is unfamiliar even to many who live in Thanjavur district. It can be reached by sea from Adirampattinam. It can also be reached by land from Peravurani through the small village called Nadiyam. Along the Tamil coast, villages and towns are very often given the distinguishing suffix “Pattinam.” Between Adirampattinam and Saluva Nayakkan Pattinam lies another charming little village called Puduppattinam. Most of the people of Puduppattinam live by depending on the sea. At any hour, nets can be seen drying in its streets, or damaged nets are being made new again. The smell of dried fish

keeps coming, fragrant in its own pungent way. Logs, boats and fishing gear lie scattered through the streets; children sit upon them and play. The sea itself, one might say, is the reason this little settlement of only four or five streets looks beautiful and gives pleasure to anyone seeking rest. A small mosque shows that Muslim people too live in the village. The low platforms built opposite it are where the villagers sit to enjoy the sea breeze. Though Puduppattinam and Saluva Nayakkan Pattinam are not far apart, travelling by sea is the easier journey.

The name Saluva Nayakkan Pattinam is not used much among the people of the region. The reason is that there is nothing there of what one expects in a pattinam—no houses, no streets, no settlement at all. The place has instead come to be called by the name of the seven-storeyed tower that stands there.

**Manora!** Yes, that is the familiar name by which Saluva Nayakkan Pattinam is known; Manora is the name of that beautiful tower. The word “minar” must, over time, have changed into Manora. Nagore in Thanjavur district is a region where Muslims live. The towers rising from the dargah there are also called manoras. Perhaps the same manner of naming was applied to this tower as well! Manora was built by King Sarabhoji, who ruled Thanjavur. The inscriptions say that this memorial was erected as a sign<!-- source join: scan 8 ends `அடை`; scan 9 begins `யாளமாக` --> announcing the victory-news that Napoleon had been defeated by the British.

<!-- source: scan 8 / printed 6 → scan 9 / printed 7 -->

A faded story also circulates: seeing British domination taking firm root, the king of Thanjavur feared it and built a fortified camp—yes, a war-fort—to oppose them; before the arrangements he intended could be completed, news of it reached the British; and, to escape the questions raised by the white rulers, King Sarabhoji answered that it was a monument to victory. Whether it was a camp of resistance or a hall praising an enemy's victory is not known. But Manora's structure is not merely attractive to the eye; it also resembles a fort in which soldiers could stay. Manora is a building made entirely with wooden beams, well-prepared lime and solid brick. In damaged portions one can see how ropes were wound around the wooden beams, stones laid over them and lime plastered on top, perhaps so the timber would not rot in the flood of time. The lime has fallen away. The stone has broken. Only the rope wound around the wood looks as though it were twisted today.

People also say that there was an underground cave in Manora which was later sealed, and they point, approximately, to traces of where it stood. It is said that if one entered that cave, one could reach the nearby village called Marungappallam.

<!-- source: scan 9 / printed 7 → scan 10 / printed 8 -->

There are many in the region who argue, with what they consider proof, that “Marundhu Pallam” eventually became Marungappallam.

A stone called **pon-kuttam** is found there in abundance. They say that when goldsmiths melt gold, specks of gold melt into a particular kind of sand they use, spill, harden, and turn into stone. Such stone can be found everywhere in the village of Marungappallam. People take that pon-kuttam stone, prepare medicine from it, and give it for ailments such as pitham, sogai and **pandu**, curing them. For that reason, local people say it would be more fitting to call Marungappallam “Marundhu Pallam.”

Another investigation goes on there too, still without reaching a conclusion. Gold worth many lakhs is said to lie buried somewhere in a corner of the village. A great bewilderment persists because no one knows with certainty where to dig for it.

Why is pon-kuttam found in such abundance? Some offer the result of their own reasoning: many goldsmiths must once have melted bars of gold there, and the pon-kuttam formed in the process of melting must be what is now scattered throughout Marungappallam.

“Everyone in the village kept describing Sevathan's daughter Karuppayi as being the colour of a lemon. But when they finally went and saw her—

<!-- source: scan 10 / printed 8 → scan 11 / printed 9 -->

she had pandu disease; the doctor said that was why she had gone so pale. There wasn't a medicine they hadn't tried. At last they made a medicine from pon-kuttam and kept giving it to her. Now Karuppayi has turned into Katta Bomman's consort—she got married and has even had a child—” Such a conversation is bound to occur now and then in the verandah-talk of Marungappallam.

If a stone strikes the foot of someone walking along the road and they discover that it is a pon-kuttam stone, each person will tell a story about its greatness and make the rest of the journey without feeling the strain. In all these ways, the pon-kuttam stone seems not only to announce the secret that immense heaps of gold are buried in that soil; people also say that a few lines in the Siva temple amid the village fields confirm the news of buried gold even more strongly:

“Between the one who rises and the one who bows,  
In the shadow of the crow's beak,  
Even if found, in the eyes of the ‘Kammalar’.”

Those are the lines. Who is “the one who rises”? Who is “the one who bows”? What does “the shadow of the crow's beak” mean? Does “Kammalar” refer only to a goldsmith? Would not questions like these arise in the minds of those who dream<!-- source join: scan 11 ends `கனவு`; scan 12 begins `காண்பவர்களின்` --> of gold worth lakhs! And who can deny that such waves of thought have the power to swell, in some people, the raw selfish desire to become the sole sovereign of those hoards of gold?

<!-- source: scan 11 / printed 9 → scan 12 / printed 10 -->

At night—at dark hours when the moon cannot be seen—who knows how many greedy people have gone out carrying spades, dug in places they had selected and roughly confirmed by ritual signs, and returned disappointed without finding pots of gold!

Thus, tempting many, deceiving many, celebrated by many, cursed by some who bore the hardship and failed, Marungappallam has acquired a character of its own. It is a beautiful village to see. A small road; along it, a canal that gives life to the fields. On the canal banks stand thatched huts like pigeon-holes. Marungappallam is a land where Mother Nature spreads a green silk garment, pours out beauty and gives delight—a region of controversy. Adirampattinam, Puduppattinam, Saluva Nayakkan Pattinam, Manora, Marungappallam, Peravurani and Nadiyam: these are the towns and regions to which we are now being introduced. The story of a few human lives that will circle through these places, cross the sea, strike the shores of Ceylon and return will make great use of the places we have just met.
