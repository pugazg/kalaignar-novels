# 35

Without the slightest agitation, Dukkaram walked behind them. He went along with the pride of a man who had accomplished a marvellous deed that no one in the world could even imagine doing. He even imagined that the government, in appreciation of such a great act of sacrifice, had adorned his hands with silver bracelets and was taking him in procession through the streets.

It is indeed astonishing that such a change should arise in the hearts of young men like Dukkaram. Only when exceptions appear in the ordinary course of the world do a few people become worthy of remembrance among the countless millions who are born and die in this world.

Rightly or wrongly, he had formed an unchanging attachment to her. He had suffered many troubles in the illusion Ambalam had created. Many times he too had felt the desire to save his life. Yet he could not bear for long the fact that Parimalam—the embodiment of his passion standing before him—hated him, and at the same time was ready to sacrifice herself for the sake of his life. It became an event capable of awakening the compassion hanging within his heart.

Mere desire—frenzy—thirst—all were pushed aside, and a spirit of sacrifice raised its head. The “beast” that had driven him went into hiding. Dukkaram rose as a human being.

Parimalam remained shut inside the kitchen, her eyes seeming to ask, “Is this that man's final journey?” The outer bolt opened and Ambalam came in. Tears rolled like pearls from her eyes, as though the lips of her heart were ceaselessly shedding “curses.”

Ambalam asked with suspicion and some agitation, “What is it, girl? Did you fall victim to that fellow's lust?”

“No! It was Dukkaram who became the victim! Dukkaram sacrificed his life for the sake of my life of love! He is the one who destroyed the dreams of his youth in order to strengthen the foundation of your mansion of desire!” Parimalam cried.

“What nonsense are you talking? Why did he shut you in here?”

“You have shut me in the big house—to profit from me! He shut me in this kitchen—to save me!”

“Speak so I can understand!”

“Dukkaram—a man of sacrifice! A man of sacrifice!! Do you understand at least now?”

He had never even heard words of that kind. So he could not grasp the life-epic of a young man contained in Parimalam's emotional words.

“You are only worried because I have kept you confined, aren't you, Parimalam? Do you know why I have taken on so much trouble and wandered about like this? What concern is it of mine? Why should I struggle so much to free Durai? Think about it a little!” he said dramatically.

“I have thought about it! I also know why you are struggling! Sacrifice! This too is one kind of sacrifice!” she said, holding back her tears.

“Your troubles will all end today, Parimalam!”

“Are you going to kill me?”

“Kill you?”

“No. I was asking whether the treasure needs some sacrifice or other!”

“Madness! Madness!! That is not what I mean. Today I am going to send you away with your lover Durai. Why are you staring? Do you know that Durai and Mayandi have been released?”

Parimalam did not dance with as much joy as he had expected upon hearing the news. Dukkaram's fate, and the uncertainty about what games Ambalam would play next, had plunged her into confusion.

“Do not be afraid, Parimalam! Tomorrow Durai will come here. You and he can leave this village without anyone seeing you. I myself will make every arrangement for you and Durai to live your life of love at a distance beyond the reach of eyes and ears,” he said, watching her face.

Though she knew he had no real concern for their love, Parimalam firmly believed he would bring Durai there only to carry out some plot of his own. She grieved that the treasure near the Siva temple at Marungappallam had entered her life like an earthquake. With the resolve that when Durai came the next night she must somehow find a way to escape from this brute, Parimalam curled up and lay down.

* * * *

Would he be able to meet Parimalam? Was what Ambalam had said true? With those doubts, Durai and Mayandi reached Puduppattinam.

As soon as they arrived, Mayandi ran to the place where he had hidden the treasure secret. It was still there as he had left it. He took it and tied it securely at his waist.

When they heard that Durai had returned, all his friends came running. Durai spent the hours impatiently waiting for the next night—when would it come, when would he be able to meet Parimalam?

Mayandi alone had little faith in Ambalam's words. They also could not understand why it had suddenly become necessary to release them. As a precaution against any danger from Ambalam, it was decided that Durai should go to the Manora with ten friends.

It was also decided that old Mayandi should go to Marungappallam and find out whether Parimalam was there, and that he was the right person for the task.

The awaited time arrived. Mayandi set out toward Marungappallam. Durai and his friends left for the Manora.

Villages such as Marungappallam were very familiar places to old Mayandi. All the villagers would speak to him with sympathy. Believing that such sympathy would help in the work he had come to do, Mayandi went to the village.

First he entered Thandavan's house. Thandavan's wife sat there with a sorrowful face. Thandavan himself sat on the front platform of the house, anger and grief competing with each other within him.

“What, Thandavan—are you well?” Mayandi asked, sitting there.

Wondering how Mayandi, who had gone to prison, could have returned so quickly, Thandavan asked, “When did you come back from jail?”

“I came back almost as soon as I went. Leave that aside—have you found your daughter?” Mayandi asked.

Instead of Thandavan answering, Parimalam's mother explained the situation through her lament: Parimalam had not returned.

After talking there for a while, Mayandi took leave because he had obtained no clue about Parimalam.

Night came. Mayandi slowly walked along the road by Velliyambalam's house. He learned that Ambalam was not at home. He asked a few people whether he might be at his bungalow beside the canal.

“He cannot reasonably be there either. That bungalow has been locked for two days,” one man replied.

Another said, “They say a murderer had been confined in that house, and yesterday the police arrested him.”

Mayandi became suspicious of the bungalow and walked toward it.

The servant Raman was sitting at its entrance. Seeing Mayandi coming, he ran up and welcomed him. “Come, grandfather!”

“Where is Velliyambalam?” Mayandi asked as he sat on the raised platform of the house.

“He... he... has gone somewhere,” Raman said evasively.

“The door is locked, but a lamp is burning inside. Why is that, boy?” Mayandi asked.

“So that Lakshmi's radiance will remain, the lamp is burning,” Raman improvised.

Mayandi called him affectionately. “Raman! I need a little snuff. Will you go and buy some?” He was looking for a way to send him away.

“I have snuff with me, grandfather; here!” Raman said, handing over the box.

Mayandi had no habit of taking snuff. Still, he took some and began sneezing. He was desperate to send Raman away somehow so that he could discover who was inside.

“Raman, do you have a cheroot?” he asked.

“Here!” Raman said, holding one out.

Now Mayandi had to suffer through smoking that too.

From inside, Parimalam could clearly hear Mayandi and Raman talking. Wondering whether it was old Mayandi's voice, she looked out through a crack in the wall. It was indeed him, and she rejoiced.

Unable to think how to let him know she was inside, she deliberately shouted as though she needed something: “Raman! Raman! Who is that, Raman?”

Mayandi understood at once. Raman began to tremble.

“What is that woman's voice, boy?” Mayandi asked.

“I knew it all along! I keep saying there is a Mohini ghost in this house! Does Ayya listen?” Raman said, pretending to shiver.

Mayandi too pretended to be afraid of ghosts. “Ayyo! Ghosts are no good for me! I am leaving!” he said and began walking quickly away.

Raman went back inside. “Hey, girl! Keep quiet!” he said, then spread his bedding on the front platform. Time kept moving.

Midnight. Parimalam could not sleep. How could she? Even the help that had appeared before her eyes had slipped away. She sank into worry.

As time passed her fear increased. She decided she had to escape from that house somehow. She now doubted whether Durai would come as Ambalam had promised. She had to get out by herself.

Dukkaram was no longer there to stop her. Only Raman remained, and he was sleeping outside. This was the right time to escape.

Could she break through the roof and jump out? No. There was no way to do it.

After long thought she picked up a crowbar lying in a corner and began, little by little, to break away the mud wall. With the desire “Freedom! Freedom!” she carried on without trembling. Somehow she had to escape from there and see her beloved.

Her beloved, meanwhile, had reached the Manora with his friends in order to see her.

A torch beam fell upon the group of friends. Durai and all the others started.

“Durai! You have come? Why have you brought so much company?” Ambalam asked with a laugh, standing before them.

“It is dark, isn't it? There is a lot of fear of thieves,” Durai replied.

“If there is weight in one's lap, only then is there fear on the road!…… All right...... come! Come!......” Ambalam said, leading him toward the lower level of the Manora. His friends too went with him.

“Where is Parimalam?” Durai asked eagerly.

“She is on the upper storey, waiting for you,” Ambalam said.

Everyone entered the Manora. Each level of the Manora had steps. They climbed them and reached the first upper level. From there, Durai first tried to climb to the next one.

But his friends said, “You can climb last,” and went ahead to the second level. Durai, one other friend and Ambalam remained on the first level.

Durai tried to climb the steps. That was enough. Ambalam struck him hard on the head with the torch in his hand.

Before Durai could even cry “Ayyo!”, more than fifty rough men rushed into the first level. Durai's friends on the second level were prevented from coming down. His one friend who remained below was badly wounded and fell in a dying condition.

Durai was bound by the men, lifted up and carried outside. His friends trapped above tried to jump down and save him. They could not. They were trapped on the upper level, while the men below prevented them from descending. Durai had fallen into Velliyambalam's hands.

There, Parimalam was trying to free herself. Here, Durai too had been caught. Who was there to tell her this news?

She struggled hard to break through the wall. There must be no sound, yet a hole had to be made. With courage she carried out that difficult task and at last succeeded.

The hole in the wall had become large enough for her to pass through. She tried to enter it. Then Raman's voice came from the street.

“Who is this? Who is pressing a cheek against me?” he shouted.

She ran to the side facing the street entrance and looked. A dog had laid its face against Raman's cheek while sleeping beside him.

“Who's this—putting cheek against cheek?” Raman shouted again, pushed the dog away and rolled over to sleep.

Parimalam regained her courage. Lying flat, she peered outside through the hole she had made in the wall. No one was there. Inch by inch she moved through and came outside.

“Appada!” she sighed.

Someone seized her shoulder.

“We are caught!” she thought in despair and fell face down.

“Parimalam,” came a weak call.

“Who? Old Mayandi?”

Yes! It was he. Parimalam rejoiced.

“Come! Come quickly!” he urged.

Without speaking, the two kept walking. They had gone far beyond Marungappallam village.

A large cart approached from the opposite direction. Seeing it, they hid. The cart passed them.

How could they know that Durai, bound hand and foot, and Ambalam were inside that very cart?
