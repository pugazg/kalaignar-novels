# 5

Dawn had not yet properly broken; the world was indistinct, like the mind of a myth-maker!

Parimalam walked in a fever of urgency, wanting to reach home before full daylight spread. Fear that someone might see her in that condition flashed in her eyes. Who would not be suspicious of a woman returning with a cloth wrapped around her in place of her upper garment? Her feet flew toward home without even noticing stone or thorn. Full dawn and her entrance into the house came at almost exactly the same time.

It was a middling tiled house. In many places the tiles had separated and remained unrepaired. The backyard, fenced with thorny bamboo, gave<!-- source join: scan 52 → 53 --> the house its beauty and completed its rural polish. At the far end stood a tall haystack. Beyond it were small chilli beds; because coriander had been scattered there, the whole yard gave off a rich fragrance. Jasmine and mullai vines lay entwined around supports. A cattle shed stood close against the house.

<!-- source: scan 53 -->

A red-brown cow and a scrawny calf were in the shed. From that one could understand that Parimalam's household sold milk.

Parimalam walked slowly in and entered the cattle shed. Just then the cow, having given its calf the milk left after milking, looked eagerly at Parimalam and cried “Amma!” Parimalam started violently. How many times had she stroked that cow affectionately when it cried! She had argued with her parents for the sake of its calf, asking them to leave plenty of milk for it.

But now she could attend neither to cow nor calf. Some terror was shaking her. Though she had spent the whole night with her lover amid dangers, she had not then felt worried about this. Now she could not bear the fear. Her parents must have searched for her all night. What explanation could she give them and escape—what would they believe—

<!-- source: scan 53 → 54 -->

Could she simply say what Durai had told her? Would they believe it?—what if they did? Her thoughts tangled in this way. From inside the cattle shed she peered toward the entrance. Her body shook without her knowing it. Her father, Thandavan, came and stood outside. His face looked exhausted as though he had been awake all night. Wondering where all he might have gone searching for her, Parimalam's eyes filled. Thandavan walked slowly to the neem tree opposite the house, broke off a twig and moved away brushing his teeth.

“Where are you going?” a voice called with pain mixed into it. Thandavan turned.

It was Parimalam's mother's voice.

“Are you questioning me the moment I leave? As though life is so grand! Can't you see where I'm going—I'm just going to brush my teeth!”

“Fine—first thing in the morning your business has to go properly—that's all! The belly that bore her is burning; what do you care about that!”

“Why is the belly that bore her burning? Go bathe a little, eat yesterday's rice and drink some rice-water!”

“So my daughter is gone—and that's that?”

“Eh, cursed woman! Why are you asking me like I'm an astrologer? I've been roaming all night too, searching everywhere there is to search—hey, Anjalai! Go and<!-- source join: scan 54 → 55 --> look around! Here, I'll finish brushing and come back—mix the old rice and keep it ready! Only if I put something in my stomach can I bend and wander around looking for your daughter!”

<!-- source: scan 55 -->

Saying this, Thandavan went away. Thandavan's figure was the original edition of the countryside itself. Anjalai was in no way less so. Their daughter Parimalam—though she did not stand dancing at the summit of modern civilisation—was educated enough that she could not simply be called a backwoods girl.

Once her father left the house, Parimalam found a little courage. If she could run to her mother and calm her before he returned, then keep Amma on her side and use her help to cool Appa's anger, she might survive. That hope began to cast light inside her.

Moving from the cattle shed toward the backyard, she opened the panel in the thorn fence and entered the house.

Inside, Parimalam searched for her mother. Anjalai sat in a corner with her hand on her cheek. Parimalam went near and called, “Amma!” That was enough. At the same instant they embraced each other. Whatever wrong children may have done before leaving home—

<!-- source: scan 55 → 56 -->

when they return, those faults disappear; the joy and affection of having found a lost treasure are feelings that arise in a mother alone. The embrace of Anjalai and Parimalam showed this.

“My eye—where did you go, amma! Girl who poured milk into the belly that bore you! My purest gold! My mirror! Why did you leave me suffering like this!” Anjalai began almost an oppari lament. Overcome by the extreme force of maternal love, Parimalam buried her head in Anjalai's lap and began to cry.

“Where did you go, amma? Tell me! Your appa—he's raging! Didn't I tell you even then not to educate her, he says; all night he nearly killed me asking whether I listened!”

Stammering, Parimalam began telling her mother the false story her lover had taught her.

“Amma! I did nothing wrong, amma! I was sleeping; then I dreamt that someone came and woke me—I opened my eyes—but I couldn't open my eyelids. It felt as though someone carried me away; that's all; I was completely dazed—then somewhere they sat me down, and a beautiful woman stood around me and laughed for a while—then<!-- source join: scan 56 → 57 --> she sang! She cried ‘Wedding—wedding!’—then she began to dance—a wonderful dance—and when she finished she shouted ‘Wedding—wedding!’ again. ‘Ayyo, they're throwing us into the sea!’ she screamed terribly. Suddenly she disappeared. I woke. It was near dawn. I was lying under a sluice, and my upper garment was missing. This cloth was there instead. I understood nothing, got frightened, and ran somewhere crying. Luckily, without even knowing it, I somehow came straight back to our own house. That's what happened, amma,” Parimalam said.

<!-- source: scan 57 -->

Tears budded in her eyes. They were not the false tears shed by women practising the craft of deception. They were tears of sorrow because she had been driven to lie and deceive her mother. Could these tears dissolve the barriers that would stop her and her beloved Durai from living together? That anxiety sawed through Parimalam's heart. One might say that drops of blood spurting from that sawn heart peeped out through her beautiful eyes, touched her cheeks, and ran down them.

The peacock-maiden could not boldly say: Durai and I are lovers; we met at the place arranged for us; we desire<!-- source join: scan 57 → 58 --> to walk hand in hand along the road of life. To escape her parents' anger, she mixed the story her lover had invented with her tears and gave it to her mother.

<!-- source: scan 58 -->

Anjalai lived in a region where people spun stories of seeing forest spirits in the movement of wild plants and பேச்சி and ghosts on the branches of the odhiya tree. She believed her daughter's story exactly as told. Not only did she believe it—she completed it.

“My eye! I know who carried you away—the blacksmith's daughter!”

“Who, amma? Which blacksmith's daughter?”

“That one—the girl from the blacksmith's house! Haven't I told you, my eye? They went for a wedding and were overturned into the sea—that girl!”

“Could be, amma—could be! That woman even had a garland around her neck!”

“Exactly! Since that girl died before her maidenhood passed, they say she's roaming as a Mohini spirit! That's what carried you off and played with you!”

“But amma—the blacksmith's daughter died hundreds and hundreds of years ago, they say. The woman I saw had not lost her youth!”

<!-- source: scan 58 → 59 -->

“Of course—of course. That's how it is. People keep growing older only until they die—once dead, after that, whatever age they were when they died, that's the age they'll remain.”

Parimalam nearly laughed. She struggled to suppress it. Anjalai did not notice; she was joyful that her daughter had returned.

“Somehow, amma, the Mohini girl from the blacksmith's house gave you back to me! By whose merit, who knows; my burning belly has cooled,” Anjalai said, stroking her daughter's flowing hair. Just then Thandavan entered, calling “Anjalai.”

“Have you heard the good news! Our eye has come back—look!” Anjalai shouted in delight.

Thandavan looked his daughter up and down. Parimalam understood only that Appa, in anger, was about to do something.

“Don't twist your moustache! A Mohini woman carried Parimalam away—” Anjalai had not even closed her mouth when Thandavan furiously slapped her hard and knocked her down. “Chi, shut up, you dog! Fine way you've raised a daughter! Mohini, Rohini!” Thandavan shouted.

“Appa!” Parimalam cried, trying to say something.

<!-- source: scan 59 → scan 60 chapter-5 portion -->

“Don't talk! Appa or goat-kid, what does it matter! I'll cut you into pieces—wait, shameless donkey!” Thandavan sprang forward and lifted a large sickle. Parimalam knew her father's nature. She understood that her life had ended.

“Appa!” she screamed. Thandavan said nothing; he rushed at his daughter with the sickle raised. Just then a voice shouted, “Hey, stop!” Thandavan turned—a hand stopped the sickle that was about to fall on Parimalam.
