# 27

Policemen surrounded Parimalam, and with them stood her father Thandavan, scattering words like flame. Trapped between them, Parimalam could not understand how she might escape. Nor did she know what had happened to her beloved back there. Had the sharp knife in the hand of that man who looked like a tortoise incarnation already plunged into her beloved's chest? Should she worry about that—or about having herself been caught where she could not get away?

It took only the flash of lightning. Within that instant the sweet-tongued young woman found a new way. She decided that it was the best way both to make herself appear innocent and to save her beloved. She spoke in agitation.

“The man who carried me away is in that house over there. Come! We can go and catch him at once.”

At this the police eagerly asked, “Where? Which house?”

“If you come with me, I will show you. Some terrifying man—a grotesque-looking creature—carried me here and tried to ruin me. Fortunately, at the right moment an old man and a young man intervened and saved me. The brute and the young man began fighting. A murder may even have happened by now. I watched for my chance and escaped,” Parimalam said breathlessly.

“Come, let us go,” the police said, setting off in haste. Parimalam led the way.

Thandavan understood nothing. Was what his daughter said really true? Had some cruel man carried her away against her will? Or had she run away with her illicit lover of her own accord and was now lying to save herself? He walked on thinking these thoughts.

Parimalam went ahead through the darkness, the police followed, and at last she stopped and said, “This is the house!” The policemen rushed inside in a body. Parimalam too peered in eagerly.

Everyone, including her, was disappointed.

Neither Ayyappan nor Mayandi nor Durai was anywhere to be seen. They found only scattered objects and broken glass vessels. Parimalam was shocked. She longed to see her beloved. She could not bear even a second without seeing his face. She suffered, melting like wax fallen into fire.

Using the lamp, the police searched the house. They found a cloth—yes, a cloth soaked in blood. Suspicion deepened. They searched the entire house again. They extended the search into the garden. A tree branch lay broken. A beautiful flowering plant had been trampled and bent. They looked closely. Footprints of two or three people were impressed in the ground. From them they inferred that the men must have run away.

“You said there was a fight. How long ago would it have been?” the police asked Parimalam.

“The fight must have been going on when I met you. It has not even been a few minutes. I do not know how they could have disappeared so quickly,” she said, showing her astonishment.

“If it hasn't even been a few minutes, where could they have gone? Strange!” the policemen said, standing with fingers pressed to their noses in amazement.

“There! There!” one policeman cried, leaping up.

“What?” Everyone turned in alarm.

A large chopping sickle lay beside a bush. They picked it up. It was stained with blood. Yes, something had happened only a short while before; otherwise would blood still be dripping from the blade?

While they were examining the bloodstained sickle, another policeman jumped and cried, “Look—have you seen this?” They examined the object he indicated. A dagger. It looked just like the dagger that had been in Ayyappan's hand. They inspected it too and took it into possession.

“Something else may turn up. Let us search,” they said, beginning again with renewed eagerness.

It was night. The backyard had not been cleared. Stones and thorns filled the place. The policemen's eyes began circling through the dense bushes there.

“Have you seen this?” one policeman exclaimed.

“What? What?” Everyone ran toward him. He moved a bush aside and said, “Look here.” By the light of the hurricane lamp in his hand, the others tried to understand what he was showing them.

A hand lay on the ground.

As though painted red, the whole hand was soaked in blood. It had been cut off at the elbow and lay wedged among the bushes. Strong suspicion arose among the police: whose hand could it be? Everyone gathered around the severed hand and began offering whatever ideas occurred to them.

“It doesn't look like the hand of a young man. There are so many wrinkles!” said one.

“No, no. It doesn't look like an old man's hand. It looks like a young hand,” said another.

“See whether there is any tattoo.”

“No. No green tattoo—and no red tattoo either!”

“There has been a brawl. At the end of it they have cut off a hand with this chopping sickle. Beyond that we cannot say anything.”

“Does this hand belong to the man who carried Parimalam away? Or to the young man who came to save her? Or to the old man Parimalam mentioned?”

Thus they exchanged ideas and sank uselessly into greater confusion.

Thandavan's heart went thud-thud with fear. In this place where conditions for murder had plainly arisen, a shock flashed through his heart at the thought of what suffering Parimalam might have endured while trapped alone. But the suspicion he had already harboured toward her did not allow that shock to last.

“How are we to find the owner of this severed hand?”

“What is difficult about that? Find the man wandering around with one arm missing and question him!”

“One man with a missing hand? By God's grace there are nine thousand such people wandering around. Which one are we to find?”

“I have an idea. Why not show this hand to an expert in palmistry and ask him whose it is?”

“You will not find a palmist like that. If such palmists existed, the police would not need dogs to help them. Those fortune-tellers alone would be enough!”

“Why all this trouble? I shall find out right now whose hand this is! Parimalam is right here; let her tell us whose hand it is!”

“Yes, that's right. Parimalam may know whose hand this is. She can tell us easily,” one policeman agreed.

“Parimalam! You tell us—whose hand is this?” the police officer asked, turning toward her.

What a surprise! Somehow Parimalam, who had been standing there, had slipped away and run. While they were exchanging theories over the severed hand, she had seized the opportunity and escaped.

“Parimalam!” Thandavan shouted angrily.

The police could hardly bear their humiliation and distress. After wandering about with such difficulty, they lamented that they had let the woman they had finally caught slip out of their hands.

“Where could she have gone? Find her at once!” the police officer ordered the others, then sat down wearily with his hand against his cheek.

* * * *

In that darkness Parimalam walked without knowing her direction. Where had her beloved and the old man gone? How was she to find them? And what if, while searching for them, she fell into the hands of the police again?

These thoughts churned endlessly in Parimalam's heart. She went wherever her feet carried her—ran for a while, walked a little, then ran again—planning only to get somewhere and hide before dawn. As if echoing the tumult in her heart, she heard the roar of the river rushing in flood.

She had come far from the village. It was the river between Puduppattinam village and the place called Raja Madam. Parimalam had to cross that river. She wondered what to do.

Even as she stood thinking, she saw figures approaching in the distance. Yes—two people. They were coming toward her. She desperately wished that they might be her beloved and old Mayandi.

The figures came closer.

“It looks like my beloved's shape!” she told herself, trying to take comfort.

The two figures drew nearer still. Parimalam stood without blinking, staring at them.
