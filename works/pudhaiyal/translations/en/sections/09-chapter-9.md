# 9

At the very time the clandestine boat carrying old Mayandi was caught by the police, Parimalam too, caught in the hands of the rough men, was being carried unconscious. Where was that beautiful jasmine vine being taken? Those thick-bodied men hurried along, half running and half walking, carrying on their shoulders that peacock-maiden limp like a plucked lily stem—that casket sweet even to the touch—that gathered cluster of flowers. Does a load-bearing donkey know the worth and splendour of the silk cloth piled upon its back? The villains carrying Parimalam too did their work like donkeys, treating obedience to their master as their only duty. Because of that, Parimalam was protected from becoming a garland caught in a monkey's hand.

It soon became clear that the herd of brutes was heading toward the shore. “Faster! Faster!” one of them kept calling in a low voice. The others ran behind him. Some repeatedly turned to see whether anyone was following. In their swift movement, the moon in the sky seemed to run quickly before their eyes. Usually one sees the reflection of a running moon only in water; Parimalam's face looked as though such an image could be seen here on land as well. Yes—her face lay dim and withered.

<!-- source: scan 85 -->

The sea breeze began to blow chill. The rough group grew excited that they had come near the shore. The sound of waves striking the coast reached their ears; they had come still closer. Lying on a villain's shoulder, Parimalam regained consciousness. She was seized by a feeling as though her heart would shatter. Slowly she opened her eyes. When she was a little girl, her grandmother used to explain what death was.

“In Yama's world, Dharma Raja who rides the buffalo gives his men the noose and tells them to capture souls and bring them. Those men are called Yama's kinkiliyar. They seize souls and take them to Yama. The whole road is frightening. A river of fire runs there—with a bridge of hair across it—and one must pass forests filled with stones, thorns, scorpions, snakes, crocodiles, tigers and elephants. That is what it means for a soul to go to Yama's world!”

Her grandmother had told such things many times, as though she herself had gone once to Yama's world, seen it, and returned. Parimalam had mocked the old woman's stories then. But now, even if Yama's world and Yama's servants were all false, the sight of herself being carried and the appearance of the men carrying her inevitably recalled those old stories. She could think only that she had died and someone was carrying her soul away.

<!-- source: scan 86 -->

For a moment she wondered whether what she saw was a dream. Ayyo—could it not simply remain a dream? she prayed. She twisted as though asleep and suffering because she could not wake quickly.

Tormented by the cruel claws of confusion, she saw ahead the beautiful seven-storeyed tower—Manora! For an instant it flashed new strength and consolation into her and vanished. Seeing it gave Parimalam a little peace, but it did not last. The relief that they were near the village; the thought that this was the tower in which she had been alone with her lover; the feeling that even now her lover might be there watching her—all of it flashed again and again inside her and threw her back into pain. Closing her eyes gave a little relief. A journey toward death inside a dream-world may be frightening, but somewhere in one corner does not the firm thought keep peeping out that once one wakes, the dream will dissolve? With that thought alone as companion, Parimalam lay exhausted.

The group entered Manora with her. As they climbed the gallery stairs, the men remained behind one by one. Only two or three carried Parimalam onward and placed her on Manora's first level.

<!-- source: scan 87 -->

Parimalam kept her eyes tightly shut, thinking: if I open them, what horrible event might be happening before me—why should I see it? From the steps above came the sound of shoes descending. The men looked upward eagerly. One climbed a little way to look, hurried down and told the others, “Hey, come—come—he has come—it's him!” The few men standing near Parimalam left. She lay alone on the gallery floor.

By day, goats and cattle grazing nearby had chosen that lower floor as a place where they could easily climb and rest. If animals had rested there, what would the floor be like? A stench pierced the nose of Parimalam lying with her eyes closed, while the sound of the descending shoes echoed terribly in her ears. The sound came nearer. Someone—a man—was coming toward her. A woman in this world must rely on herself and use her own strength and firmness of mind to protect that exalted thing of hers; now someone had come to steal it. Even after losing life, people may continue to live in the body of fame. But if honour is lost, one cannot live even while life remains in one's own body—can that be called life? These scrolls of thought spread through her heart.

<!-- source: scan 88 -->

They reached and touched the edges of her heart. A roar rose within her as though storm and thunder competed with one another. Suddenly there was silence. The shoe-sound stopped. For one second the stillness of a cremation ground surrounded her mind too.

“Parimalam! Parimalam! Hey, girl! Hey—little one! Get up!”

The voice struck her ears. Parimalam could no longer think of anything. She wished she could simply become a corpse.

“Open your eyes—hey!—will you open them or not?”

A small stick touched Parimalam's body. On her skin it felt like a snake crawling.

“Hey—don't be afraid, little one! Get up—otherwise......”

There was authority and roughness in the voice, but no kindness.

<!-- source: scan 88 → 89 -->

The words “Get up! Otherwise......” were enough. Parimalam screamed and, without knowing it, rose to her feet. She looked ahead. She opened her eyes wide, blinking them rapidly, uncertain and frightened whether she was truly seeing clearly. Gathering a little courage, she stared at the man speaking before her. Velliyambalam stood there.

“Don't tremble, girl—you will come to no harm—”

“What name do you give to what has happened now?”

“You think this is danger?”

“I'll turn through a dictionary and see whether there is another word!”

“You yourself are a big dictionary—”

“Ayya—great man of the village—why did you have me carried here? Tell me that.”

“Parimalam! I'm not merely a big man of the village. If I decide, a hundred murders...”

“I know—you can cut a hundred coconuts. Tell me what matters.”

“Girl! Nothing you imagine is going to happen here. I brought you here only to protect you. Your appa is full of suspicion about you. He planned to kill you and told me angrily this morning. From then on, my first concern was to protect you.”

“Ah—the wolf wept because the goat was getting wet.”

“You fool—listen. I too know you have a partner. Your appa knows it too. Now I have rescued you from your father's hands. Do me just one favour—that is enough!”

“I don't even understand what you are saying, ayya!”

<!-- source: scan 89 → 90 -->

“Go back home once and see! Then you'll know whether what this Ambalam said was true or false. I was the one who heard Thandavan leap and rage at me that he would cut you down in one stroke and bury you.”

“It seems it might have been better even if I had died that way, ayya—rather than come here and stand like this in the darkness...”

“Ayyo, ayyo—you die? Chi, chi! Mad girl. All I want is that you must not die... and along with that, you must tell me just one thing!”

“What thing?”

“That beloved lover of yours—who is he?”

Parimalam stood silent. Why had this great man taken so much trouble merely to ask her lover's name? she wondered.

“Why have you gone silent? Tell me, amma—who is he? Where is he? Don't hide all that. I know you have someone. Look at this—your man's sandal!” Ambalam said, picking up a sandal lying near the steps and laughing as he showed it to her. She recognised it as belonging to her lover Durai. Merely seeing an object worn by her beloved gave her a little happiness.

<!-- source: scan 90 → 91 -->

“What, did I discover it? See how clever I am—Parimalam—tell me who that fellow is.”

“Why do you need to know about him?”

“I'll find the man and get you married to him! You know yourself—everything good and bad that happens in this village happens through me!”

“You need not take the trouble of arranging my marriage.”

“Parimalam—don't talk uselessly. Tell me who he is! Otherwise I won't let you go. If you tell me about him, you can stay in my house until your appa's anger cools. If you are in my house, your appa cannot do anything!”

“I don't need anyone's house—let me go, ayya!”

“I can't. Parimalam, don't stir my anger. You know about me. No one yet knows where Santhana Krishnan of Sandhu House lost his head! The body floated at Puduppattinam, the leg at Sethu Bava Chathiram, the hand at Nadiyam, and the head in mid-sea. I'm talking about the secret of Narayana Perumal's death. Keep all that in mind when you speak to me!”

Parimalam stood without answering.

“Will you tell me or not?” Ambalam came closer. Just then some of his men ran noisily upstairs crying, “Master! Master!”

“What?” Ambalam barked.

“Two boats are coming toward shore!”

“Ah—fine. You go watch. I'll come now.” The rough men ran away.

“Will you or won't you, Parimalam?” Ambalam shouted again, coming close. Now he had another urgency too: he must not be seen by the people arriving in the boats. Parimalam did not know that her own Durai was in one of them. Ambalam looked once through the window at the boats cutting through the waves toward shore.

“Hmm—are you going to tell me now, or are you going to suffer?” he shouted frighteningly and sprang forward.

Parimalam's expression showed that she was preparing some lie. She cleared her throat.

<!-- source: scan 91 → 92 -->

“Hmm—good girl, tell me!” Ambalam stood with his mouth open. If he learned the man's name, he could catch him and through him discover “where the gold was buried.” Ayyappan, who too had wanted to dig for gold, was arriving across the sea as a prisoner. Here Ambalam was flying about in feverish eagerness.

“Tell me, amma!”

“I'll tell you.”

“Tell me.”

“If I tell you, no harm will come to him, will it?”

“None at all!” Ambalam said, looking once more toward the sea. The boats were coming quickly.
