# 38

A beautiful young woman stood there, carrying the fullness of youth. There was no sign of calm on her face. Lines of longing and eagerness for something she awaited ran across it. Her eyes were red. She must have gone without sleep all night; she must also have been sunk in deep thought.

Why had she come and stood there? Had she come merely to look at him? Questions filled with anger pierced Durai's heart.

“Who are you, girl?” he asked, swallowing his anger and fury.

“This house,” she replied softly.

“You are Velliyambalam's……?” Durai asked, drawing the question out.

“You tell me. What am I to him?” she replied.

“Ma…” he began again.

“Ma is right! Tell me the next syllable,” she said.

“Ka,” he replied.

“No—kaa,” she said.

“Ma”—“kaa”—Durai became suspicious.

“Yes—ma—kaa—raa—ni,” she completed, and released a deep sigh.

“Maharani—is that a name? Or…?” he asked, extending his doubt.

“Not a name! A maharani indeed! The second wife!” she explained.

Durai remained silent.

“Why are you silent? You have not even asked my name. Why?” she continued.

Durai gave only a foolish smile as his answer and said nothing.

“Poor man—I feel sorry when I look at you. Here, stretch out your hands. I will untie you,” she said, reaching her hands through the window.

Durai extended his hands toward her. She untied his bonds. He shook the hands that had been tied so long and had grown painful, and for a moment felt some relief.

That relief did not last even a second. Fear of what forms Ambalam's plans might take surrounded and confused him.

Understanding that his mind was wandering somewhere else, she looked at him with concern. “Why did this happen to you?”

“What, girl, you are asking me? Would your husband have kept it from you……?”

“He did not tell me!”

“Perhaps…… he thought secrets should not be told to a young woman!”

“Am I a young woman? Is that what you think?”

“Think?…… One can see it just by looking. That is why I said so.”

“Can you tell my age merely by looking? Is age all you can see? Can you see the stirrings of that youth too?”

The direction of her speech frightened Durai. He wondered whether this too was one of Velliyambalam's tricks—whether he had sent a **rasa-valli** and planned to obtain the treasure secret through her.

“Girl…… I would like to be alone for a while. Go now and come back later,” Durai said, turning toward another corner of the room.

“What is this ‘girl, girl’ you keep calling me? My name is Menaka!”

“All right, so what?” Durai said, moving to a place where she could not see him.

She too went away.

One unsolvable confusion after another arose in Durai's heart.

“Who is she? Why should she untie my bonds? Why should a woman who became Ambalam's wife show sympathy toward me? Is this too a plot? Or has youth, ravaged by time and waiting for prey, decided that this is its moment?”

He did not even feel the pain of hunger. Menaka had become such a great riddle to him.

Seeing no movement of shadow by the window, Durai understood that she had gone and came back there. He heard Velliyambalam speaking loudly with some men near the front platform of the house. Durai listened, trying to understand what was being said. He could not.

Then Menaka hurried to the window. She threw a bundle through it and whispered, “Careful—careful!” before disappearing.

Caught in astonishment, Durai opened it. Idlis!

He sighed deeply and began eating them. She came again and placed a small cup of water through the window bars before disappearing once more.

Durai remained alert. Somehow, amid the collision of his thoughts, he finished the morning food and lay down wearily.

He heard the door being opened. Durai rose eagerly. The door opened. The servant Raman and one rough man came in.

“Ayya's order—you may come outside for ten minutes to brush your teeth,” Raman said.

“I do not need to brush my teeth,” Durai replied.

“That will not do—you must brush them,” Raman ordered.

Durai came outside with him. In the garden, rough men stood around holding sticks like warders in a prison. Under the protection of those warders, just as murder prisoners attend to their bodily needs, Durai too attended to his.

“A small village—and here a wild king's court is being run like this,” Durai thought, burning with anger.

He was locked up again. As though feeding a prison inmate, Raman placed a cup of gruel through the window and walked away.

Durai picked up the gruel and looked at it, laughing to himself. Then her hand entered through the window—a porcelain cup!

She disappeared. Hot tea!

Durai tasted it. Of all the pain he had suffered, only that hot tea offered him some comfort. Hot tea! It was delicious. But he could not understand the mystery behind it.

He thought and thought, but could reach no conclusion.

Then she came again. The porcelain cup and the other vessel were near the window. She took them, looked sharply at him once, and hurried away.

Ambalam came toward her.

“What, Menaka!” he asked, turning and following her.

She set the vessels down on a raised platform and embraced his shoulders. He squeezed her hands in his rough palms.

“Ayyo! Ayyo!” she cried and pulled her hands free.

“Appada! They have turned red—look!” she complained.

“Chi! Go away, dog! Crying over this!” he said, giving her a slap on the back.

She bent like a bow to escape the blow. “Shoo! What kind of roughness is this?” she pouted.

“Hey, donkey! Look how much fuss this dog makes!” Ambalam said fondly, pinching her cheek.

“Ayyo, don't pull the flesh off with your hand!” she cried.

“What, is it some great guava fruit that flesh will come away if I pinch it?” he mocked.

She stood and released a deep breath. How many thoughts were contained in that sigh!

It was nearly three years since she had become Ambalam's wife.

“What is there for Menaka to worry about? Won't she find some Indra or other?” the villagers had once said, and she herself had inwardly rejoiced at such talk.

Thiruthuraipoondi was her native place. She belonged to a middle-class family. Her parents wanted their daughter to study like others. She studied. Her education ended. Then began the search for a bridegroom.

There was a related young man in Marungappallam village. He too was educated. Menaka herself wished to marry him.

The marriage was fixed. It was to take place at the bridegroom's house in Marungappallam. The bride was brought there the previous day. The wedding was to be in the morning.

That night a serious quarrel broke out between the bride's family and the bridegroom's family.

The bridegroom's people had not given the jewels they had promised. Menaka's father began to deepen the quarrel over it. The argument grew until the two sides were close to fighting.

Menaka's father was hot-tempered. He struck the bridegroom's father. The bridegroom could not bear seeing his father attacked and rushed at his father-in-law. The father-in-law, without regarding him as the bridegroom, seized his neck and strangled him to death.

Once a killing had taken place, the disturbance subsided…… Velliyambalam intervened at that moment.

He laid down a condition: if Menaka's father was to be saved from the gallows, Menaka must be given to him in marriage.

Until the killing, her father had never thought of the gallows waiting for him. Now he began trembling in fear.

Menaka understood that the responsibility of saving her father had fallen upon her. The marriage between Velliyambalam and Menaka was fixed immediately.

The panchayat decided that the bridegroom's death was not murder but suicide. The bridegroom's family, cowed by Velliyambalam's threats, submitted and suffered in silence.

Menaka became Ambalam's wife.

From then on her ears never failed to hear the loving descriptions “donkey” and “dog.” At school she had read Tamil literature. Moon! Honey! Flower! Jewel! Beloved! Life! Precious medicine! In the pages of epics she had met lovers who played the harp of love with words like these.

She had built countless castles imagining that the beautiful pictures seen in books could also be seen in married life.

But circumstance had handed a rose over to iron hands. Somehow a situation had suddenly arisen in which a wild buffalo played in a lotus pond.

For the first few months of marriage Menaka took pride in the sacrifice she had made for her father. After that, the invasion of her tender youth grew stronger.

Inside a thorn fence of age and brutality, she lay like a jasmine vine withering in some corner for want of anyone to pluck its flowers.

Dog; donkey; hey you; get lost—she could no longer bear hearing only such words. She desired a gentle breeze, but a storm kept blowing through her life saying, “Am I not wind too?”

It was in this condition that Durai was brought there. She knew very well that Ambalam was doing all sorts of things in search of the treasure. Before he could obtain that treasure, she believed that the treasure she herself had long searched and withered for had come into her hands.

Between Velliyambalam's dry voice asking, “What, is your cheek some guava fruit?” and the cry of youth that would embrace her saying, “Sweet sugarcane! Give me your guava cheek!… Give me your kovai-red lips, lovely one! In return I will give you ten thousand strings of kisses!” Menaka had spent so long yearning over the difference. Now she was certain that the time had come to put a full stop to that yearning.

“Durai is a handsome man! If he agrees to my freedom, I will agree to his freedom,” she whispered in a low voice, unheard by anyone.

Velliyambalam came to the room where Durai was confined. Standing at the window, he looked at him.

“Well, boy! Are you going to tell the truth or not? What have you decided?” he asked in a commanding tone.

“I have decided—to die!” Durai replied.

“Even if Yama himself comes with his account book and calls you, I will not send you. Like Lord Siva who saved Markandeya, I will save you. Do you know why? Only after I get the treasure can your burial take place! Look here, Durai. Listen to me. Tell me that secret. Show me where it is. We will divide it between the two of us!” Ambalam said, trying to conciliate him.

Durai stood silent.

Then the voice of a kudukuduppai fortune-teller was heard in the street.

“Good times are being born for the ayya of this house! Great luck is coming! Luck is coming! Sudden luck is coming!”

Ambalam listened eagerly to those words. Leaving Durai, he went toward the street.

“Even in the night, at dawn Saibu told it! Saibu told what rose in the cremation ground! Look at Ayya—he is like a maharaja! Sudden luck is going to strike Ayya! Ayya is struggling over one matter—another fellow is trying to snatch it away—it will not happen! Good times are being born for Ayya! Wealth is coming! Saibu asks for some torn cloth Ayya has worn, or some old sari Amma has thrown away!…… Good times are being born! Give! Give! Give! Give! Give! Give! Give! The god who gives is going to tear through the roof—split open the earth—and give it to Ayya!”

Ambalam could not contain his delight. He called the fortune-teller inside.
